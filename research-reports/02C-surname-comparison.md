## Abstract

The Bengali surname system is one of the most elaborate and historically layered caste-rank systems in the Indian subcontinent. This report provides a comprehensive comparative analysis of Bengali surnames across the three traditional upper castes — Brahmin, Kayastha, and Baidya — with additional attention to Vaishya trading communities. Central to this analysis is the **Kulin system**, formalized under the Sena dynasty king Ballala Sena (r. 1158–1179 CE), which divided the Kayastha and Brahmin castes into internally ranked lineages, creating a hierarchical matrix that governed marriage, status, and social reproduction for eight centuries. The surname Dey/De is situated as a **Maulika Kayastha, Siddha (first-class) tier** — the eighth position below Kulin, but substantially above the 72 lower Maulika families. This report contextualizes that placement within the full architecture of Bengali social stratification.

## 1. Foundations: Bengal's Unique Social Hierarchy

### 1.1 The Two-Varna Structure of Bengal

[WELL-ESTABLISHED] Unlike the orthodox Hindu four-varna schema (Brahmin, Kshatriya, Vaishya, Shudra), Bengal developed a distinctive **two-varna** social structure. The Hindu community in Bengal was divided, practically speaking, into only Brahmins and Shudras — with the Kshatriya and Vaishya varnas largely absent as distinct, functioning social categories.

As Malcolm McLean (1998) notes: "there being no Kshatriya or Vaishya element in the indigenous population of Bengal... the rank of the Baidya and the Kayasthas is the same as those of the Nabasakhs with whom they constitute the upper strata of the Bengali Sudras known as *satsudra*." Yet simultaneously, Brahmins, Kayasthas, and Baidyas together formed what was called the **uchchajati** (high castes), and they are together described as the "three traditional higher castes of Bengal" (Bandyopadhyay 2004, p. 20; Hutton 1961, p. 65).

This paradox — high social status alongside official classification as Shudra — defines the peculiarity of Bengali upper-caste identity. Historian Richard Eaton (1996, p. 102) captures it elegantly: after absorbing remnants of Bengal's ancient Hindu ruling dynasties, the Kayasthas became "the region's surrogate Kshatriya or warrior class," without ever achieving the ritual recognition that formal Kshatriya status would have conferred.

### 1.2 The Three Upper Castes (Bhadralok Complex)

The **bhadralok** ("respectable people") of Bengal — the educated, land-controlling, professional elite — were drawn primarily from three castes:

| Caste | Sanskrit Term | Traditional Occupation | Varna Claim |
|-------|--------------|----------------------|-------------|
| **Brahmin** | Brāhmaṇa | Priestly, scholarly | Brahmin (undisputed) |
| **Kayastha** | Kāyastha | Scribal, administrative | Contested: Kshatriya claimed; Shudra assigned |
| **Baidya** | Vaidya | Medical, scholarly | Contested: Kshatriya/Brahmin adjacent claimed |

[WELL-ESTABLISHED] In Bengal between 1500 and 1850 CE, Kayasthas were regarded as the highest non-Brahmin caste in the region (Inden 1976, p. 1). During the British Raj, all three castes collectively produced the bhadralok professional class that dominated Calcutta's intellectual, administrative, and commercial life.

## 2. The Kulin System — Architecture and Social Consequences

### 2.1 Origins and Historical Controversy

[WELL-ESTABLISHED with CONTESTED elements] The **Kulin system** (*kulīnism*) is a system of ranked lineages within certain Bengali castes, most notably the Brahmin and Kayastha communities. The system is credited — in both historical sources and tradition — to **Vallala Sena** (Ballala Sena), the Sena dynasty king who ruled Bengal approximately 1158–1179 CE.

The etymological foundation: *kulīna* derives from Sanskrit *kula* (family/lineage/clan) + *-īna* (belonging to/of good), meaning literally "of good family" or "belonging to a noble lineage." According to the Banglapedia (citing Vachaspati Mishra), a Kulina family was one marked by nine qualities: *achara* (ceremonial purity), *vidya* (learning), *vinaya* (discipline), *pratistha* (reputation), *tirtha-darshana* (pilgrimage), *nistha* (piety), *tapasya* (asceticism), *avrtti* (marriage among equals), and *dana* (liberality).

[CONTESTED] Whether Ballala Sena truly "invented" the Kulin system or formalized pre-existing practices is debated. The Banglapedia notes: "The Sena king Vallalasena is also credited to have introduced Kulinism in Bengal though there are no Sena records, literary and epigraphic, to support this claim." The *Bangaon* copperplate of Vigrahapala III already points to genealogical ranking during the Pala era. However, most scholars agree that Ballala Sena's reign represents a decisive formalization of clan rankings — likely connected to his political project of using Brahmanical Hinduism to reassert royal authority after the Buddhist Pala dynasty.

The **political logic** of Kulinism (Banglapedia, PK Bhattacharyya): "The promulgation of Kulinism may be considered as a fine excuse for dividing the Brahmanas and thereby weakening their power as a potential challenge to the authority of the king." By elevating some Brahmin lineages over others, the Sena kings created loyal aristocratic allies while fracturing potential clerical opposition.

### 2.2 The Three-Tier Hierarchy

The Kulin system created a three-tier structure within both the Brahmin and Kayastha castes:

**Tier 1 — Kulina (Kulin)**  
High clan rank (*kulīna* or *Kulin*). These were the lineages designated as exemplars of purity, learning, and aristocratic conduct. Kulin status was heritable but could be degraded by improper marriages.

**Tier 2 — Maulika / Srotriya / Sreni**  
"Middle" clan rank. Among Radhiya Brahmins, the middle tier is called *Srotriya* (lit. "learned in the Shrutis"). Among Dakshina Radhi Kayasthas, it is called *Maulika* or *Maulik* (lit. "original/fundamental"). Within each of these, further sub-ranks existed (see below).

**Tier 3 — Vamshasreni / Kap / Banshajas**  
The lowest ranked, sometimes called *Vamshajas* among Brahmins or unranked families. These lineages had the lowest marriage desirability and fewest social privileges within the caste.

[WELL-ESTABLISHED] According to Ronald Inden's authoritative study (1976, pp. 34, 55–56): "The four major subcastes [of Bengali Kayastha] were *Daksina-radhi*, *Vangaja*, *Uttara-radhi* and *Varendra*. The *Dakshina-radhi* and *Vangaja* subcastes were further divided into *Kulina* or Kulin ('high clan rank') and *Maulika* or Maulik, the lower clan rank. The Maulika had four further 'ranked grades'."

### 2.3 The Kulin Marriage Problem

[WELL-ESTABLISHED] The Kulin system generated one of Bengal's most notorious social pathologies: **Kulin polygamy**. The logic was structural:

- A Kulin woman *must* marry a Kulin man (to preserve her lineage's status)
- A Kulin man *could* marry down (to Maulika women, elevating them through *hypergamy*)
- But the number of Kulin men was by definition restricted (only families of the 5 original clans)
- Result: A single Kulin man married dozens — sometimes hundreds — of women

The practice became an extreme social crisis by the 18th–19th centuries. The Banglapedia records that "one Kulin Brahmana sometimes married some 300 wives." These wives lived with their natal families; the Kulin husband was a periodic visitor who "would visit them maybe once or twice a year." The wives of such men were, for practical purposes, permanently abandoned — unable to remarry (the prohibition on widow remarriage applied), reduced to dependents in their natal homes.

The social reformer **Ishvarchandra Vidyasagar** (1820–1891) campaigned against Kulin polygamy throughout the 1850s–1870s. His 1871 tract *Against High-Caste Polygamy* was a detailed legislative argument, though it ultimately produced no new law (Brian Hatcher, *Against High-Caste Polygamy*, 2023). His earlier success — the **Hindu Widow's Remarriage Act of 1856** — addressed the related problem of abandoned and widowed Kulin women. 

The Kulin system's irony was profound: the very families designated as *most noble* by the system had, by the 19th century, reduced their women to the most degraded social conditions.

### 2.4 The Kulaji Literature

[WELL-ESTABLISHED] The genealogical records of the Brahmin and Kayastha castes were maintained in texts called **kulaji**, **kulakarika**, or **kulapanjika** — "books of clan rank." These were maintained by professional genealogists called **ghatakas** (matchmakers), who recited them at weddings as a form of social credential verification. Inden (1976, p. 2): "Their contents were judged for accuracy at the time of recitation by the persons of the subcaste assembled for the wedding, and their contents had to be approved before the genealogists received remuneration."

These texts were divided into three sections: the cosmogonic creation myth of the clans; the rules (vidhana) of clan rank for marriage; and the actual genealogy of births and marriages across ten to twenty generations. The system constituted a kind of feudal social registry, maintained collectively by subcaste members.

## 3. Bengali Brahmin Surnames

### 3.1 The *-padhyaya / -jee* System

[WELL-ESTABLISHED] The most distinctive feature of Bengali Brahmin surnames is the suffix **-padhyaya** (or its colloquial forms *-jee*, *-jye*, *-jha*), derived from Sanskrit *upādhyāya* meaning "teacher" or "learned one." Bengali Brahmins historically received their surnames by combining the name of their *assigned village* (granted by the king) with this occupational/honorific suffix.

The Banglapedia explains the mechanism: "Each Brahmana and his descendants were known by the name of the village in which they lived, which became their *gain* (belonging to a village) and later developed into surname. For example the residents of Mukhati village had Mukhati gain, and had the surname Mukhati or Mukhopadhyaya, by the addition of *upadhyaya* (teacher) to the village name."

This produced the characteristic triple form of Bengali Brahmin surnames:
> **Original Bengali/vernacular form → Sanskritized form → Anglicized form**

### 3.2 Rarhi (Radhiya) Brahmin Surnames

[WELL-ESTABLISHED] Rarhi Brahmins (*Rāḍhī*) are those settled in the *Radha* or Rarh region of southwestern Bengal. They trace descent from five Kanauj Brahmin families said to have been imported by King Adisura. The five principal Rarhi Brahmin Kulin surnames are:

| Anglicized | Sanskritized | Vernacular Origin | Gotra | Notes |
|------------|-------------|-------------------|-------|-------|
| **Banerjee** | Bandyopadhyay | *Bandoghat* village | Shandilya | "Teacher of Bando village"; Kulin |
| **Mukherjee** | Mukhopadhyay | *Mukhati* village (or *mukha* = "chief") | Bharadwaja | "Chief teacher" or "Teacher of Mukha village"; Kulin |
| **Chatterjee** | Chattopadhyay | *Chatta* village | Kashyap | "Teacher of Chatta village"; Kulin |
| **Ganguly** | Gangopadhyay | *Ganga* (river) | Savarna | "Teacher/associated with the Ganges"; Kulin |
| **Bhattacharya** | Bhattacharya | *bhatta* (learned) + *acharya* (teacher) | Various | Honorific: "learned teacher"; not village-derived; can be applied to any of the 5 Rarhi surnames |

[WELL-ESTABLISHED] The *-jee* anglicization comes from the Bengali colloquial *-jye* or *-ujye*, itself a reduction of *upadhyaya*. The Ganguly/Ganguli case is interesting: the original surname was *Gangal* (a vernacular approximation), which symmetrically became *Gangopadhyay*, but the anglicized form followed the original Bengali (*Ganguli*) rather than the pattern that produced *Gangerjee*.

Among the Rarhi Brahmins, the full Kulin hierarchy was: Mukhya-Kulina (highest), Gauna-Kulina, and Srotriya. The 56 original Kulin villages organized into four *thaks* (orders): Phule, Vallabhi, Kharda, and Sarvanandi.

### 3.3 Varendra (Barendra) Brahmin Surnames

[WELL-ESTABLISHED] Varendra Brahmins are those settled in northern Bengal (*Varendra* / *Barendra* = present-day Rajshahi and surrounding areas). They share the same mythological origin as Rarhi Brahmins (descendants of the five Kanauj families) but developed separately due to geographic isolation. The major Varendra Brahmin Kulin surnames include:

| Surname | Notes |
|---------|-------|
| **Sanyal** | Varendra Brahmin Kulin; Vatsya gotra |
| **Lahiri** | Varendra Brahmin Kulin; Shandilya gotra |
| **Bagchi** | Varendra Brahmin Kulin; Shandilya gotra |
| **Maitra/Moitra** | Varendra Brahmin Kulin; Kashyap gotra |
| **Bhaduri** | Varendra Brahmin Kulin |
| **Chakraborty** | An honorific title (*chakravartin* = "emperor/king"); can be held by Rarhi or Varendra Brahmins |

[WELL-ESTABLISHED] Rarhi and Varendra Brahmins, despite sharing mythological origins, do not intermarry — they constitute separate endogamous groups with distinct customs.

### 3.4 Brahmin Title Surnames

Several surnames are occupational titles that can be held by Brahmins of any group:
- **Bhattacharya**: Learned Brahmin teacher of sacred texts (*bhatta* + *acharya*)
- **Chakraborty**: Royal title (*chakravartin*); conferred on particularly powerful or influential Brahmin families
- **Goswami**: Vaishnava religious leader, often associated with the Chaitanya movement
- **Gopal**: Divine title, associated with Krishna devotion

## 4. Bengali Kayastha Surnames — The Complete Hierarchy

### 4.1 Historical Origin of the Kayastha Caste

[WELL-ESTABLISHED] The Bengali Kayastha (*kāyastha*, lit. "situated in the body") evolved as a caste from what was originally a professional group of scribes, record-keepers, and administrators. As Sekhar Bandyopadhyay (2004) observes, the caste crystallized between the 10th–12th century CE. Tej Ram Sharma's analysis of Gupta inscriptions (1978) shows surnames like Datta, Ghosha, Varman, Naga, and Mitra appearing as administrative names in Bengal as early as the 4th–6th century CE — before they became specifically "Kayastha" surnames.

[CONTESTED] The varna status remains genuinely unresolved: "Hindu communities labelled 'Kayastha' are found all over northern India, but historically, their social ranking was not uniform. At different times and in different places, those labelled Kayastha were accorded the same status as Brahmins, Kshatriyas or Sudras, and there was even a claim that they formed a fifth varna within the Hindu caste structure" (Pagani et al. 2017; Bengali Kayastha Wikipedia). The Bengal census of 1931 was, in Bellenoit's words, "disturbingly ambiguous" about their status.

### 4.2 The Five Original Clans and Their Fate

The founding origin myth posits five Kayasthas who accompanied five Brahmin families from Kanauj at the invitation of King Adisura:

| Kayastha | Brahmin Patron | Surname Derived |
|----------|---------------|-----------------|
| Makaranda Ghosh | Bhattanarayana (Shandilya) | **Ghosh** |
| Kali Dasa Mitra | Sriharsha (Bharadwaja) | **Mitra** |
| Dasaratha Guha | Vedagarva (Sawarna) | **Guha** |
| Dasaratha Basu | Chhandara (Vatsya) | **Bose/Basu** |
| Purushottama Datta | Daksha (Kashyap) | **Datta** |

[UNCERTAIN] This legend is accepted by scholars as myth rather than history. Swarupa Gupta (2009, pp. 103–104): it was "fitted into a quasi-historical, sociological narrative of Bengal and deployed to explain the realities of caste and sub-caste origins and connections during the late nineteenth and early twentieth century." A modern genetic study (Pagani et al. 2017) found that "individuals belonging to some of the Kayastha lineages... show genetic relatedness with present-day populations in Uttar Pradesh (Bose, Pal), while others show a significant genomic contribution from South India... or do not yield any informative signal" — suggesting diverse origins rather than a single Kanauj migration.

### 4.3 Kulin Kayastha: The Elite Three (Dakshina Radhi)

[WELL-ESTABLISHED] Among the **Dakshina Radhi** (South Rarhi) subcaste, which is the dominant Kayastha group in West Bengal, only **three** of the five original clans achieved Kulin status:

| Surname | Sanskrit Root | Gotra | Notes |
|---------|--------------|-------|-------|
| **Ghosh** | *ghosa* (proclamation/announcement; also cowherd association) | Soukalin | Kulin; associated with cowherd/agricultural origin by some; Kulin Kayastha by position |
| **Bose / Basu** | *vasu* (good, wealth, prosperity) | Gautam | Kulin; produced Subhas Chandra Bose, Jagadish Chandra Bose, Satyendra Nath Bose |
| **Mitra** | *mitra* (friend, ally; also the solar deity Mitra) | Vishwamitra | Kulin; also found in other South Asian communities |

**Notable omission — Datta**: [WELL-ESTABLISHED] Although Datta/Dutt is one of the five original Kanauj clans, they *did not* achieve Kulin status in the Dakshina Radhi subcaste. A tradition recorded in multiple sources explains this: "It is said that the Dattas not acknowledging the Brahmanas as their masters and themselves as servants of the Brahmanas were not honoured as Kulins." This famous Bengali saying captures the Datta position: *"Datta karo bhritya noy, songey eshechhe"* — "Datta is nobody's servant, but has come along with." The Dattas' refusal to accept a servile origin myth distinguished them from the three Kulin clans — at the cost of Kulin rank, but gaining a reputation for aristocratic independence.

**Notable addition — Guha in Bangaja**: In the **Vangaja** (East Bengal) subcaste, **Guha** is the Kulin clan. In Dakshina Radhi, Guha is Maulika First Class, not Kulin.

### 4.4 Maulika Kayastha: The Eight Siddha Families

[WELL-ESTABLISHED] Below the Kulin three (Ghosh, Bose, Mitra) lies the Maulika tier, itself divided into two sub-ranks. The **Siddha** (also called *attghar*, "eight families" or *Siddha-Maulika*) are the first-class Maulika — the aristocracy of the non-Kulin Kayastha world:

| Surname | Sanskrit Root | Meaning | Notes |
|---------|--------------|---------|-------|
| **Dey / De** | *deva* (god, divine) | "Of/pertaining to the divine" | Robin's family; Eastern Bengal connection; also Deva dynasty link |
| **Datta / Dutt** | *datta* (given, gift) | "That which is given" | Originally Kulin-aspirant; placed here due to origin myth dispute |
| **Kar** | *kara* (doer, hand, tax) | "One who does/makes" | Administrative/occupational origin |
| **Palit** | *pālita* (protected, nourished) | "One who is protected/nurtured" | Possibly from royal protection |
| **Sen** | *sena* (army, general) | "Warrior/army-related" | Possibly connected to Sena dynasty; Note: Baidya Sen is separate |
| **Sinha / Singha** | *siṃha* (lion) | "Lion" / "Leonine" | Martial connotation |
| **Das / Dasa** | *dāsa* (servant) or *dāśa* (fisherman) | "Servant (of God)" | Complex: see Section 6 below |
| **Guha** | *guha* (cave; Kartikeya) | "Cave-dweller" or Kartikeya (the war god) | Kulin in Vangaja, Maulika here |

[WELL-ESTABLISHED] This eight-family tier — the **Siddha Maulika** — constitutes what one might call the Bengal Kayastha upper middle class. They ranked significantly above the remaining Maulika families (the Sadhya/Bahatture — "72 families") but below the Kulin three. As the caste system article (IndiaDiv) notes: "The Mauliks again are divided into two classes: the Deys, the Dutts, the Kars, the Palits, the Sens, the Sinhas, the Dasses and the Guhas are of the first class, the rest (which number 72) being of the 2nd class."

### 4.5 Maulika Second Class — The 72 Families

The second tier of Maulika (*Sadhya-Maulika* or *Bahatture*) consists of approximately 72 additional Kayastha family names of lower ranked status. Surnames commonly found here include various occupational and place-derived names. Intermarriage patterns typically did not easily cross the Siddha-Sadhya boundary.

| Surname | Meaning | Position | Notes |
|---------|---------|----------|-------|
| **Nandi** | *nanda* (joy, happiness) | Kayastha (position varies) | Also associated with Shiva's bull attendant |
| **Nag** | *nāga* (serpent, tribal) | Kayastha | Also used in trading communities |
| **Chanda / Chandra** | *candra* (moon) | Kayastha | Common in East Bengal |
| **Biswas** | *vishvāsa* (trust, confidence) | Mixed: Kayastha and Baidya | Also used across communities |

| **Majumdar** | Persian: *majmū-dār* (record-keeper) | Administrative title | Similar pan-caste honorific |
| **Roy / Rai** | *rāja* (king) | Administrative title | Broad usage; Kayastha, Baidya |

## 5. Bengali Baidya Surnames

### 5.1 The Baidya Community

[WELL-ESTABLISHED] The **Baidya** (or *Vaidya*, lit. "physician; one learned in the Vedas") community occupies the third position in the Bengali upper-caste trinity. A *jati* of traditional Ayurvedic physicians, Baidyas have historically claimed Brahmin-adjacent or Kshatriya status. Their kulin-ism paralleled that of the Brahmins and Kayasthas, dividing them into Radha Vaidyas, Varendra Vaidyas, and Sylheti Vaidyas, each with ranked sub-grades (Siddha, Saddhya, Kastha).

Manu's *Dharmashastra* classifies Ambashtha (*ambaṣṭha* = traditional name for the physician sub-caste) as offspring of a Brahmin father and Vaishya mother — giving Baidyas a claim to mixed Dvija heritage. In practice, Baidyas along with Kayasthas are classified by some as the highest category of *sat-sudra* (honorable Shudra).

### 5.2 Baidya Surname System

A distinctive feature of Baidya surnames is the **compound form**: the root surname (*Sen*, *Dash*/*Das*, *Datta*) combined with the Vaishya indicator *Gupta* (Sanskrit: *gupta*, "protected/hidden"). According to the Vaidyas of Bengal document: "The practice of writing Sengupta, Dasgupta seems to have started since the time of Raja Rajballabh (he used to sign as Rajballabh Sen Gupta), as a way to distinguish the Vaidyas from other castes bearing surnames like Sen, Datta etc."

| Surname | Origin | Notes |
|---------|--------|-------|
| **Sen** | *sena* (army/general) | Highest-status Baidya surname; linked to Sena dynasty; claimed Kshatriya |
| **Gupta** | *gupta* (protected, hidden) | Standalone; also common in North Indian Vaishya communities |
| **Sengupta** | *Sen* + *Gupta* | Compound distinguishing Baidya Sen from Kayastha Sen |
| **Dasgupta** | *Das* + *Gupta* | Distinguishes Baidya Das from Kayastha and lower-caste Das |
| **Duttagupta** | *Dutta* + *Gupta* | Rare; Baidya Datta |
| **Kar** | *kara* (doer) | Used in Baidya (Saddhya class); also Kayastha |
| **Dhar** | *dhāra* (holder, bearer) | Baidya Saddhya class; also Kayastha in some regions |
| **Rakshit** | *rakṣita* (protected, guarded) | Baidya; occupational — "protector/guardian" |
| **Chaudhuri** | Persian/Sanskrit title | Used across many upper castes; zamindar honorific |
| **Roy / Rai** | *rāja* (king) | Title used by some Baidyas |
| **Biswas** | *vishvāsa* (trust) | Both Baidya and Kayastha |

[WELL-ESTABLISHED] The Siddha class of Baidyas consists of three root surnames: **Das, Gupta, and Sen**. The Saddhya class includes: **Dutta, Duli, Dhar, and Kar**. This classification appears in the 16th-century texts *Chandraprabha* (Bharat Mullick, 1567) and *Baidya Kula Panjika* (Rama Kanta Das, 1575).

Notable Baidya figures: Sir Jagadish Chandra Bose (physicist), Rabindranath Tagore's family had Baidya connections, Bidhan Chandra Roy (physician and West Bengal's first Chief Minister, a Kayastha but with Baidya patronage).

## 6. Vaishya and Trading Community Surnames

### 6.1 The Subarnabanik (Gold Trader) Community

[WELL-ESTABLISHED] The **Subarnabanik** (also *Suvarna Banik*) — traditional gold traders — represent Bengal's major Vaishya community. They claim Vaishya *varna* status and constitute one of the "Nabasakha" (nine pure) artisan/trading communities. Their major surnames include:

| Surname | Meaning | Notes |
|---------|---------|-------|
| **Saha / Shah** | Sanskrit *sādhu* (honest, good) or merchant | Dominant Subarnabanik surname; also used by Tili, Napit, and other communities |
| **Poddar** | Sanskrit *potadāra* (treasurer, treasury-holder) | Prominent merchant family name |
| **Bhowmik / Bhoumik** | *bhaumika* (of the earth/land) | Landholding merchant families |

[WELL-ESTABLISHED] The Subarnabanik community suffered a significant status demotion under Ballala Sena, who reportedly downgraded them to Shudra when they refused to advance him the money he wanted (N.K. Sinha 1967; Suvarna Banik Wikipedia). By the 1901–1911 censuses, Subarnabaniks ranked third highest in literacy among Bengali Hindu castes — demonstrating that educational achievement and caste rank had diverged significantly under colonial modernity.

### 6.2 Gandhabanik (Scent/Spice Trader) Community

The Gandhabanik are traditional perfume and spice traders, organized into nine *gotras* and four territorial groups (*Desh, Sangha, Abat, Santrish*). Their family names (from the *Desh* group) include: **Saha, Sadhu, Laha, Khan, Rudra**. The *Auts* group family names include: **Datta, Dhar, Nag** — showing surname overlap with Kayastha and Baidya communities.

## 7. The Special Case of Das

### 7.1 A Surname Across Caste Lines

[WELL-ESTABLISHED] The surname **Das** (Sanskrit *dāsa*, "servant," often "servant of God") is unique in Bengali onomastics for its extraordinary spread across the social spectrum — from Brahmin communities (in Punjab) to high-caste Kayastha and Baidya families to Scheduled Castes.

In Bengal, Das appears in:
- **Kayastha Maulika Siddha** (first class): Part of the eight high Maulika families
- **Baidya Siddha class**: *Das* (alongside Gupta and Sen) is one of the three highest Baidya root surnames; appears as *Dasgupta* to distinguish from lower-caste usage
- **Mahishya community**: Agricultural Shudra caste with substantial Das population
- **Namasudra community**: Scheduled Caste community with heavy Das usage
- **Teli, Dhobi, Patni, and other artisan communities**: Das used across these groups

According to Wikipedia (*Das* surname): "Das is a common surname among various castes and classes of Bengalis including Baidya, Bengali Kayastha, Mahishya, Tantubay, and others. In Bengal, the surname is also used by both Scheduled Castes and General Castes."

### 7.2 The Social Implications

The cross-caste Das ambiguity created profound social complexity. A person named "Das" could be:
- A high-caste Baidya professional (Dasgupta compound marking this clearly)
- A Kayastha landowner (Das alone, without gupta suffix)
- A Namasudra farmer (Das with very different social position)

The **Dasgupta compound** therefore served as a social passport — a way for Baidya Dases to signal their distinction from lower-caste Dases. Without the gupta suffix, a Kayastha Das had to rely on other markers (education, occupation, village of origin) to establish position.

## 8. Dey vs. Das vs. Datta: A Direct Comparison

### 8.1 The Three Surnames in Comparative Perspective

| Feature | **Dey / De** | **Das** | **Datta / Dutt** |
|---------|--------------|---------|-----------------|
| Sanskrit root | *Deva* (divine/god) | *Dāsa* (servant) | *Datta* (given/gift) |
| Primary caste | Kayastha (Dakshina Radhi) | Multi-caste | Kayastha (Dakshina Radhi) |
| Kulin position | Maulika Siddha | Maulika Siddha | Maulika Siddha (refused Kulin myth) |
| Origin myth status | Among the 8 Siddha families | Among the 8 Siddha families | Originally Kulin-eligible; declined |
| Cross-caste usage | Moderate (Vaishya, Teli, etc.) | Very high (Brahmin to SC) | Lower cross-caste spread |
| Distinctive reputation | Divine/dynastic connotation | Devotional/servile connotation | Noble independence (refusal of servant status) |

[WELL-ESTABLISHED] All three — Dey, Das, and Datta — occupy the same first tier of Maulika Kayastha (the Siddha class). They are social peers within the Kayastha hierarchy. [UNCERTAIN] Whether Deys and Dattas historically intermarried freely is not well-documented, but given their equal Siddha Maulika status, such marriages would have been considered appropriate. The marriage barrier in practice ran primarily along the Kulin/Maulika divide — Kulin families preferred not to marry their daughters "down" to Maulika families, though hypergamy (Maulika daughter to Kulin son) was possible and documented.

### 8.2 The Datta-Dey Intermarriage Question

[UNCERTAIN] The specific question of Dey-Datta intermarriage versus Dey-Das intermarriage cannot be definitively resolved from available sources. However, the structural logic suggests:
- **Dey-Datta**: Both Siddha Maulika; comparable status; historically appropriate
- **Dey-Das**: Also both Siddha Maulika; appropriate within Kayastha context; but the cross-caste ambiguity of Das would require attention to *which* Das family (Kayastha Das vs. lower-caste Das)
- **Dey-Bose/Ghosh/Mitra**: Kulin-Maulika cross; the Kulin family giving a daughter to a Dey would have been considered a degradation of rank; a Dey family giving a daughter to a Kulin man (hypergamy) was possible

## 9. The Colonial Transformation of Surname Politics (1858–1947)

### 9.1 Anglicization and Its Social Meanings

[WELL-ESTABLISHED] British colonial rule produced a systematic anglicization of Bengali upper-caste surnames. The transformation was not random — it followed the phonological and social logic of the Bengali educated class adapting their identities to colonial administrative needs:

| Bengali Form | Anglicized Form | Mechanism |
|-------------|----------------|-----------|
| Bandyopadhyay | Banerjee | *Bandyo-* → *Baner-*; *-padhyay* → *-jee* |
| Mukhopadhyay | Mukherjee | *Mukho-* → *Mukher-*; *-padhyay* → *-jee* |
| Chattopadhyay | Chatterjee | *Chatto-* → *Chatter-*; *-padhyay* → *-jee* |
| Gangopadhyay | Ganguly/Ganguli | *Gango-* → *Ganguly*; symmetry from *Gangal* original |
| Bandyopadhyay | Banerjee (also Batabayal remained) | Some families kept vernacular forms |

[WELL-ESTABLISHED] The surname *De* (formal literary) versus *Dey* (colloquial) represents a similar transformation for Kayastha families — with *De* being the preferred form in educated colonial-era contexts (e.g., the litterateur Sukumar De), while *Dey* remained colloquial. This is not a caste distinction — both forms are used by the same community.

### 9.2 The 1901 Census Controversy

[WELL-ESTABLISHED] The colonial census, particularly the 1901 operations under H.H. Risley, became a major battleground for Bengali Kayastha identity. As Ghosh (2023, p. 340) documents: "The British reacted by defining them as a uniform caste and demoting them to Shudra category in ethnographic surveys. A report by Herbert Hope Risley and Edward Albert Gait cited their failure to observe scriptural dogma and rejection of the sacred thread as the reason for the loss of their upper-caste status."

The Kayastha community's response was organized: the **Kayastha Sabha** (caste organization) was formed, publishing *Kayastha Patrika* to argue against the Shudra classification. Bengali Kayasthas formally petitioned to be counted as Kshatriya in the 1901 census, citing:
- Their historical role as administrators and generals
- Rabindranath Tagore's argument that "the Dutts, Ghoshs and Guhas were of Kshatriya origin"
- Their high literacy and professional achievement

[WELL-ESTABLISHED] The 1884 Calcutta High Court ruling (*Rajcoomar Lal v. Bissessur Dayal*) had already adjudged the Shudra designation, though with the qualification that they had been "degraded from an earlier Kshatriya status." The 1931 census Bengal volume was, in Bellenoit's (2023) characterization, "disturbingly ambiguous" — associating Kayasthas with both the upper classes and the Shudra category simultaneously.

### 9.3 Education, Colonialism, and Caste Mobility

[WELL-ESTABLISHED] British colonial education and its associated economy fundamentally disrupted caste surname distinctions in practice. By the late 19th century, educated Kayasthas and Baidyas were serving alongside Brahmins in the ICS, legal profession, medicine, and journalism. The bhadralok class identity — built on English education, professional occupation, and urban residence — transcended older Kulin-Maulika distinctions within each caste.

The ironic consequence: by the time Kulin polygamy was abolished (declining from the late 19th century, largely extinct by independence), the Kulin status premium had already lost much of its practical social meaning for the educated urban class. A Dey from Calcutta with a BA from Presidency College and a government posting was not disadvantaged in marriage negotiations by his Maulika status — the bhadralok cultural capital had superseded it.

## 10. Geographic Distribution and the Partition Effect

### 10.1 Regional Concentrations Before 1947

[WELL-ESTABLISHED with UNCERTAIN quantitative specifics] Bengali surnames show regional concentrations connected to their communities' historical settlement patterns:

**West Bengal dominant surnames** (Rarh/Southwestern Bengal):
- Ghosh, Bose, Mitra: Concentrated in the Hooghly, Burdwan, Midnapore districts (Rarh region)
- Bandyopadhyay/Mukherjee/Chatterjee: Concentrated in same Rarh region

**East Bengal dominant surnames** (present-day Bangladesh):
- Bangaja Kayastha surnames (Guha as Kulin; various others)
- Dey/De: Strong presence in the Dhaka division, particularly **Munshiganj/Bikrampur** area — connected to the Deva dynasty of eastern Bengal (c. 1200–1294 CE)
- Das, Saha: Broader East Bengal distribution

**Assam/Tripura presence**:
- Deb: Strong North Bengal and Assam presence; connected to minor royal families
- Various Kayastha surnames among the Bengali communities of Assam and Tripura

### 10.2 The 1947 Partition Effect

[WELL-ESTABLISHED] The Partition of Bengal (1947) and subsequent migrations produced a major demographic reshaping of surname distributions:

**Migration patterns by caste**: Upper-caste Bengali Hindus — Brahmins, Kayasthas, and Baidyas — disproportionately migrated from East Pakistan (later Bangladesh) to West Bengal after 1947. This was followed by additional waves after the 1950 communal riots, the 1964–65 unrest, and the 1971 Liberation War.

**Consequences for Dey distribution**:
- The Munshiganj/Bikrampur Dey families (strongly associated with the Deva dynasty area) largely migrated to Calcutta and West Bengal between 1947–1971
- This created significant Dey populations in Calcutta, North 24-Parganas, and South 24-Parganas districts
- A substantial Dey community remained in Bangladesh, particularly in the Dhaka, Munshiganj, and Sylhet divisions

[UNCERTAIN] Precise census-level data on Dey surname distribution by district is not readily available from post-colonial sources, as Indian census after 1951 does not capture surname-level data, and Bangladesh census similarly focuses on religion rather than surname.

## 12. Implications for the Dey Family of Bengal

### 12.1 Dey's Position — A Realistic Assessment

The Dey/De surname occupies a position that is simultaneously:
1. **Well-placed within the upper-caste Kayastha complex** — one of the eight Siddha families, above 72 lower Maulika families, and part of the bhadralok social world
2. **Non-Kulin** — historically disadvantaged in marriage negotiations compared to Ghosh, Bose, Mitra families; daughters could not as easily access Kulin men
3. **Historically significant** — the connection to the Deva dynasty (Bikrampur, 12th–13th c.) gives the surname a distinctly eastern Bengal and royal administrative dimension
4. **Socially mobile** — by the colonial era, Dey families with education and professional positions (as clerks, zamindars, lawyers, etc.) functioned identically to Kulin families in practical social terms

### 12.2 The Bhadralok Achievement

[WELL-ESTABLISHED] By the late 19th century, the Kulin-Maulika distinction had become largely ceremonial among the educated urban class. A Dey family in Calcutta with access to English education, a professional career, and land ownership in their ancestral district would have been indistinguishable, in social practice, from a Mitra or Bose family of the same class.

The sociological point that emerges from Inden's analysis (1976) and the EPW Kayastha study (Pagani et al. 2017): clan rank "did not correlate with the political offices held by the Brahmans and Kayasthas in the Muslim regional administration or under the local Hindu rajas or landholders. Nor did it correlate with landholding sizes. In fact, some of the largest, most powerful landholders of the middle period were not Kulinas but Srotriyas or Maulikas."

A Maulika Dey zamindari family could be substantially wealthier and more politically powerful than a Kulin Ghosh family with little land.

### 12.3 The Surname in the Context of Bengali Identity Today

[WELL-ESTABLISHED] In post-independence India and Bangladesh, the functional social meaning of Kulin-Maulika distinctions has essentially disappeared among urban, educated Bengali Hindus. The bhadralok identity — defined by education, professional achievement, and cultural capital — has superseded the genealogical hierarchy. Dey remains a common, respected, and recognized upper-caste Bengali Kayastha surname.

In Bangladesh, the Dey/De surname marks a community that is both historically deep (Deva dynasty connections in Munshiganj) and increasingly dispersed through migration, with significant diasporic populations in Kolkata, Tripura, Assam, and internationally.

## 13. Summary: The Bengali Surname Ecosystem

The Bengali surname system represents one of history's most elaborate documented experiments in genealogical stratification. What began as a practical system of tracking village origins and lineage purity became, over eight centuries:

1. **A marriage regulation mechanism** — determining who could marry whom, at what status, with what bride-price or honor
2. **A social passport** — the kulaji recited at weddings as proof of lineage
3. **A political instrument** — used by Sena kings to manage their clergy and aristocracy
4. **A source of social pathology** — the Kulin polygamy crisis that destroyed the lives of thousands of women
5. **A colonial battleground** — fought over in census classifications and court cases
6. **A modernizing artifact** — hollowed of practical meaning by education and colonial mobility, but still psychologically and socially resonant

The Dey surname sits within this ecosystem as a **member of the inner circle of the Bengali Kayastha world** — not among the Kulin elite of three families, but solidly among the eight Siddha families that constituted the first rank of non-Kulin aristocracy. This is a position of genuine historical distinction.
