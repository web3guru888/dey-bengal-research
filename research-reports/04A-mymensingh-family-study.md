## Foreword

This report is the most focused and personally targeted document in the DEY-BENGAL research corpus. Where previous reports (1A–3A) cast the net wide — tracing the Dey surname across all of Bengal, from the Gupta Empire to the global Bengali diaspora — this study narrows its gaze to one town, one family, and, ultimately, one man: **Aswini Kumar De**, umbrella manufacturer of Mymensingh Burra Bazar, whose grandson Robin Dey commissioned this research.

We know Aswini Kumar De existed — precisely, and in his own words — because of an extraordinary primary source: *Loke Nath Sen v. Aswini Kumar Dey*, Calcutta High Court, 1 September 1937 (AIR 1938 Cal 216). In this trademark infringement case, Aswini Kumar testified about his business, his brand, his market position, and the way customers recognised his umbrellas by name. The case record, preserved in the archives of Indian law, gives us a rare window into a single Bengali Hindu family's commercial life in the final decade before Partition.

Around that documented moment, this report builds outward — backward in time through seven centuries of Kayastha history in eastern Bengal, and forward through the catastrophic disruption of 1947, which almost certainly ended the Dey family's long occupation of Mymensingh soil.

## Part I — The Place: Mymensingh and Its World

### 1.1 Geography: The Old Brahmaputra Frontier

Mymensingh town stands on the western bank of the **Old Brahmaputra River** — the former main channel of one of the world's great river systems, abandoned by the river in the late eighteenth century when the Brahmaputra shifted course westward to its present Jamuna channel. The shift was catastrophic for Mymensingh's trade: the town had been a river port of consequence, with sea-going vessels from England ascending from the Bay of Bengal. After 1787 — the year the district was formally constituted by the East India Company — the river that made the town shrank to a seasonal flow, and Mymensingh reinvented itself as an administrative and commercial inland centre rather than a trading port.

The district's geography shaped its social history in crucial ways. Bounded in the north by the forested Garo Hills of what is now Meghalaya, Mymensingh was always a **frontier district** — "loosely-controlled by the state and a frontier area dividing the more populous regions of Bengal from the Garo Hills" (Wikipedia, *Mymensingh District*). This frontier character meant that Mymensingh was settled relatively late by the waves of Hindu zamindari families who colonized eastern Bengal. When they came, they came in force.

### 1.2 The Colonial District: Established 1787

The district of Mymensingh was established by the East India Company in **1787**, drawn from territories that had previously been part of the Niabat (Sub-Province of Dhaka) and various zamindari estates including Atia, Kagmari, Pukuria, and Patiladaha. Its original extent was vast — it once included what are now the separate districts of Tangail, Jamalpur, Kishoreganj, Sherpur, and Netrokona. At its greatest extent, Mymensingh was said to have been the **largest district of the Indian subcontinent**.

The town of Mymensingh itself was earlier known as **Nasirabad** (a Mughal-period toponym) and as **Momenshahi** (a popular corruption of "Mominshahi," possibly honoring a local Muslim notable). These two names indicate the district's ambiguous character: a frontier region that had been formally incorporated into the Mughal administrative system but remained a zone of competing zamindari authority and limited central control.

By the time of the Permanent Settlement (1793), Mymensingh was governed by several major zamindari families:
- **Maharajas of Susang** (third in Order of Precedence at Government House, Calcutta — a remarkable rank for an eastern Bengal family)
- **Muktagacha Estate** (also called "Mymensingh zamindari")
- **Ramgopalpur** and **Gouripur** estates
- Smaller taluqdari holdings scattered across the district

These zamindars were the dominant social class — and crucially, as the census record confirms, the Hindu zamindari and professional class of Mymensingh was heavily Kayastha.

### 1.3 The Hindu Community of Mymensingh: Predominantly Hindu in the Town

A critical demographic fact: according to the 1941 Census of India (the last British census before Partition), **19.95% of the Mymensingh district population was Hindu** — approximately 321,264 people out of 1.6 million. Within Mymensingh town itself, the proportion was much higher: multiple sources confirm that *"during the British Raj most of the inhabitants of the town were Hindus"* (History of Bangladesh blog; Traveltill.com; Bangladesh.com).

This is a pattern repeated across eastern Bengal's district towns: while the rural hinterland was predominantly Muslim (many having converted since the Sultanate period), the urban centres — where commerce, administration, law, and education concentrated — were dominated by Hindu families, particularly from the upper castes (Brahmin, Baidya, Kayastha). The Kayastha community's traditional role as scribes, revenue officials, lawyers, and merchants made them the backbone of district-town commercial life.

### 1.4 Kayasthas in Mymensingh: The Second-Largest Concentration in Bengal

The most striking demographic fact about Mymensingh from the perspective of the Dey family is this: according to H.H. Risley's 1891 ethnographic survey, Mymensingh district contained **105,537 Kayasthas** — the **second-highest concentration of Kayasthas in all of Bengal**, exceeded only by Dhaka district.

This statistic is not coincidental. It reflects:

1. **The Bangaja origin**: Mymensingh falls within the cultural and geographical sphere of **Vangaja (Bangaja) Kayastha** settlement — the eastern Bengal branch of Bengali Kayastha society, historically distinct from the Daksina Radhi (western/Rarhi) branch. Bangaja Kayasthas are associated with eastern Bengal from the Dhaka division northward through Mymensingh, and their genealogical traditions trace their social organization to the Deva-era Chandradwip (Barisal) kingdom.

2. **The River Network**: Mymensingh's river connectivity (Old Brahmaputra, Kangsha, Narsunda) allowed relatively comfortable communication with Dhaka and the broader Gangetic system, facilitating both trade and the movement of educated families seeking administrative appointments.

3. **Zamindari concentration**: The large zamindari estates of Mymensingh employed substantial numbers of Kayastha *naib* (deputy), *muktear* (attorney), and *munim* (accountant) staff. These professional Kayastha households accumulated over generations, creating the large community Risley counted in 1891.

Aswini Kumar De, operating his umbrella business from **Burra Bazar** in Mymensingh town in the 1910s–1930s, was embedded in this dense Kayastha social world. His neighbours and customers, his competitors and courtroom witnesses — all inhabited the same upper-caste Hindu commercial milieu that defined Mymensingh town before 1947.

## Part II — The Surname: How "De" Reached Mymensingh

### 2.1 The Sanskrit Root: Seven Centuries of Phonological Change

The surname borne by Aswini Kumar — variously spelled **De** (formal/legal) and **Dey** (colloquial) — derives from the Sanskrit root **Deva** (देव), meaning "divine," "celestial," "god-like." The phonological evolution follows a standard Bengali pattern:

> **Deva → Dev → Deb → De / Dey**

This is not merely a modern transliteration issue. The transition from the hard 'b' of 'Deb' to the 'e'/'ey' endings reflects a genuine phonological shift that occurred in medieval Bengali, in which terminal consonants softened and final vowels were elided. The formal legal spelling "De" (as in "Aswini Kumar De" on the trademark design) represents the educated/written form; "Dey" (as in the case title *Aswini Kumar Dey*) represents the colloquial Bengali pronunciation as anglicised.

### 2.2 The Dynastic Connection: The Vaishnava Deva Dynasty (c. 1231–1294 CE)

The most historically significant crystallisation event for this surname was the rise and fall of the **Vaishnava Deva dynasty** of Bikrampur, which ruled eastern Bengal — including regions north toward Mymensingh — from approximately 1231 to 1294 CE.

This dynasty was founded by **Purushottamadeva**, who rose from the position of *gramani* (village headman) — a non-royal origin that is itself significant, suggesting the Deva family were not Kshatriya aristocrats but ambitious local administrators. His son **Madhusudanadeva** (also Madhumathana) took the title *nripati* (lord of men). The dynasty's most powerful ruler, **Damodaradeva** (r. 1231–1243 CE), is documented through three copper-plate inscriptions — the Mehar plate (1234 CE), the Shobharampur plate (1236 CE), and the Chittagong/Nasirabad plate (1243 CE) — which form the primary documentary record of the dynasty.

The final recorded event of the Deva dynasty came in **1294 CE**, when **Bikramaditya Deva** retreated eastward as Islamic forces under the Lakhnauti Sultanate pressed deeper into Bengal. By 1322 CE, under **Shamsuddin Firuz Shah**, Muslim political authority was consolidated across eastern Bengal, and the Deva polity ceased to exist as an independent entity.

The scholarly consensus is that the surname De/Dey crystallised as a hereditary marker during this period of **social disruption** — precisely when Hindu families displaced from or dispossessed by Deva administrative service needed stable markers of identity. The mechanism was simple: families who had served the Deva court, or who bore *-deva* as part of their personal names as an honorific suffix (extremely common in Bengal from the Gupta period onward), found the Deva name preserved as a heritable surname as the dynasty collapsed around them.

This crystallisation was **accelerated by Islamic conquest**: when the Muslim administration replaced Sanskrit/Bengali names with Persian titles for its new officials, Hindu families who remained outside the new administrative apparatus had strong incentives to preserve their pre-conquest identity through fixed surnames. "De/Dey" became such a marker.

### 2.3 The Two Kayastha Systems: Which One Are the Mymensingh Deys?

A critical discovery from our Phase 6B research concerns the **two separate Kayastha classificatory systems** in Bengal, which map onto the two historically distinct regions of Bengal:

**System 1: Daksina-Radhi / Rarhi** (western Bengal, centred on Murshidabad, Birbhum, Burdwan, Hooghly)
- This is the system discussed in most general sources on Bengali Kayastha hierarchy
- Three Kulin clans: Ghosh, Bose/Basu, Mitra
- Siddha Maulika tier includes: Dey, Datta, Singha, Nandi, etc.
- Associated with the Sena dynasty's reforms and Ballala Sena's (c. 1097–1160 CE) codification of the Kulin system

**System 2: Bangaja / Vangaja** (eastern Bengal, centred on Dhaka, Bikrampur, Mymensingh, Barisal, Chittagong)
- This is the system directly relevant to the Mymensingh Dey family
- Organised around the **Chandradwip kingdom** (modern Barisal), whose ruling Deb family was the *samaj-pati* (president) of all Bangaja Kayasthas
- Five tiers: (1) Kulin, (2) Siddha, (3) Sadhya, (4) Mahapatra, (5) Amulaja
- Within this system, **Dey/Deb** families are classified as **4th tier: Mahapatra** (literally "great-vessel/title"), a position of substantial dignity but not Kulin elevation
- Risley (1891) specifically documented the Chandradwip family as *"samaj-pati (president) of all Bangaja Kayasthas for 17 generations"*

The **implication for Robin Dey's ancestry** is significant: his grandfather Aswini Kumar De, operating in Mymensingh, would have been classified within the **Bangaja Kayastha system** as a Mahapatra-tier Dey — not within the Rarhi/Siddha-Maulika classification that most general sources describe. Both are upper-caste Kayastha positions, but they represent different genealogical traditions.

### 2.4 The Bangaja Origin Tradition: Danujmardan Deb and Chandradwip

The Bangaja Kayastha tradition traces its organized social structure to the kingdom of **Chandradwip** (modern Barisal Division), founded c. 1280 CE by **Danujmardan Deb** (also Danujmardan Deva, or Danuja-Marddana-Deva in inscriptions). This king — who is described in the Bengali verse *"danujmardan raja chandradwip-pati, sei hoilo bangaja kayastha gosthipati"* ("King Danujmardan, lord of Chandradwip, became the head of the Bangaja Kayastha community") — named his kingdom after his guru Chandrasekhar Bhattacharya and established himself as the head of all Bangaja Kayastha families in eastern Bengal.

The Chandradwip dynasty lasted approximately 500 years (c. 1280–1799 CE) through three ruling families (Deb → Basu → Mitra Mazumdar). Its genealogical significance is profound: as the recognised *samaj-pati* of all Bangaja Kayasthas, the Chandradwip rulers served as the apex of the kinship network that included the Mymensingh Dey families. When Kayastha families in Mymensingh arranged marriages, adjudicated rank disputes, or maintained their kulagrantha genealogies, the Chandradwip tradition provided the framework.

The geographic logic is clear: Chandradwip (Barisal) is the riverine hub of the Bengal delta, connected by waterway to Dhaka (and thence to Mymensingh) via the interconnected delta river systems. The same Brahmaputra-Old Brahmaputra system that made Mymensingh commercially viable also connected it to the Barisal-Dhaka social network of Bangaja Kayastha families.

## Part III — The Dey Family in Mymensingh: What We Can Infer Before Aswini Kumar

### 3.1 The Absence of a Paper Trail

Before the 1937 court case, we have **no documented record specifically naming Aswini Kumar De's ancestors**. This is typical for Kayastha families of the Maulika/Mahapatra tier: unlike the great zamindari families (whose land records, revenue settlements, and estate papers have sometimes survived), the smaller commercial and professional Kayastha families of district towns rarely appear in the surviving documentary record until the twentieth century.

However, the broader historical and demographic evidence allows us to construct a **probabilistic family history** — a reconstruction of the conditions, social world, and likely trajectory of the Dey ancestors who preceded Aswini Kumar in Mymensingh. This is not fiction; it is informed historical inference from the evidence we do have.

### 3.2 When Did Dey Families Arrive in Mymensingh? (c. 15th–18th Century)

During the late 15th and early 16th centuries, **many families which would later become prominent zamindars arrived in [Mymensingh] and were granted ownership of lands by the Bengal Sultans** (Wikipedia, *Mymensingh District*). This period — the height of the Bengal Sultanate under Alauddin Husain Shah (r. 1493–1519 CE) — was the great age of Kayastha consolidation in eastern Bengal.

Kayastha families from the Dhaka/Bikrampur heartland (the former Deva dynasty territory) would have been well-positioned to receive sultan-era land grants or to move northward into the newly opening Mymensingh frontier. The Mughal conquest of Bengal under Akbar (Bengal Subah established 1576) further accelerated this process: Mymensingh was part of the Sarkar of Bazuha, and the Mughal revenue system required trained Kayastha *qanungos* (hereditary land-record keepers) and *peshkars* (scribes) throughout the district.

By the **Permanent Settlement of 1793**, Kayastha families were entrenched in Mymensingh's agrarian and administrative hierarchy. The 1793 settlement "froze" property rights at the zamindari level, but below the great zamindars sat hundreds of smaller taluqdari (sub-zamindar) and professional families — Kayastha *naibs*, lawyers, scribes, and merchants — who formed the backbone of district civil society.

### 3.3 The Professional Pathway: From Scribal to Commercial

The most likely ancestral trajectory for Aswini Kumar's family runs through the characteristic Kayastha vocational path:

**Generation 1–3 (c. 1790–1850)**: Post-Permanent Settlement Kayastha household, probably holding a small taluqdari interest or employed as a *mukhtar* (registered agent), *naib* (deputy), or *munim* (accountant) in one of the Mymensingh zamindaris. Literate in Bengali and possibly Persian; later in English as the colonial educational infrastructure expanded.

**Generation 3–5 (c. 1850–1900)**: The railway arrived in Mymensingh from Dhaka in **1884** (Eastern Bengal Railway), transforming the town's commercial geography. The railway era opened new possibilities for trade: goods could move faster, and commercial entrepreneurs could connect Mymensingh to Calcutta and the broader Bengal market. This is the period when Kayastha families who had previously depended on scribal-administrative employment began moving into trade and small-scale manufacture.

**Generation 5–6 (c. 1900–1912)**: Aswini Kumar De established his umbrella business. The court record states that *"the complainant's business is of over 25 years' standing"* at the time of the 1937 case — placing the founding of the business at approximately **1912**. But the claim that Aswini Kumar *founded* the business in 1912 does not preclude earlier family involvement in the trade. It is possible that the business was inherited or reorganised from an earlier family enterprise.

### 3.4 The Kayastha Social Network in Mymensingh Town

In the late colonial period, Mymensingh town's Hindu upper-caste community — particularly the Kayastha families — formed a tightly interconnected social world. The major institutions that organised this world were:

- **The Durga Puja committees**: The great autumn festival was organised by prominent Kayastha families, who competed in the scale and grandeur of their puja pandals. The umbrella trade would have been particularly active in the months leading up to the monsoon (April–June), positioning a successful umbrella manufacturer like Aswini Kumar as a person of some commercial consequence.

- **The *samaj* (caste assembly)**: Bangaja Kayastha families maintained a *samaj* — a community assembly that adjudicated marriage alliances, rank disputes, and social sanctions. The Chandradwip tradition gave this samaj its organizational framework.

- **The legal-professional class**: Mymensingh Zilla School and later Ananda Mohan College (established 1901) educated the sons of Kayastha families, many of whom entered the colonial legal and administrative professions. The very fact that Aswini Kumar De successfully prosecuted a trademark case before a Sessions Judge, and then defended the appeal before Justice Biswas at the Calcutta High Court, indicates that he had access to legal counsel — almost certainly through Kayastha professional networks.

- **The *ghatak* (matrimonial broker)**: The Kayastha ghatak maintained the *kulagrantha* (genealogical register) that recorded marriages, births, and the rank of families within the Bangaja system. It is through ghatak records — if they survive — that the Dey family's specific genealogy within the Mahapatra tier could potentially be traced. The destruction of records by the "Mad King" Shivnarayan Mitra of Chandradwip (who *burned his kingdom's records* in the 18th century) was a catastrophic loss for the broader Bangaja kulagrantha tradition, though some records survive in scattered hands.

## Part IV — Aswini Kumar De: The Man, the Brand, and the Court

### 4.1 What We Know with Certainty

From the Calcutta High Court judgment *Loke Nath Sen v. Aswini Kumar Dey*, AIR 1938 Cal 216, decided 1 September 1937, the following facts are established with documentary certainty:

**Personal details:**
- **Full name**: Aswini Kumar De (spelled *De* in the trademark; *Dey* in the case title — both are his name)
- **Location**: Mymensingh Burra Bazar, Bengal Presidency
- **Religion/community**: Hindu (implied by the Sanskrit name *Aswini* = *Ashvini*, the twin celestial physicians of Vedic mythology)
- **Occupation**: Umbrella manufacturer and dealer

**Business details:**
- **Trade name**: "Aswini Chhati" (অশ্বিনী ছাতি — *Aswini's Umbrella*; *chhati/chhata* is the Bengali word for umbrella)
- **Business standing**: "Over 25 years" as of 1937 — established c. **1912**
- **Registered trademark**: Design registered as declaration of ownership on **23 August 1926** under the Registration Act
- **Registry number**: **964** (inscribed on all trademark designs, with a warning to purchasers to verify this number)

**The trademark design** (described in minute detail in the judgment):
> *"a swan holding a closed umbrella between its beaks in the centre, with the name 'Aswini Kumar De' in prominent type stretching over it like an arch from end to end, and the figure 8 A 4 (the letter 'A' overlapping the digits 8 and 4) on the top of it. Below in smaller type are the words 'Mymensingh Burra Bazar', while in the space between, in slightly smaller type, are the words 'Trade Mark' and 'Registry No. 964' in two lines, intercepted in the middle by the figure of the swan."*

Additionally, the umbrella bore a **secondary oval impression** containing "Aswini Kumar De Marka" (*aswini kumar de marka* = "Aswini Kumar De brand") and the figure 964, with a consumer warning to verify the registry number.

**Market recognition**: Multiple witnesses testified that customers in Mymensingh asked for umbrellas by the name "Aswini Chhata" without specifying any other details. Witness P.W. 7 stated: *"By Aswini Chhata, people mean the umbrella with Aswini brand manufactured by Aswini Kumar Dey of Burrabazar, Mymensingh. I do not know of any other brand of Aswini Chhata in the market."* This testimony establishes that "Aswini Chhata" was not merely a trade name but a market institution — a vernacular synonym for quality umbrellas in Mymensingh's marketplace.

### 4.2 The Three Brands: Reading a Business

The judgment reveals that Aswini Kumar manufactured **three distinct brands** of umbrellas:

1. **Brand 1** (the flagship): Bears the full registered design — the swan + "Aswini Kumar De" arch + "8 A 4" figure + "Mymensingh Burra Bazar" + "Trade Mark" + "Registry No. 964." This was the premium brand.

2. **Brand 2**: Bears simply the name "Aswini Kumar" — a simpler, presumably lower-cost product.

3. **Brand 3**: Bears "Aswini Kumar De" — the name alone, without the full design.

The existence of three separate product tiers tells us something important: this was **not a tiny artisanal operation** but a structured commercial enterprise with differentiated product lines serving different market segments. A single manufacturer with three brands, a registered trademark, and 25 years of market presence was, by the standards of an eastern Bengal district town in the 1920s–1930s, a **substantial business**.

### 4.3 The Swan: A Heraldic Choice

The choice of a **swan** (*hans* or *maraghini* in Bengali) as the central motif of the trademark is worth dwelling on. In Bengali iconography, the swan is associated with:

- **Saraswati**, the goddess of learning, arts, and wisdom — whose mount is the swan or goose (*hamsa*)
- **Purity and discernment** — the *paramahansa* ("supreme swan") is the highest designation for a realized spiritual teacher in the Hindu tradition; the swan was said to be able to separate milk from water, symbolizing wisdom's ability to discern the real from the unreal
- **Brahminical and upper-caste associations** — the swan image was used by learned and cultured families

For a Kayastha family with aspirations of cultural standing, the swan was a natural choice as a commercial emblem: it evoked learning, purity, and upper-caste Hindu identity, while being visually distinctive and easily recognisable even to semi-literate consumers who might ask for "the umbrella with the bird."

The figure **"8 A 4"** on the trademark is more opaque. It may represent a **quality designation** (a grade marker, or an original registration number from an earlier system), or possibly a **date code** (though 1884 and 1848 are the obvious readings, neither connects obviously to the business's 1912 founding).

### 4.4 The Case: A Trademark Battle in Colonial Mymensingh

The court case tells an absorbing story of commercial competition in a small Bengali town. The accused, **Loke Nath Sen** of Chhota Bazar, Mymensingh (note: *Chhota Bazar* = "Small Market," as distinct from *Burra Bazar* = "Large Market"), manufactured umbrellas with a design strikingly similar to Aswini Kumar's:

- A swan (holding an **open** umbrella, versus Aswini Kumar's **closed** umbrella)
- The name **"Sree Aswini Kumar Das"** arching over it (substituting "Das" for "De" — a very deliberate choice of a common and similar-sounding Bengali surname)
- The figure **"84"** (without the "A" between 8 and 4 that distinguishes Aswini Kumar's version)
- Below: **"proprietor Sree Loke Nath Sen"** and **"Mymensingh Chhota Bazar"**
- A fake registry number: **"965"** (attempting to appropriate the authority of Aswini Kumar's registered "964")

Loke Nath Sen's design is a masterclass in deliberate counterfeiting: it replicated every element of Aswini Kumar's brand while providing minimal legal distance — different surname (Das not De), open not closed umbrella, 965 not 964. The Sessions Judge convicted Sen under both Sections 482 and 486 of the Indian Penal Code (false trade mark; selling goods with counterfeit trade marks) and fined him Rs. 100 under each section.

Sen appealed to the Calcutta High Court, where Justice Biswas ultimately **set aside the conviction** — not because Sen was innocent of copying, but on the technical ground that the prosecution had failed to establish Aswini Kumar's design as a legally "distinctive" trade mark under Indian law (there being, at the time, no formal trademark registration statute in India, only registration of declarations of ownership under the Registration Act). The judge noted that "the figure 84 and a swan" were common features used by multiple umbrella manufacturers in Mymensingh, citing competitors Butta Kristo Paul and Sons, Ashutosh Paul, and Nagendra Nath Ghatak — all of whom used similar motifs.

**What the judgment reveals about the umbrella trade in Mymensingh**: There was a **thriving, competitive cottage/light-industry umbrella trade** in Mymensingh town in the 1920s–1930s. Multiple manufacturers operated simultaneously: Aswini Kumar De at Burra Bazar, Loke Nath Sen at Chhota Bazar, plus Butta Kristo Paul and Sons, Ashutosh Paul, and Nagendra Nath Ghatak. The fact that multiple manufacturers used the "swan + 84" motif suggests this combination had become a visual shorthand for quality umbrellas in the Mymensingh market — which is precisely why it needed protecting, and precisely why Aswini Kumar sought registered protection for his specific version.

### 4.5 Aswini Kumar's Testimony: Reading the Voice Behind the Case

Beyond the dry facts of the judgment, Aswini Kumar's own deposition (as reported in the judgment) gives us a glimpse of his voice:

*"He himself deposed that he had 'registered' the mark, that is, had registered a declaration of ownership in respect of it, and that his umbrella with this trade mark was known in the market as 'Aswini Chhata'"*

*"Shown an umbrella with the trade-mark 84-Sree Aswini Kumar Ghatak a swan holding an open umbrella between its bills, the witness says: 'I have not seen an umbrella with similar brand in the market before this day.'"*

*"Exhibit 1 (c) is an umbrella manufactured by Butta Kristo Paul and Sons. I have also seen umbrellas manufactured by Ashutosh Paul. Its trade-mark also consists of the figure 84 and a swan holding an umbrella between its bills."*

The second and third statements are remarkably candid for a complainant in a court case: having first claimed he had never seen a similar design, he then concedes under cross-examination that he had. This combination of commercial aggressiveness (prosecuting a competitor) with a certain businesslike transparency is the impression of a man comfortable in the colonial legal system but not sophisticated enough to maintain a purely strategic courtroom posture. He was a manufacturer, not a lawyer.

### 4.6 The Business Name as Personal Identity

Throughout the case and the associated evidence, Aswini Kumar's business name and personal name are virtually inseparable. His umbrella does not have a separate brand name detached from his identity — it is simply called "Aswini Chhata" (Aswini's Umbrella). The brand *is* the man. His name arches over the trademark design like an inscription on a temple gate.

This fusion of personal name and commercial identity is characteristic of a specific type of Bengali Hindu merchant: a **first-generation or second-generation entrepreneur** who built a business from scratch through personal reputation, and whose entire commercial standing rested on the singular association of his name with his product's quality. In a town where customers asked for "Aswini Chhata" by name, he had built something rare: a genuine brand, in the modern sense, before the word "brand" was in common commercial use.

The fact that Loke Nath Sen chose to imitate not just the design but the *name* "Aswini Kumar" (using "Das" instead of "De") is the clearest possible testimony to how valuable that personal-brand equity had become.

## Part V — Before Aswini Kumar: The Deep Genealogical Picture

### 5.1 The Bangaja Tier and the Mahapatra Status

Within the Bangaja Kayastha system, the Dey/Deb surname occupies the **Mahapatra tier** — described in Risley (1891) and in Bengali kulagrantha traditions as the 4th of five ranks, comprising 19 named families. The list of Mahapatra families includes: "Sen, Deb/Dey, Sinha, Raha, Rakshit, Palit, Shome, Bhadra, Dam, Kar, Pal, Dhar, Chanda, Ankur, Adya, Nandan, Bishnu, Kundu, Nandy."

The placement of **Deb/Dey at second position** — immediately after Sen — is significant within the logic of the kulagrantha tradition. It indicates that among the 19 Mahapatra families, the Dey/Deb community occupied a position of particular respect. In eastern Bengal, where the Bangaja system prevailed, this translated into practical social advantages: easier marriage alliances, clearer social standing, and greater community prestige within the Kayastha *samaj*.

### 5.2 The Deva Dynasty and Administrative Ancestry

The deepest plausible genealogical connection for a Mymensingh Dey family runs through the **administrative class of the Vaishnava Deva dynasty** (c. 1231–1294 CE). When Damodaradeva and his successors issued copper-plate land grants from Bikrampur, the grants were witnessed and executed by officials — *amatyas*, *vishayapatis*, scribes designated as Kayastha — whose names ending in *-deva* would have been the precise phonological ancestors of the "De/Dey" surname cluster.

The **Chittagong copper plate of 1243 CE** (the most important Damodaradeva plate) and the Shobharampur plate of 1236 CE (from Dhaka district, close to the Mymensingh border) both fall within the administrative geography that produced the ancestors of Mymensingh's Kayastha families. If the witness lists of these plates — not yet fully transcribed in accessible scholarship — contain names ending in *-deva*, these are the possible nominal ancestors of the entire De/Dey surname community in eastern Bengal.

The collapse of the Deva dynasty in 1294 CE was, for these administrative families, a moment of rupture and reconstruction. Those who had served the Deva court were now without royal patrons. Some would have adapted into the new Islamic administrative apparatus; others would have retreated to smaller, more autonomous positions as village zamindars, revenue collectors, and scribes for whichever authority prevailed locally. Over the subsequent two centuries (1294–c. 1500), as Bengal was reorganised under successive sultans, these Kayastha families accumulated small landholdings and consolidated their social position. By the time the Mughals arrived in 1576, they were ready to serve as *qanungos* and revenue officials throughout the Bengal Subah — the same positions that would eventually, through the Permanent Settlement, convert into zamindari proprietorship.

### 5.3 The Mughal *Qanungo* Tradition in Eastern Bengal

**Irfan Habib's** analysis of the Mughal revenue system is directly relevant here: "By the eighteenth century, Kayasthas' control of the *qanungo* position had essentially become hereditary" — meaning that across four to five generations of Mughal Bengal (c. 1576–1757), a specific Kayastha family could hold the same *qanungo* post, passing it from father to son. This hereditary service had three consequences:

1. It provided **income security** — the *qanungo* received a share of the revenue he processed.
2. It built **land knowledge** — the hereditary *qanungo* accumulated encyclopaedic knowledge of local land ownership, boundaries, and revenue history. This knowledge was power.
3. It created **genealogical continuity** — the *qanungo* family became identified with a specific territory, making them embedded members of that territory's landed hierarchy.

For a Mymensingh Dey family, three to five generations of *qanungo* service (c. 1600–1780) would have created the precise conditions for a later transition into either zamindari proprietorship (under the Permanent Settlement) or — if the family lacked the capital to maintain zamindari status — into trade and manufacture. Aswini Kumar De's umbrella business, established c. 1912, may represent a family that had been zamindars or taluqdars in the Permanent Settlement era and, having lost that status through the economic pressures of the late 19th century (the distress of smaller zamindars was chronic by the 1890s), reinvented itself as commercial manufacturers.

## Part VI — The Genetic Picture: Who Are the Mymensingh Deys, Biologically?

### 6.1 The Four-Component Bengali Ancestry

The biological ancestry of an upper-caste Bangaja Kayastha family from Mymensingh — such as Aswini Kumar De and his descendants — can be described through the framework established by Reich et al. (2009), Narasimhan et al. (2019), and the Pagani et al. (2017) study of Bengali Kayasthas.

The ancestry is a four-way mixture, accumulated over sixty millennia:

**1. Ancient Ancestral South Indian (AASI) — ~20–30%**
The oldest genetic layer. These are the descendants of the first anatomically modern humans to arrive in the Indian subcontinent from Africa, approximately **65,000–70,000 years ago**. They were hunter-gatherers who had separated from other human populations — most closely related to the indigenous Andamanese — and had lived in South Asia for tens of thousands of years before any of the other ancestry layers arrived. They likely spoke languages unrelated to any modern Indian language family. In a Bengali upper-caste genome, they appear as the "South Asian" component at its most ancient — present in roughly equal proportions across all Bengali communities, slightly diluted in upper castes relative to lower castes.

**2. Iranian Neolithic / IVC-related ancestry — ~30–40%**
The second layer arrived from the west. The Indus Valley Civilization (IVC) people — who built Harappa, Mohenjo-daro, and the thousands of smaller sites of the Saraswati-Sindhu system — were themselves a mixture of Iranian hunter-gatherer ancestry and AASI. The Rakhigarhi individual sequenced by Shinde et al. (2019) shows precisely this composition: ~50–60% Iranian hunter-gatherer, ~40–50% AASI, and **zero steppe ancestry**. As the IVC's population dispersed after c. 1900 BCE, they mixed with other South Asian populations, contributing this Iranian-AASI compound to virtually all modern South Asians.

**3. Bronze Age Steppe / Indo-Aryan — ~12–20%**
This is the most historically charged component. Steppe pastoralists from the Sintashta-Andronovo culture of the Central Asian steppes (modern Kazakhstan/southern Russia), carrying Y-chromosome haplogroup **R1a-Z93**, arrived in South Asia in two waves: first through Central Asian BMAC populations (c. 2100–1800 BCE), then more directly after c. 1500 BCE. These are the **Indo-Aryan speakers** who brought the precursors of Sanskrit, the Vedic hymns, the horse, the chariot, and the ritual fire cult to South Asia. In Bengali Kayasthas, this component registers at an estimated **12–20%** — higher than the Bengal average (~8–14%), lower than North Indian Brahmins (~20–30%), reflecting both geographic distance from the steppe entry point (northwest) and the diluting effect of the East Asian component unique to Bengal.

**4. Austro-Asiatic / East Asian — ~7–12%**
The most uniquely Bengali component. Around **1,500–2,000 BCE**, rice-farming populations from Southeast Asia — ancestral to the modern Munda-speaking tribal peoples (Santhal, Ho, Mundari) of eastern India — expanded westward into the Bengal delta. They carried Y-chromosome haplogroup **O2a-M95** and brought rice cultivation, Austro-Asiatic languages (from which scattered loanwords survive in Bengali), and distinctive genetic markers. Upper-caste Bengali families carry a smaller proportion of this ancestry (~7–12%) than the tribal Santhal (50–65%), but it is nonetheless a real and significant component. This is what makes Bengali DNA distinctive among South Asian populations: *Bengali is the only South Asian population regularly used as a proxy in genetic studies specifically because it is the only major South Asian population that harbours detectable East Asian ancestry*.

### 6.2 Y-Chromosome Probabilities for Robin Dey

For Robin Dey, whose patrilineal descent runs through Aswini Kumar De and the Bangaja Kayastha community of Mymensingh, the most likely paternal haplogroup (Y-DNA) is one of:

| Haplogroup | Probability | What It Means |
|------------|-------------|---------------|
| **R1a-Z93** | ~35–45% | Direct paternal descent from the Bronze Age Indo-Aryan steppe migration. R1a is found at highest frequency in North Indian Brahmins (55–65%) and at substantial frequency in Bengali Kayasthas. If Robin carries R1a, his unbroken paternal line connects to Bronze Age Central Asia, ~2,000–1,500 BCE. |
| **H-M69** (or subclades) | ~15–25% | The oldest South Asian paternal lineage. H is found in all South Asian communities but at highest frequency in tribal and lower-caste groups; its presence in Kayastha families reflects the deep indigenous ancestry of the Bengal delta going back 35,000–50,000 years. |
| **J2-M172** | ~8–15% | Associated with the westward spread of Neolithic farming and possibly with populations ancestral to the IVC. J2 entered South Asia from the west and is found in moderate proportions in all upper-caste Indian communities. |
| **R2-M124** | ~5–12% | An ancient South Asian haplogroup related to R1a but diverged much earlier, before the steppe migration. Present across South Asia with no obvious directional correlation to caste. |
| **O2a-M95** | ~5–10% | The Austro-Asiatic signature. Its presence in Kayastha males represents ancestral mixing with Munda-related populations at some point before the emergence of fixed caste endogamy. |

The personal DNA test Robin could take — **Family Tree DNA's Big Y-700** for Y-chromosome analysis, combined with an autosomal test from any major provider — would definitively answer which of these haplogroups runs in his paternal line, and would provide R-squared values against thousands of other tested individuals to identify likely cousins and ancestral population clusters.

### 6.3 What the Genetics Cannot Tell Us

Population genetics illuminates the *aggregate* history of communities, not the specific history of families. The genetics tells us that the Kayastha community of Bengal is genetically heterogeneous — descended from diverse ancestral populations that merged over thousands of years. It cannot tell us whether **this specific Dey family** of Mymensingh descends from a Deva dynasty administrator, from a Mughal *qanungo*, or from a more recent migrant to Mymensingh. Those questions require documentary genealogy — the ghatak records, the kulagranthas, the zamindari papers — that may or may not survive in Bangladesh archives.

## Part VII — After Aswini Kumar: The Partition Question and the Diaspora

### 7.1 The 1947 Rupture

The most dramatic event in the recent history of all Hindu families from what is now Bangladesh is the **Partition of British India on 14–15 August 1947**, which created the Muslim-majority nation of East Pakistan out of the eastern portion of Bengal Province. For Hindu families in Mymensingh — which became part of East Pakistan — this was an existential crisis.

The 1941 census had recorded Mymensingh as approximately 20% Hindu. By the 1981 Bangladesh census, the Hindu proportion had fallen to **4.93%**. By 2022, it was **3.43%**. This catastrophic demographic collapse reflects successive waves of displacement:

- **1947 Partition**: The initial migration of Hindu families to India (West Bengal, Assam, Tripura) following Partition and communal violence.
- **1950 East Bengal riots**: Severe anti-Hindu violence in East Pakistan in February 1950 drove another major wave of refugees westward.
- **1964 riots**: Anti-Hindu violence following a communal incident in Kashmir triggered further displacement from East Pakistan.
- **1971 Liberation War**: The Pakistan Army's genocidal campaign against Bengali intellectuals, Hindus, and nationalists created the largest single refugee wave — approximately 10 million refugees, of whom a disproportionate number were Hindu.
- **Post-Liberation pressures**: Continued discrimination, land seizures under the Vested Property Act (Enemy Property Act), and economic marginalisation drove further departure through the 1970s–2000s.

### 7.2 The Almost Certain Departure of the Mymensingh Deys

Given this history, the probability that Aswini Kumar De's family remained in Mymensingh through 1950 is very low. Several trajectories are possible:

**Trajectory 1 (most likely): Partition migration, 1947–1950**
Aswini Kumar De would have been in his sixties at Partition, having established his business c. 1912. His sons (Robin's likely father or uncles) would have been adults. Faced with the prospect of living as a religious minority in a new Muslim-majority state, with the umbrella business potentially endangered by communal violence or economic disruption, the family almost certainly migrated to West Bengal — most probably to **Calcutta** (Kolkata), where vast numbers of East Bengal Hindu refugees settled, or possibly to other cities (Asansol, Howrah, Delhi) with established Bengali communities.

**Trajectory 2: Stayed through 1950, then left**
Some Hindu families initially stayed in East Pakistan, hoping conditions would stabilise. The 1950 riots (the Noakhali-type violence that spread across East Bengal in January–February 1950) would have ended that hope for most. By 1951, the great majority of Hindu professional and commercial families from eastern Bengal district towns had relocated to India.

**Trajectory 3 (less likely): Stayed through 1971**
A few Hindu families, particularly those with deep local roots and relatively good relationships with their Muslim neighbours, stayed through the 1950s and 1960s. The 1971 Liberation War was a second catastrophic rupture for many of these families. Very few Hindu families from the original district-town upper-caste class remained by the late 1970s.

### 7.3 The Refugee Experience and Its Genealogical Consequences

The displacement of Hindu Bengali families from East Pakistan to India had profound genealogical consequences that make tracing these families more difficult:

1. **Loss of documents**: In the chaos of sudden migration, birth records, land deeds, marriage certificates, and kulagrantha entries were often left behind, destroyed, or lost.
2. **Naming convention changes**: Some families simplified or changed their names and spellings during resettlement (De → Dey, or vice versa; addition of "Kumar" as a middle name for men, "Rani" or "Devi" for women, became more common in the resettlement context).
3. **Network disruption**: The *ghatak* matchmaker networks that had maintained Bangaja Kayastha genealogical records were shattered. Families from the same *samaj* in Mymensingh might end up in different cities of West Bengal, losing touch with the genealogical tradition.
4. **Economic disruption**: The umbrella business in Mymensingh — Aswini Kumar De's life's work — would almost certainly have been left behind or sold at distress prices. Starting again in Calcutta meant reinvention, often in entirely different occupations.

## Part IX — Synthesis: Who Were the Mymensingh Deys?

### 9.1 The Integrated Picture

Assembling all the evidence — genealogical, historical, sociological, genetic — the following integrated picture emerges:

**Genetic ancestry (60,000+ years)**: The Mymensingh Dey family carries the characteristic four-component ancestry of upper-caste Bengali Hindus: ~48–55% ANI (Ancestral North Indian, including IVC-related and steppe components), ~33–40% ASI (Ancestral South Indian / AASI), ~7–12% East Asian (Austro-Asiatic/Munda-related). This places them genetically in the middle of the Bengal spectrum — more ANI-shifted than the tribal Santhal or the lower castes, less ANI-shifted than North Indian Brahmins, with a uniquely Bengali East Asian signature. Their paternal haplogroup is most likely R1a (~35–45% probability) or H (~15–25%), with J2, R2, and O2a also possible.

**Administrative ancestry (c. 400–1200 CE)**: As Bengali Kayasthas, the family descends from the scribal-administrative tradition that began with Gupta-period *Prathama-Kayasthas* (4th–6th centuries CE) and continued through the Pala Buddhist administration (750–1174 CE). In the Pala period, Bengal was the intellectual heart of Buddhist Asia, and Kayastha scribes served in a court that patronized Nalanda and Vikramashila universities. The family's Kayastha DNA — in the social sense — runs deep: literacy, administration, and service to whichever political authority prevailed.

**Dynastic ancestry (c. 1231–1294 CE)**: The Dey/De surname almost certainly crystallised in the context of the Vaishnava Deva dynasty of Bikrampur — either from direct administrative service at the Deva court, or from the adoption of *-deva* as a personal honorific that later became heritable. The social disruption of the Islamic conquest (post-1294 CE) froze these names into permanent hereditary markers.

**Eastern Bengal positioning (c. 1280 CE onwards)**: Within the Bangaja Kayastha framework established by Danujmardan Deb of Chandradwip, the Dey family occupied the Mahapatra tier — a position of substantial dignity, placing them in the second rank of a nineteen-family Mahapatra group, behind only the Sen family. This framework would have governed their marriage alliances, social standing, and community belonging for centuries.

**Frontier settlement (c. 16th–18th century)**: The family arrived in Mymensingh district during the Bengal Sultanate or Mughal period — most likely between c. 1500 and 1700 CE — as part of the wave of Kayastha families that colonized the district's administrative and revenue positions. Mymensingh's second-highest Kayastha concentration (105,537 in 1891) reflects this sustained settlement over several generations.

**Commercial transition (c. 1800–1912)**: Through the Permanent Settlement era and the colonial modernisation of the 19th century, the family transitioned from whatever revenue-administrative position they had occupied under Mughal rule into the commercial sphere of Mymensingh town. The railway connection to Dhaka and Calcutta (1884) opened new trade possibilities. By approximately 1912, Aswini Kumar De had established an umbrella manufacturing business in Burra Bazar.

**Peak commercial success (c. 1912–1937)**: Over 25 years, Aswini Kumar built a brand — "Aswini Chhati" — that became a household name in Mymensingh's umbrella market. He registered a trademark, manufactured three product lines, and commanded sufficient market recognition that competitors deliberately counterfeited his brand. He litigated his trademark rights through the Sessions Court to the Calcutta High Court. He was, by any measure, a successful Bengali entrepreneur of his era.

**Displacement and diaspora (post-1947)**: The family almost certainly migrated from Mymensingh to India at or after Partition in 1947, joining the approximately 3–4 million Hindus who fled East Bengal in the years 1947–1952. The umbrella business that Aswini Kumar had built over three decades was almost certainly abandoned or sold.

### 9.2 A Note on the Name

Aswini Kumar De signed his name as **"Aswini Kumar De"** on his trademark — the formal, written Bengali spelling. The court case is titled **"Aswini Kumar Dey"** — the anglicised colloquial form. Robin Dey bears the surname **Dey**. All three are the same name, in different orthographic registers: the Sanskrit *Deva*, contracted to *Deb*, softened to *De*, and colloquially extended to *Dey*.

This name carries seven centuries of Bengali history. It is the name of the Vaishnava kings of Bikrampur; the name of the Chandradwip kingdom that organised the social world of eastern Bengali Kayasthas; the name of administrators who served under Pala Buddhist kings, Hindu Sena kings, Muslim sultans, Mughal emperors, and East India Company collectors; and — most recently — the name of an umbrella manufacturer in Mymensingh Burra Bazar whose brand was so recognizable that his competitors spent years trying to steal it.

## Appendix C — The Umbrella Trade in Context

### Why Umbrellas?

The umbrella (*chhati/chhata/chhatri*) holds a special place in Bengali culture — partly practical (Bengal's 2,200mm annual rainfall makes umbrellas a necessity for approximately five months of the year), partly symbolic (the royal *chatri* or canopy was a symbol of authority since ancient times; even today, large ceremonial umbrellas feature in Hindu religious processions).

Bengal's heavy monsoon made umbrella manufacturing a **high-demand seasonal business** with a predictable annual market cycle. The months of Chaitra–Baisakh (March–May) were the selling season, before the monsoon broke. A successful umbrella manufacturer needed to produce inventory well in advance and distribute through retail channels across the district — no small logistical feat in a district the size of original Mymensingh.

The competitive umbrella market in Mymensingh — with at least five identifiable manufacturers (Aswini Kumar De, Loke Nath Sen, Butta Kristo Paul & Sons, Ashutosh Paul, Nagendra Nath Ghatak) — suggests a well-developed local industry. The fact that multiple manufacturers used the "swan + 84" design combination indicates either shared iconographic convention or competitive imitation from an earlier period. A study of Bengal's cottage umbrella industry in the colonial period would be a worthwhile parallel research project.

### The Materials and Methods

Umbrellas in 1920s–1930s Mymensingh were almost certainly:
- **Frame**: Bamboo and/or early steel ribs (steel-frame umbrellas became available in Bengal from the late 19th century through import trade)
- **Cover**: Cotton or light silk fabric, often treated for water resistance
- **Handle**: Wood, often bamboo

The manufacturing process for a small enterprise like Aswini Kumar's would have involved:
- Procurement of frame components (bamboo or steel ribs, handles)
- Fabric cutting and assembly
- Branding/printing of the inside design (the elaborate swan trademark was printed on the inside of the fabric canopy)
- Quality control and packing

The printing of the trademark design — with its complex text, heraldic swan, and registry number — required either a press in Mymensingh town or outsourcing to a Calcutta printer. The sophistication of the design (as described in the court record) suggests Aswini Kumar invested meaningfully in his brand presentation.
