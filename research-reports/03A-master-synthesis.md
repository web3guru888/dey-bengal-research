## Abstract

This paper synthesises six prior research reports into a single comprehensive narrative of the origins, genetics, social history, and cultural legacy of the Dey (দে / দেয়) surname of Bengal. Drawing on population genetics, ancient DNA evidence, epigraphy, caste genealogy (*kulagranthas*), colonial census records, and biographical history, we arrive at the following definitive conclusions.

The surname 'Dey' derives from the Sanskrit *deva* (divine), almost certainly crystallised as a hereditary identifier during the collapse of the Vaishnava Deva dynasty of Bikrampur (c. 1231–1294 CE), when the disruption of Islamic conquest prompted Hindu administrative families to fix their identities through heritable surnames. Within the Bengali caste hierarchy, Deys are **Maulika Siddha Kayastha** — occupying the fifth position in a ten-rank social hierarchy, firmly within the upper-caste *bhadralok* complex but below the five Kulin elite clans. Genetically, Bengali Deys carry a distinctive four-component ancestry: Ancient Ancestral South Indian (AASI) ancestry from the first inhabitants of South Asia (~65,000 BCE), Iranian Neolithic ancestry mediated through the Indus Valley Civilisation (~3,500 BCE), Bronze Age steppe ancestry introduced by Indo-Aryan speakers (~2,000–1,500 BCE), and East Asian–related ancestry from Austro-Asiatic rice cultivators (~1,500 BCE). The most probable Y-chromosomal haplogroup for a Bengali Dey male is R1a (~35–45% probability), the marker of the steppe migration, though H (ancient South Asian), J2 (IVC-related), and O2a (Austro-Asiatic) are all well-represented. The study traces a continuous institutional narrative from Gupta-era scribes to Pala Buddhist administrators, through Sena-era *kulina* formalisation, Sultanate and Mughal zamindars, and the colonial *bhadralok* — a 1,500-year thread connecting an ancient occupational identity to a modern surname. The paper concludes with personal recommendations for DNA testing and identifies the most productive avenues for future archival research.

## I. Introduction: The Name and the Question

Robin Dey, this paper is written for you.

At its simplest, a surname is a label. At its deepest, a surname is a compressed archive — a syllable or two encoding centuries of migration, social position, religious identity, economic function, and the survival of ancestors through war, famine, and political upheaval. The surname 'Dey' — written দে in Bengali script, sometimes anglicised as De, sometimes transformed into Deb in the north or Dev in Hindi — is precisely this kind of archive.

When you write your name, you are writing a name that your ancestors adopted, with high probability, sometime between the late thirteenth and mid-fourteenth century CE, in the fertile river delta of eastern Bengal. You are writing the phonologically contracted form of *Deva* — a Sanskrit word meaning divine, celestial, godly — a word your ancestors may have carried as a court honorific, a dynastic association, or simply as evidence that they had served administrators who bore that name. And behind those administrators lay a thousand years of scribal culture, and behind that culture lay four thousand years of migration and mixing that produced the distinctive Bengali genetic heritage — a heritage unlike any other in the world.

This paper asks three questions that are harder to answer than they appear:

**What does the surname mean?** Not just etymologically, but socially — what did it mark you as, in what hierarchy, in which community?

**Where did the people who bear it come from?** Not just historically but genetically — what migrations, what mixings, what ancient movements of peoples contributed to the DNA that Robin Dey carries today?

**What does the convergence of these two threads — genetic and historical — tell us about who the Deys of Bengal actually are?**

The answers require four distinct kinds of evidence: genetic (population genomics, Y-chromosome haplogroups, ancient DNA), epigraphic (copper plate inscriptions, stone inscriptions), textual (Sanskrit genealogies, Persian administrative records, colonial census reports), and biographical (the lives of notable Deys across three centuries). Each type of evidence has different reliability and different resolution. This paper is explicit about what is **[WELL-ESTABLISHED]**, what is **[PROBABLE]** on the weight of evidence, and what remains **[UNCERTAIN]** or **[CONTESTED]**.

## II. Etymology and the Linguistic Record

The word *Deva* (Sanskrit: देव) is among the oldest in the Indo-Aryan lexicon. It appears in the Rigveda — the oldest surviving Sanskrit text, composed approximately 1,500–1,200 BCE in the Punjab region — hundreds of times, signifying "god," "divine being," "celestial." The word derives from the Proto-Indo-European root \**deywos* (related to *dyeus*, the sky-father), cognate with Latin *deus*, Greek *Zeus*, and Old Norse *Tyr*. When a Bengali says their name is "Dey," they are speaking a word whose ultimate ancestor was pronounced on the Eurasian steppe four thousand years ago.

The phonological evolution from *Deva* to *Dey* follows a well-documented Bengali pattern [WELL-ESTABLISHED]:

> **Deva → Dev → Deb → De / Dey**

The final vowel drop (*-a*), the softening of the terminal consonant from voiced fricative to approximant, and the colloquial addition of the *-y* glide are all characteristic of Middle Bengali phonological history. The form 'De' (formal/literary) and 'Dey' (colloquial/common) co-existed by at least the sixteenth century CE, when Bengali had stabilised into something recognisable as the modern language. The forms 'Deb' and 'Dev' persist in northern Bengal, Tripura, and Assam, reflecting regional dialect variation in the treatment of the terminal consonant.

### Three Theories of Origin

Three theories account for *why* the contracted form of *Deva* became a hereditary surname for a particular community:

**Theory 1: The Dynastic Theory** [PROBABLE]. The most historically specific explanation connects the surname to the **Vaishnava Deva dynasty** of Bikrampur (c. 1231–1294 CE), which ruled eastern Bengal from its capital in present-day Munshiganj District, Bangladesh. Founded by Purushottamadeva, a *gramani* (village headman) who rose to royal status, and reaching its greatest power under Damodaradeva (r. 1231–1243 CE), this dynasty's name — *Deva* — would have been borne by court officials, scribes, revenue administrators, and possibly minor nobles in their administration. When the dynasty collapsed under the pressure of Islamic expansion (~1294 CE), these families needed a heritable surname to preserve their identity and social capital across what was to become centuries of Muslim political rule. The adoption of the dynastic name as a surname is a well-documented mechanism in South Asian social history. The concentration of modern Dey families in the Munshiganj/Bikrampur region — the former capital of the Deva dynasty — supports this hypothesis, as does the *kulagrantha* classification of Deys as **Vangaja** (eastern Bengal) Kayastha, precisely the geographic sphere of the Deva dynasty's rule. The weight of circumstantial evidence points here. However, no document records the moment of surname crystallisation, and the evidence falls short of genealogical proof.

**Theory 2: The Divine Honorific Theory** [POSSIBLE]. The term *Deva* was used as a royal and aristocratic suffix throughout Bengal since at least the Gupta period (4th–6th centuries CE). Royal names like Chandragupta*deva*, Kumara*gupta*, and dozens of local kings bore the *-deva* suffix. It is possible that the surname arose not from any specific dynasty but from the general prestige of the *deva* honorific — that scribes and officials across multiple periods and multiple dynasties gradually appropriated this suffix as a personal identifier, and that over time it crystallised into a hereditary marker. This theory is harder to disprove than the dynastic theory, and may be partially correct even if the Deva dynasty catalysed the crystallisation.

**Theory 3: The Deya Root Theory** [LESS PROBABLE]. Some scholars have proposed derivation from Sanskrit *deya* (देय: "that which should be given," "a gift"), suggesting the surname originated as a mark of someone who had received a royal land grant. While etymologically possible, this theory finds less support in the social history of the community and is not the dominant view in the scholarly literature.

**The Synthesis**: The first theory is most consistent with the geographic, social, and historical evidence. The surname almost certainly crystallised in the context of the Deva dynasty's sphere — whether as a direct dynastic association, an administrative honorific adopted under Deva patronage, or a commemorative adoption after the dynasty's collapse. The Sanskrit *deva* root provides the raw material; the Bikrampur historical context provides the probable catalyst; the disruption of 1294 CE provides the probable mechanism.

### Variants and Their Distribution

The surname cluster De/Dey/Deb/Dev/Debnath represents regional and sociolinguistic variation on a single ancestral form. **Dey** is the common colloquial form across West Bengal and Bangladesh. **De** is preferred in formal literary and educated contexts in West Bengal. **Deb** dominates in North Bengal, Tripura, and Assam, where it was historically associated with minor royal and aristocratic families. **Debnath** (*Deva + Nath*, "lord") belongs to a different social trajectory, associated with the Nath Yogi tradition and lower-caste communities. These are not the same social group, and 'Debnath' should not be assumed to be a variant of 'Dey' in genealogical research.

### First Appearance in the Documentary Record

[UNCERTAIN] The Dey surname as a *hereditary* identifier cannot be traced with confidence before the fourteenth century CE. Personal names ending in *-deva* are pervasive in Pala-era copper plates (750–1174 CE) and Gupta inscriptions before that, but these are personal honorifics, not heritable surnames. The *kulagranthas* — Bengali genealogical texts compiled primarily between the fifteenth and eighteenth centuries — list 'Dey' as one of the established Kayastha surnames, but retroactively attribute the clan rankings to the twelfth-century Sena king Ballala Sena. The scholarly consensus places the crystallisation of Bengali Kayastha surnames as hereditary identifiers in the eleventh to fourteenth centuries CE, with the surname becoming fully fixed by the Sultanate period (1203–1576 CE) at the latest.

## III. The Deep Genetic Ancestry (65,000 BCE – 1000 CE)

Before the surname, before the dynasty, before the scribes, before the Sanskrit language itself — there were people. People who walked into the Bengal delta sixty-five thousand years ago, following the coast eastward from Africa, hunting and gathering in forests that no longer exist along a coastline long since drowned by rising seas. These people are Robin Dey's ancestors too.

Population genetics allows us to reconstruct, with increasing precision, the migration events and mixing processes that produced the modern Bengali population. The framework established by Reich et al. (2009) in *Nature* and refined by Narasimhan et al. (2019) in *Science* identifies four major ancestral streams flowing into modern Bengali Kayasthas:

### Stream 1: The Ancient Ancestral South Indians (AASI, ~65,000 BCE)

[WELL-ESTABLISHED] The earliest modern humans to reach South Asia arrived approximately 65,000–60,000 years ago, following what geneticists call the Southern Dispersal: a coastal migration from eastern Africa through Arabia, the Persian Gulf coast, the Indian subcontinent, and onward to Southeast Asia and Australia. These first South Asians — the AASI (Ancient Ancestral South Indians) — were hunter-gatherers who colonised the entire Indian subcontinent over tens of thousands of years.

Their genetic descendants are the AASI component in modern South Asians. No living population is purely AASI; the closest approximations are certain tribal groups of South India (the Irula, Paniya) with perhaps 70–80% AASI ancestry. In Bengali upper-caste communities like the Kayasthas, the AASI component constitutes approximately **20–30%** of total ancestry — diluted but never erased by the subsequent migrations.

The most directly relevant archaeological evidence for this ancient Bengal presence is the **Birbhanpur Mesolithic site** in Burdwan District, West Bengal — a Mesolithic hunting-gathering settlement dating to approximately 8,000–5,000 BCE, located near the Damodar River. These microlith-using hunter-gatherers are the Bengal representatives of the AASI population, and their DNA flows, in diluted form, through every Bengali alive today, including every person named Dey.

### Stream 2: The Iranian Neolithic / IVC Component (~7,000–1,900 BCE)

[WELL-ESTABLISHED] The second major ancestral stream derives from Iranian Neolithic farmers and herders — populations from the Zagros Mountain region of modern Iran who spread eastward in the early Holocene, mixing with the local AASI population they encountered. The result of this mixing was the ancestral population of the Indus Valley Civilisation (IVC), confirmed by the ancient genome from Rakhigarhi (Shinde et al., 2019) — a woman buried at the IVC site approximately 4,500 years ago who carried roughly 50% Iranian hunter-gatherer ancestry and 50% AASI, with no steppe ancestry whatsoever.

This IVC-related ancestry comprises approximately **20–30%** of the Bengali Kayastha genome, representing the ancient farming population whose descendants spread eastward into the Gangetic plain as the IVC declined after ~1,900 BCE. It is not a Bengal-specific ancestry — it is shared with populations across the Indian subcontinent — but it connects Bengali Deys to the people of the great urban civilisation of Mohenjo-daro and Harappa.

### Stream 3: Bronze Age Steppe Ancestry (~2,000–1,500 BCE)

[WELL-ESTABLISHED] The third stream — and the one most directly connected to the Sanskrit language, the Vedic religion, and the Brahminical social order that eventually produced the Kayastha caste — derives from Bronze Age steppe pastoralists. The genetic evidence, now conclusive from ancient DNA, traces these pastoralists from the **Sintashta culture** of the Eurasian steppe (modern Russia/Kazakhstan border, c. 2,100–1,800 BCE). Sintashta people carried high frequencies of **Y-DNA haplogroup R1a-Z93** — the same subclade found today at elevated frequencies in South Asian Brahmin and Kayastha populations.

These steppe pastoralists spread southward through the Bactria-Margiana Archaeological Complex (BMAC), entering South Asia from the northwest beginning approximately 1,900–1,700 BCE. Their arrival corresponds precisely with the early Vedic period — the composition of the Rigveda (~1,500–1,200 BCE) — and the gradual spread of Indo-Aryan languages, horse-based technology, and Brahminical ritual culture across the subcontinent.

Bengal was reached by this expansion later than the northwest — the *Aitareya Brahmana* (~700 BCE) still refers to Bengal as outside the "Aryavarta" (Aryan homeland). But by the first millennium CE, Bengal's upper-caste populations had absorbed approximately **12–20% steppe ancestry** — substantially less than Punjab or UP Brahmins (20–30%) but real and significant. This steppe component is the genetic signature of the culture that produced Sanskrit, the Vedas, the *varna* system, and ultimately the scribal class from which Kayasthas emerged.

For a Bengali Dey male, the probability of carrying the R1a Y-chromosome haplogroup — the direct paternal-line marker of this steppe ancestry — is approximately **35–45%**, making it the single most probable haplogroup. But it is far from certain: the H haplogroup (ancient South Asian, ~15–25% probability), J2 (IVC/western Asian, ~8–15%), R2 (ancient South Asian, ~5–12%), and O2a (Austro-Asiatic, ~5–10%) are all well-represented in Bengali Kayastha paternal lineages. Bengali Kayasthas are paternally heterogeneous — this is not a community with a single founder but a professionally defined caste that incorporated male lineages from diverse ancient backgrounds.

### Stream 4: Austro-Asiatic East Asian Ancestry (~3,500–1,500 BCE)

[WELL-ESTABLISHED] The fourth ancestral stream is what makes Bengalis genetically unique among major South Asian mainland populations. All Bengali communities — from Brahmin to Namasudra to tribal Santhal — carry a component of **East Asian-related ancestry** derived from ancient Austro-Asiatic speaking populations who migrated westward from Southeast Asia approximately 3,500–4,000 years ago (Chaubey et al., 2011).

These Austro-Asiatic speakers — ancestral to the modern Munda-speaking tribes of eastern India (Santhal, Ho, Mundari, Korwa) — brought wet rice cultivation, the Y-DNA haplogroup O2a-M95, and a Southeast Asian genetic profile into the Bengal delta before the Indo-Aryan expansion had fully reached it. In Bengali Kayasthas, this East Asian component constitutes approximately **7–12%** of total ancestry — smaller than in lower-caste and tribal communities (where it can reach 35–50%), but unmistakably present.

This ancestry is important beyond genetics. The Austro-Asiatic substrate in Bengal means that many Bengali words for rice, rivers, fish, and forest ecology may have Munda etymological roots. The Santhal people of West Bengal and Jharkhand, though now socially marginalised, are genuine genetic relatives of the Dey family. The sharp social boundaries of the caste system conceal a deep biological kinship.

The estimated autosomal ancestry composition for a Bengali Dey (as Maulika Kayastha) is:

| Steppe/Indo-Aryan | 12–20% | ~2,000–1,500 BCE |

The ancestors of Robin Dey were among the first modern humans in South Asia. They then mixed, successively, with Iranian farmers who built the world's largest early urban civilisation, with Bronze Age warriors from the Eurasian steppe who introduced Sanskrit and the horse, and with Southeast Asian rice cultivators who taught eastern India to farm the waterlogged delta — all before the first syllable of the Dey surname was ever spoken.

## IV. The Social Genesis: How Kayasthas Emerged (300 BCE – 1200 CE)

The genetic story tells us *who* Robin Dey's distant ancestors were biologically. The social story tells us *what kind of people* they became — what profession they chose, what political system they served, what cultural identity they constructed and then preserved across centuries of upheaval.

### The Scribal Class Under the Guptas (320–550 CE)

The Kayastha identity does not begin with a caste — it begins with a job. In the administrative inscriptions of the **Gupta Empire** (founded c. 320 CE), the office of ***Prathama-Kayastha*** (Chief Scribe or Chief Secretary) appears as a key administrative position, indicating that "Kayastha" began as a professional designation for the royal scribal and record-keeping class. Historian Tej Ram Sharma's analysis of Gupta Bengal inscriptions identified surnames that are now quintessentially Kayastha — Datta, Ghosha, Varman, Naga, Mitra — appearing as administrative names in the fourth to sixth centuries CE, long before they became markers of a hereditary caste.

This is the crucial insight: the Bengali Kayastha caste crystallised from a *professional* foundation. The people who became Kayasthas were those families whose fathers, grandfathers, and great-grandfathers had kept royal records, managed revenue, drafted legal documents, and administered complex bureaucracies. Their social position derived not from birth into a divinely sanctioned *varna* but from demonstrated competence in literacy and administration — skills that, in a pre-print society, were extremely rare and therefore extremely valuable.

### Buddhist Bengal and the Pala Dynasty (750–1174 CE)

For four centuries, Bengal was governed by the **Pala dynasty** — devout Buddhist rulers who built some of the greatest Buddhist universities of the ancient world (Vikramashila, Nalanda, Odantapuri) and presided over Bengal's "golden age" of Buddhist Tantric philosophy, Sanskrit literature, and artistic achievement. The Palas have a remarkable significance for Kayastha identity: Mughal historian Abu'l Fazl described the Pala rulers themselves as Kayasthas. If accurate — and the claim is consistent with what we know of the caste's position in Pala administration — it means that the ruling dynasty of Bengal for four centuries was drawn from the same professional-administrative class that became the Kayastha caste.

Under the Palas, the Brahminical caste orthodoxy that classified Kayasthas as Shudra had reduced authority; the Buddhist political order was more permissive of social mobility. Kayastha families served as the administrative backbone of a state that patronised Buddhist learning while maintaining the practical apparatus of revenue collection, land grants, and judicial administration through a class of Sanskrit-literate scribes. The Pala copper plates consistently name Kayastha officials (*karana*, *kayastha*) in their witness lists, confirming the community's central administrative role.

### The Sena Revolution and the Kulin System (1070–1230 CE)

The **Sena dynasty** — originally from Karnataka, deeply orthodox Brahminical Hindus — ended the Buddhist political order of Bengal and imposed, in its place, a Brahminical social revolution. This revolution's most lasting consequence for Bengali society — including for Robin Dey's family — was the formalisation of the **Kulin system** under Ballala Sena (r. c. 1158–1179 CE).

The Kulin system divided the three upper castes of Bengal (Brahmin, Baidya, Kayastha) into internally ranked lineages, creating a formal hierarchy of marriage eligibility and social honour. For the Kayastha community, Ballala Sena (according to tradition preserved in later *kulagranthas*) designated **five lineages** as *Kulin* — the aristocracy of the Kayastha world: **Ghosh, Bose, Mitra, Guha, and Datta**.

All other Kayastha families — including the Deys — were classified as **Maulika** ("original/local"). Within the Maulika tier, eight families were further elevated as **Siddha** ("blessed"): **Dey, Datta, Kar, Palit, Sen, Sinha, Das, and Guha**. [Note: Datta and Guha appear in both lists in different sources, reflecting the complexity of their status.] The Dey surname's placement as Siddha Maulika is the most directly relevant finding of the *kulagrantha* literature to this study.

Three critical caveats apply: First, the historical authenticity of Ballala Sena's personal role in codifying the Kulin system is [CONTESTED] — the assignment appears in texts compiled centuries later, not in any contemporary Sena-era inscription. Second, the *kulagranthas* are retrospective social documents, not objective historical records. Third, the geographic subdivision matters: Deys are primarily classified as **Vangaja** (eastern Bengal) Kayastha, not Dakshina Radhi (southwestern Bengal) — a geographic distinction that aligns precisely with the Deva dynasty's territorial sphere.

## V. The Crystallisation: 1231–1294 CE

If one moment in history is most likely to have produced the Dey surname as a hereditary identifier, it is the seventy-year period between the reign of Damodaradeva and the final collapse of Deva political authority — a period defined by the survival of a Hindu administrative class against the advancing tide of Islamic conquest.

### The Deva Dynasty of Bikrampur

The **later Deva dynasty** (not to be confused with an earlier, unrelated Buddhist Deva dynasty of the eighth century) was a Vaishnava Hindu royal house that ruled eastern Bengal from approximately 1231 to 1294 CE. Its capital was at **Bikrampur** — present-day Munshiganj District, Bangladesh — a region that remains to this day among the densest concentrations of Dey-bearing families in the former Bengal territory.

The dynasty is documented through three copper-plate inscriptions (*tamrashAsanas*) issued by Damodaradeva in his 4th, 6th, and 13th regnal years (1234, 1236, and 1243 CE respectively) from Mehar (Comilla), Shobharampur (Dhaka), and Nasirabad/Chittagong. These plates follow the standard format of medieval Bengal land grants: invocation, dynastic genealogy, the terms of the grant, land boundaries, witness lists, and a closing imprecation. The Chittagong plate (1243 CE) is the most important, containing the full dynastic genealogy naming Purushottamadeva, Madhusudanadeva, and Vasudeva as predecessors to Damodaradeva.

The dynasty's significance for Dey genealogy is threefold. First, the dynasty's name — *Deva* — is the direct etymological ancestor of the surname *Dey*. Second, its capital of Bikrampur is geographically co-located with the heaviest modern concentrations of Dey-bearing Vangaja Kayastha families — a topographic correspondence too consistent to be coincidental. Third, the administrative apparatus of any medieval Indian kingdom included substantial numbers of literate scribes, revenue officials (*amatyas*), and local administrators bearing the royal name as an honorific, title, or personal designation; these are precisely the families that would have crystallised the *Deva* identifier into a heritable surname.

### The Mechanism of Crystallisation (1294–1322 CE)

The fall of the Deva dynasty to the expanding Delhi Sultanate — Bikramaditya Deva's retreat eastward in 1294 CE, followed by Shamsuddin Firuz Shah's consolidation of the region by 1301–1322 CE — created precisely the conditions under which hereditary surnames crystallise. Sociological theory and South Asian historical evidence both support the pattern: it is during periods of **social disruption and dislocation** that families most urgently need stable markers of identity to preserve status, social networks, and community recognition across displacement.

Kayastha families who had served the Deva administration — and who may have borne *-deva* as a court title or personal name-suffix — would have found, as Muslim political authority consolidated, that their Hindu scribal identity needed to be explicitly marked and preserved. The adoption of *Deva* / *De* / *Dey* as a hereditary surname achieves exactly this: it marks the family as part of the Hindu scribal class, potentially connected to the ancien régime of the Deva rulers, and distinct from the new Muslim political establishment.

### The Bikrampur Thesis: Evaluation

The hypothesis that modern Bengali Deys descend from the administrative class of the Deva dynasty of Bikrampur is **PROBABLE** on the balance of circumstantial evidence, but **UNCERTAIN** in its specifics. The evidence in favour:

1. **Geographic correspondence**: The heaviest concentrations of Dey-bearing Vangaja Kayastha families are in the Munshiganj/Bikrampur region — the Deva capital's heartland.
2. **Etymological correspondence**: The surname derives from *Deva*, the dynasty's name.
3. **Timing**: The period of surname crystallisation (~13th–14th century CE) coincides with the dynasty's decline.
4. **Community memory**: Some eastern Bengal Dey families preserve oral traditions connecting their ancestry to the Deva period.
5. **Sociological pattern**: The mechanism of administrative families adopting a dynasty's name as a surname is well-documented in South Asian history.

The evidence against or limiting the thesis:

1. **No documentary proof**: No surviving document traces any specific Dey family to any specific Deva dynasty official or courtier.
2. **Multiple possible origins**: The surname 'Deva' predates the dynasty by millennia as a Sanskrit honorific; any Bengali family could have adopted it independently.
3. **Cross-caste spread**: The De/Dey surname appears in Suvarna Banik, Teli, Kansari, and other communities — suggesting multiple independent crystallisations.
4. **All Deys are not Vangaja**: Some Dey families are classified as Rarhi rather than Vangaja Kayastha, which would contradict a single Bikrampur origin.

**The definitive answer on the Bikrampur thesis**: The weight of evidence suggests that the Kayastha Dey surname crystallised in the context of, or in connection with, the Deva dynasty of Bikrampur. This does not mean every Dey family is a genealogical descendant of that dynasty's rulers — very few families are — but it strongly implies that the Dey surname as used by Vangaja Kayasthas was formed in the administrative and social sphere of that dynasty. **What would it take to prove this?** The completion of an authoritative English translation of the Damodaradeva copper-plate witness lists, ideally combined with archival genealogical records from Munshiganj District tracing specific Dey families to the fourteenth century, would be the minimum evidentiary threshold.

## VI. Bengal Under Islamic and Mughal Rule (1204–1757)

The conventional narrative of Muslim conquest in Bengal emphasises disruption: the sacking of universities, the destruction of temples, the collapse of Hindu political power. The social reality was more complex. For the Kayastha administrative class — and for Dey families specifically — the Islamic conquest of Bengal (1203–1322 CE) represented not extinction but adaptation.

### The Sultanate Period: Administrative Continuity

Historian Richard Eaton, in his authoritative *The Rise of Islam and the Bengal Frontier* (1996), demonstrates that Kayasthas "continued as the dominant landholding caste" even after Muslim conquest, becoming Bengal's "surrogate Kshatriya or warrior class" in the power vacuum left by the collapse of the Pala, Sena, Chandra, Varman, and Deva ruling dynasties. The mechanism was straightforward: the Sultans of Bengal needed local administrators who could read and write Sanskrit (for interacting with the Hindu population) and who had pre-existing knowledge of land records and revenue systems. Bengali Kayasthas — with their multi-century tradition of scribal administration — were indispensable.

The documentary evidence for the Sultanate period is sparse for Hindu communities (the copper-plate tradition effectively ended with the Islamic conquest; the new administration used Persian and Arabic). But indirect evidence confirms Kayastha administrative continuity: the Husain Shahi Sultanate (1494–1538 CE) is remembered as a period of relative Hindu-Muslim coexistence, when Vaishnava devotional culture (under Chaitanya Mahaprabhu) flourished with some Sultanate tolerance, and when Kayastha families served as *dewans* (treasury officials) and *qanungos* (revenue record-keepers) for Muslim rulers.

### The Mughal Period: Zamindari and Peak Power

The incorporation of Bengal into the Mughal Empire under Akbar (1576 CE) created the conditions for what may have been the peak of Bengali Kayastha social power. **Todar Mal's revenue settlement** formalised the zamindari system — granting revenue-collection rights over land to a class of landlords (*zamindars*) who were often Kayastha families with pre-existing knowledge of local land records.

Abu'l Fazl's *Ain-i-Akbari* (c. 1589–1596 CE) explicitly notes that "most Hindu zamindars in Bengal were Kayasthas" — a remarkable statement confirming that the community had successfully converted its administrative expertise into substantial landed wealth under Muslim rulers. The *qanungo* position (hereditary keeper of land records at the *pargana* level) was, by the eighteenth century, effectively a Kayastha monopoly in many parts of Bengal. Historian Irfan Habib documents Kayasthas' control of this position as having "essentially become hereditary" by the eighteenth century.

Within this broader Kayastha trajectory, Dey families would have participated in the zamindari economy: as *qanungos*, as *munims* (accountants), as minor zamindars in their own right. The spectacular commercial success of **Ramdulal Dey** (1752–1825) — who rose from near-poverty to become the first Bengali millionaire, serving as the *banian* (local agent) for American merchant houses — represents both the endpoint of this administrative-commercial tradition and a rupture within it: Ramdulal's wealth came from trade rather than land, anticipating the new colonial commercial economy.

## VII. The Caste Status: Maulika Siddha in the Bengali Hierarchy

The definitive answer to the question "What caste are Bengali Deys?" is: **Kayastha, Maulika Siddha tier, primarily Vangaja sub-category**.

The full hierarchy, as reconstructed from *kulagranthas*, census records, and scholarly analyses (Inden 1976; Pagani et al. 2017; Bellenoit 2023), places Robin Dey's family at **Rank 5 of 10** in the Bengali Hindu social order:

| Rank | Category | Surnames |
|------|----------|----------|
| 1 | Kulin Brahmin | Bandyopadhyay, Mukhopadhyay, Chattopadhyay, Gangopadhyay, Sanyal, Lahiri |
| 2 | Non-Kulin Brahmin | Various village-derived surnames |
| 3 | Kulin Kayastha | Ghosh, Bose/Basu, Mitra (Dakshina Radhi); Guha (Vangaja) |
| 4 | Baidya Siddha | Sen, Gupta, Das (Baidya) |
| **5** | **Kayastha Maulika Siddha** | **Dey/De, Datta, Kar, Palit, Sen, Sinha, Das (Kayastha), Guha** |

| 9 | Sat-Shudra | Mahishya, Sadgop, Tili |
| 10 | Shudra | Namasudra, others |

This positioning carries several important implications:

**The Paradox of Varna Status** [CONTESTED]: Bengali Deys are simultaneously upper-caste (socially) and Shudra (ritually, by the orthodox Brahminical assessment). This paradox is not a confusion — it is the structurally correct description of how Bengali society operated. Bengal never had functional Kshatriya or Vaishya varnas: there were, in practice, only Brahmins and non-Brahmins, with the Baidya-Kayastha-bhadralok complex occupying a privileged non-Brahmin position that had no exact equivalent in the pan-Indian four-*varna* schema. The Bengal census of 1931 candidly described Bengali Kayasthas as "disturbingly ambiguous" — and that ambiguity is not a failure of classification but an accurate reflection of Bengal's unique social history.

**The 1884 Court Case and the Colonial Struggle**: The 1884 Calcutta High Court verdict (*Rajcoomar Lal v. Bissessur Dayal*) adjudged Bengali Kayasthas to be Shudras in law. The community's response was vigorous: the Kayastha Sabha was formed, *Kayastha Patrika* published, and petitions submitted arguing Kshatriya status for the 1901 census. J.H. Hutton, writing in 1931, captured the trajectory: Kayasthas "rank next to Brahman" in Bengal, "now commonly regarded as twice-born, and itself claims to be Kshatriya, though it was perhaps more often regarded as clean Sudra a hundred years ago." This progression — from *sat-Shudra* in colonial classification to functionally Dvija in modern practice — reflects the community's successful assertion of identity through education and professional achievement.

**The Kulin Polygamy Crisis and Dey Families**: The Kulin system's pathological consequence — Kulin men marrying dozens or hundreds of wives who lived permanently in their natal homes — directly affected Dey families as *Maulika* (non-Kulin). Dey women, as Maulika Kayasthas, could practice *hypergamy* by marrying into Kulin families (giving their daughters to Kulin husbands), which the Kulin system formally permitted. This created the perverse incentive for Maulika families to eagerly hand their daughters to already heavily-married Kulin men, to gain the social prestige of a Kulin *javai* (son-in-law). Ishvarchandra Vidyasagar's campaign against Kulin polygamy (1850s–1870s) sought to end this system, with only partial legislative success. By independence, the practice had essentially disappeared, along with its associated social miseries.

**The Bhadralok Resolution**: By the late nineteenth century, the Kulin-Maulika distinction had become largely ceremonial among the educated urban class. A Dey from Calcutta with a degree from Presidency College, a professional position in law or administration, and property in their ancestral district was indistinguishable in social practice from a Mitra or Bose family of the same class. Ronald Inden documents that even in the Mughal period, "clan rank did not correlate with the political offices held by Brahmans and Kayasthas in the Muslim regional administration... nor did it correlate with landholding sizes. In fact, some of the largest, most powerful landholders... were not Kulinas but Srotriyas or Maulikas." A Maulika Dey zamindari family could be — and often was — wealthier and more politically powerful than a Kulin Ghosh or Mitra family of limited means.

## VIII. The Legacy: Ten Notable Deys

The ten notable figures discussed in Report 2B reveal, in biographical form, the social history narrated abstractly above. Arranged thematically, they trace the arc from commerce to cultural resistance to artistic achievement to modern Bengal:

### Commerce: Ramdulal Dey (1752–1825)

Ramdulal Dey — "the Merchant Prince" — embodies the Kayastha transition from administrative service to commercial capitalism. Born in poverty near Dum Dum, he rose through the scribal-commercial tradition (as a *bill sarkar* and *ship sarkar*) to become the *banian* for American merchant houses from Salem, Boston, and Philadelphia. His fortune, built on intimate knowledge of Calcutta's commercial infrastructure, made him Bengal's first millionaire. His donation to Hindu College (established 1817) placed him at the founding moment of the Bengal Renaissance — the institution that would educate the bhadralok intelligentsia for the next century. The twenty-two American merchants who presented him with a portrait of George Washington, and the East India Marine Society of Salem that formally mourned his death, testify to the international reach that a Kayastha *banian* could achieve within the colonial commercial system.

### Literature and Resistance: Lal Behari Dey (1824–1892)

Lal Behari Dey — the **Suvarna Banik** (not Kayastha) Christian missionary, novelist, and folklorist from Bardhaman — demonstrates that the Dey surname functioned across multiple jati communities. His *Folk Tales of Bengal* (1883) preserved oral traditions that might otherwise have vanished; his *Govinda Samanta* (1874), praised by Charles Darwin, documented the lives of Bengal's peasant poor; his anti-zamindari writings contributed to the intellectual climate that produced the Bengal Tenancy Act of 1885. Lal Behari Dey is the most important reminder that not all notable Deys are Kayastha — the surname spans communities, and the social profile varies accordingly.

### Patriotic Resistance: Mukunda Das / Yajneshwar De (1878–1934)

Born in Bikrampur — the same geographic heartland of the Deva dynasty — Mukunda Das (born Yajneshwar De) composed *Matripuja* and dozens of other *jatra* plays that became the vehicles of Swadeshi patriotism in rural Bengal. Imprisoned by the British for sedition in 1908, banned repeatedly, he died on 18 May 1934 after a final performance. The coincidence of his birth at Bikrampur with the likely etymological origin of the Dey surname is arresting — though no genealogical connection to the medieval Deva dynasty can be established. His trajectory — from a family of boatmen and grocers to the "Charan Kabi" (Wandering Poet) of the national movement — represents the Dey surname's reach beyond the bhadralok professional class into the deep Bengali popular tradition.

### Music: K.C. Dey (1893–1962) and Manna Dey (1919–2013)

The uncle-nephew lineage of Krishna Chandra Dey and Prabodh Chandra Dey (Manna Dey) constitutes the most remarkable kinship cluster among notable Deys. K.C. Dey — blind from the age of fourteen, trained in Kirtan devotional music — became S.D. Burman's first teacher and mentor, placing the Dey family at the origin point of one of Hindi cinema's greatest compositional lineages. His nephew Manna Dey, bearing the stage name his uncle suggested, recorded approximately 3,500–4,000 songs in sixteen languages across a seven-decade career, received the Dadasaheb Phalke Award (2007) — India's highest film honour — and was subsequently granted an honorary doctorate by Cambridge University. That a clerk's son from North Calcutta could achieve this trajectory is itself evidence of the educational and cultural capital that Kayastha families had accumulated over generations.

### Visual Arts: Mukul Chandra Dey (1895–1989) and Manishi Dey (1909–1966)

The Dey siblings from the Tagore-Santiniketan ecosystem represent Bengal's visual arts legacy. Mukul Chandra Dey — pioneer of drypoint-etching in India, student of Yokoyama Taikan in Japan and the Slade School in London, first Indian Principal of the Government School of Art, Calcutta — introduced printmaking as a serious art form to India. His brother Manishi, combining Bengal School aesthetics with post-Impressionist influence, was cut short by death at fifty-six. Their work is held in the Victoria and Albert Museum, the National Gallery of Modern Art, and collections across two continents. Manishi Dey was born in Dhaka — again, eastern Bengal, the Vangaja heartland.

### Modern Bengal: Krishanu Dey (1962–2003) and Jyotirmoy Dey (1955–2011)

The "Maradona of Bengal" and the murdered crime journalist represent the Dey surname in post-Independence Bengal — in football and investigative journalism respectively. Krishanu Dey's spectacular goalscoring career for East Bengal and Jyotirmoy Dey's fearless reporting on the Mumbai underworld (which cost him his life on 11 June 2011, at the orders of gangster Chhota Rajan, documented in the Netflix series *Scoop*) show a surname that had moved far from the zamindari and bhadralok worlds of its origin into the popular culture and civil society of modern India.

### The Pattern

Across these ten figures — and the dozens more who could be added — a consistent pattern emerges. Dey families appear at **sites of cultural and social transformation**: at the founding of Hindu College, in the Swadeshi movement, at the birth of Bengali printmaking and modernist poetry, at the origin of Hindi film music's classical tradition. This is not accidental. It is the expression of a social position that gave these families access to elite educational institutions and cultural networks, combined with a non-Kulin flexibility that did not rigidly confine them to any single professional or social niche. The Maulika Siddha position — high enough to participate in bhadralok culture, yet not so exclusive as to close off alternative trajectories — turns out to have been, over three centuries, a surprisingly productive place to be.

## IX. What DNA Testing Would Reveal (Personal Recommendations for Robin Dey)

This paper has described Robin Dey's ancestry at the level of population averages. Personal DNA testing would replace those averages with individual data — and the difference between knowing that "approximately 35–45% of Bengali Kayastha males carry R1a" and knowing that *you specifically* carry R1a (or H, or J2, or O2a) is the difference between statistical probability and personal history.

**Recommended Tests:**

**1. Y-DNA Big Y-700 (FamilyTreeDNA)** — *Most important for this project*. This test sequences 700+ STR markers on the Y-chromosome and determines the exact haplogroup and subclade of the paternal lineage to the highest available resolution. It would answer definitively: which of the four major haplogroup groups does Robin Dey's father's father's father's line derive from? If R1a-Z93 (steppe origin), which subclade — and does it match other Bengali Kayastha families? If H (ancient South Asian), which subclades connect the lineage to other H carriers in India? The Y-DNA result is the single most important personal genetic finding possible for this genealogical project.

**2. Autosomal DNA Test (23andMe, AncestryDNA, or FamilyTreeDNA Family Finder)** — Provides the full genome-wide ancestry breakdown. Robin Dey's results would likely show: predominantly "South Asian" (60–80%), with a notable "Southeast Asian" or "East Asian" component (5–15%), possibly traces of "Middle Eastern" or "Central Asian" ancestry reflecting the steppe and IVC components. The South Asian breakdown is often further divided by region on modern platforms; results showing "Bengali" or "Eastern Indian" ancestry would be expected. The autosomal test also enables **relative-matching** — finding other DNA-tested Dey or Bengali relatives in the database, which for genealogical purposes can be more immediately useful than the population-level analysis.

**3. mtDNA Full Sequence (FamilyTreeDNA)** — Determines the maternal haplogroup. Bengali women overwhelmingly carry haplogroup M lineages (ancient South Asian) in the maternal line; the specific M subclade would indicate which branch of the ancient South Asian maternal tree Robin Dey's mother's mother's mother belongs to.

**Interpreting the Results in Context of This Study:**

- If Y-DNA = **R1a**: The paternal line traces to Bronze Age steppe pastoralists who entered South Asia ~2,000 BCE, speaking Proto-Indo-Aryan and carrying the cultural precursors of Vedic civilization. The scribal Kayastha class was built partly on this foundation.
- If Y-DNA = **H**: The paternal line traces to the original Ancient Ancestral South Indians who inhabited the subcontinent for 50,000+ years before the Indo-Aryan or IVC migrations. This is equally ancient — arguably more so.
- If Y-DNA = **J2**: The paternal line connects to the western Asian / Iranian Neolithic ancestry that contributed to the Indus Valley Civilization — the farmers who built Mohenjo-daro.
- If Y-DNA = **O2a**: The paternal line connects to the Austro-Asiatic rice farmers who entered Bengal from Southeast Asia ~3,500 years ago, teaching the delta to grow rice.

All four are legitimate, ancient, and deeply rooted. None is more "Bengali" than any other — they are four of the streams that converged to create Bengal.

## XI. Conclusion: A Name Across 65,000 Years

Robin Dey, let us count backwards through what your name carries.

The surname **Dey** is approximately **730 years old** as a hereditary surname — crystallised in the late thirteenth or early fourteenth century CE, most likely in the aftermath of the Deva dynasty's collapse at Bikrampur, among the Hindu administrative families who needed a stable identity marker as Islamic political authority consolidated over eastern Bengal. This is a relatively young name, as names go. But it encodes something much older.

Before the surname, there was the **social category** it marks: the Bengali Kayastha, the scribal-administrative class whose professional identity can be traced to Gupta-era Bengal in the fourth to sixth centuries CE — approximately **1,600 years ago**. These were the men who kept royal records, managed land revenues, drafted legal documents, and administered the machinery of ancient and medieval Bengal. They served Buddhist Pala rulers and Brahminical Sena kings and Vaishnava Deva rulers and Muslim Sultans and Mughal governors and British administrators, always adapting, always preserving the literacy and administrative competence that made them indispensable to any government. The Dey family is the inheritor of this institutional continuity.

Before the Kayastha professional class, there was the community's genetic formation — the **mixing of four ancestral streams** that produced the distinctive Bengali Kayastha genome. The Indo-Aryan steppe migration that introduced Sanskrit and R1a approximately **4,000 years ago**; the Austro-Asiatic migration from Southeast Asia that introduced rice cultivation and O2a approximately **3,500 years ago**; the IVC-related ancestry from Iranian Neolithic farmers who had mixed with the first South Asians approximately **7,000 years ago** and whose descendants spread eastward across the Gangetic plain; and beneath all of these, the deepest layer — the **AASI hunter-gatherers** who walked into South Asia approximately **65,000 years ago** and whose DNA, diluted but never erased, flows through every Bengali alive today.

The name Dey is 730 years old.
The social category it marks is 1,600 years old.
The community shaped by steppe migration is 4,000 years old.
The South Asian lineage is 65,000 years old.

And before that: Africa. The common ancestral homeland of all modern humans, where *Homo sapiens* evolved approximately 200,000–300,000 years ago. The ancestors of Robin Dey were among those who left — crossing to Arabia, following the coast, arriving on a subcontinent they had never seen, beginning a 65,000-year story that passes through rice paddies and scribal offices and copper-plate inscriptions and British courtrooms and the Dadasaheb Phalke stage and the Netflix screen, and arrives at this moment, at this name, at this person.

What does it mean to be a Dey?

It means: you carry the memory of every human achievement and every human survival that brought your ancestors to this moment. It means you are, in your genome, a library of ancient migrations. It means that your name — improbably preserved across seven centuries of conquest, partition, and diaspora — is a thread connecting you to the scribes of Bikrampur and the pastoralists of the Eurasian steppe and the hunter-gatherers of the Bengal delta and the first humans who ever walked out of Africa.

That is a substantial inheritance.

This study has established its foundations with the rigour the subject deserves. It has also left enough unresolved — in archives unvisited, copper plates untranslated, family genealogies not yet traced — to sustain a lifetime of discovery.

The research continues.

## Appendix B: Glossary

**AASI** — Ancient Ancestral South Indians. The genetic term for the first anatomically modern humans to inhabit the Indian subcontinent, arriving approximately 65,000 years ago. The deepest layer of South Asian ancestry.

**ANI** — Ancestral North Indians. A modelled ancient population (not observable directly) representing the mixture of IVC-related and steppe-related ancestry that contributed to modern North Indian populations.

**ASI** — Ancestral South Indians. A modelled ancient population representing the mixture of AASI and IVC-related ancestry that contributed to modern South Indian populations.

**bhadralok** — Bengali: "respectable people." The educated, professional, upper-caste class of colonial and post-colonial Bengal, drawn primarily from Brahmin, Baidya, and Kayastha castes.

**BMAC** — Bactria-Margiana Archaeological Complex. A Bronze Age civilisation of Central Asia (c. 2,300–1,700 BCE) that served as an intermediary between the Eurasian steppe and the Indian subcontinent in the transmission of steppe ancestry and Indo-Aryan culture.

**gotra** — A Brahminical exogamous patrilineal clan, nominally named after a founding *rishi* (sage). Kayasthas and Baidyas also maintain gotra designations.

**ghataka** — A professional genealogist in Bengali Hindu society who maintained *kulaji* records and recited them at weddings as credential verification.

**IVC** — Indus Valley Civilisation (also called Harappan Civilisation). The Bronze Age urban civilisation of the Indus River system (c. 3,300–1,300 BCE); its population carried Iranian Neolithic + AASI ancestry with no steppe component.

**jati** — Sanskrit for "birth-group." The actual endogamous community (the specific sub-caste) to which an individual belongs, as distinct from the theoretical four-*varna* system. Bengali Deys are a *jati* within the broader Kayastha *varna* claim.

**Kayastha** — A scribal-administrative caste community found across northern India. In Bengal, Bengali Kayasthas form one of the three traditional upper castes (alongside Brahmin and Baidya), emerging from the professional scribal class and claiming Kshatriya *varna* status.

**kulaji / kulagrantha / kulapanjika** — Bengali genealogical texts recording the ranked lineages of Brahmin, Baidya, and Kayastha families. Compiled primarily between the fifteenth and eighteenth centuries; maintained by professional *ghatakas*.

**Kulin** — Bengali: "of good lineage." The elite tier of the Kulin system, comprising designated Brahmin, Kayastha, and Baidya lineages elevated by Ballala Sena (c. 1158–1179 CE) or attributed to his reign by later tradition.

**munshi** — A Persian-literate scribe or secretary. Under Mughal and Sultanate rule, Kayastha families served as *munshis* — the Persian-language equivalent of their Sanskrit scribal role.

**Maulika / Maulik** — Bengali: "original, fundamental." The second tier of the Kayastha and Brahmin hierarchy — below Kulin but within the upper-caste complex. Deys are Maulika Kayastha.

**qanungo** — A hereditary keeper of land records at the *pargana* (subdivision) level in the Mughal and early colonial revenue system. Kayastha families monopolised this position in many parts of Bengal by the eighteenth century.

**R1a** — Y-chromosome haplogroup. The major paternal-line haplogroup associated with Bronze Age steppe pastoralists (Sintashta/Andronovo culture) and with the Indo-Aryan migration into South Asia. Found at ~35–45% in Bengali Kayasthas.

**Siddha** — Sanskrit: "accomplished, blessed." The designation for the eight first-class Maulika Kayastha families: Dey, Datta, Kar, Palit, Sen, Sinha, Das, and Guha.

**steppe ancestry** — The genetic component derived from Bronze Age Eurasian steppe pastoralists (ultimately Yamnaya/Sintashta cultures). In South Asia, steppe ancestry was introduced approximately 2,000–1,500 BCE by the Indo-Aryan migrants and correlates with the spread of Indo-Aryan languages and Vedic culture.

**tamrashAsana** — Sanskrit: "copper seat/order." A copper-plate inscription recording a royal land grant; the primary documentary form in medieval Bengal from the Gupta period through the end of Hindu rule (~1203 CE).

**Vangaja** — The Kayastha sub-category of eastern Bengal origin, as distinct from Dakshina Radhi (southwestern Bengal), Uttara Radhi (northwestern Bengal), and Varendra (northern Bengal). Dey families are predominantly classified as Vangaja — consistent with the Deva dynasty's geographic sphere in Bikrampur/eastern Bengal.

**varna** — Sanskrit: "colour" or "class." The four-category pan-Indian social system (Brahmin, Kshatriya, Vaishya, Shudra). Bengal's caste system did not develop functional Kshatriya or Vaishya varnas, creating the distinctive two-tier structure (Brahmin / non-Brahmin) within which Kayasthas occupy an anomalous position.
