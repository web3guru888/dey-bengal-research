## Abstract

This report investigates whether the Deva dynasty of Bengal (~1231–1294 CE) and the Shan/Ahom kingdoms of mainland Southeast Asia engaged the Mongol Empire in any coordinated or simultaneous military action during the 1270s–1290s. The answer, supported by converging evidence from Persian chronicles, Chinese dynastic histories, Burmese royal chronicles, and Assamese Burnjis, is **unambiguous**: no such coordination occurred. The three polities — the Deva dynasty of eastern Bengal, the Ahom kingdom in Assam, and the Shan states emerging from the ruins of Pagan — faced entirely different geopolitical situations, lacked any documented diplomatic contact with each other, and fought (when they fought at all) on disconnected, continent-spanning fronts separated by over a thousand kilometres of jungle, delta, and hill country. This report documents each theater separately, examines what the sources actually say, and identifies where gaps in the record remain.

## 1. The Mongol Threat to Bengal: Context and Reality

### 1.1 The Mongols and the Delhi Sultanate's Northwest Frontier

The Mongol Empire's invasions of the Indian subcontinent were focused overwhelmingly on the Punjab-Sindh corridor — the northwestern approaches through the Khyber and Bolan passes. Between 1221 and 1327 CE, the Mongols (initially the main Mongol Empire, later the Chagatai Khanate acting under Kublai Khan's rival western khans) launched at least a dozen major raids into Punjab, occasionally besieging Lahore (1241, 1246), capturing Multan and Uch, and in 1299 reaching the outskirts of Delhi itself.

Sultan Ghiyas-ud-Din Balban (r. 1266–1287) organized the entire edifice of his reign around this threat. He repositioned his military, built fortified lines in Punjab, delegated his son Muhammad Khan to command the frontier, and — crucially — refused to be drawn into distant campaigns that would expose the Punjab flank. As Balban's court historian Ziauddin Barani records, his preoccupation with the Mongols shaped every political decision of his reign.

**The key finding**: Mongol forces never reached Bengal during this period. The Bengal frontier was not a Mongol frontier. The invasions recorded for the years 1279 and 1285 (the two major campaigns during Balban's reign) struck Punjab under Prince Muhammad Khan, not eastern Bengal.

### 1.2 Tughral's Rebellion and the Mongol Pretext

The major Bengal crisis of 1277–1281 was not a Mongol invasion of Bengal — it was a rebellion by Tughral Tughan Khan, Balban's appointed governor of Bengal (Lakhnauti), who declared independence as Sultan Mughisuddin. Tughral had conducted successful raids on Jajnagar (Odisha) and withheld the *khums* (Sultan's share of spoils). According to multiple sources, he was "encouraged by the Mongol threats" besieging Delhi's attention — not by any Mongol presence in Bengal. He calculated that Balban's northwest preoccupation left Bengal unguarded.

**Source**: The Wikipedia article on Balban's conquest of Bengal (citing Mehta 1979, Habib & Nizami 1970) states: "In 1279, the Mongols launched a formidable offensive in Punjab, engaging the royal army under Prince Muhammad Khan in a fierce conflict. Concurrently, Tughral Tughan Khan...felt confident of his power, eventually asserted independence." The Mongol threat was a catalyst for Tughral, not a direct military force in Bengal.

### 1.3 What the Tarikh-i-Mubarak Shahi Actually Says About Danuj Rai

The primary source for the Deva dynasty-Balban meeting is the *Tarikh-i-Mubarak Shahi* of Yahya bin Ahmad (composed c. 1434–1436, drawing on earlier records). Yahya describes Balban's personal campaign to Bengal (1280–1281) and his arrival at Sonargaon, where he met "Danuj Rai," identified by historians as Danujamadhava Ariraja Dasarathadeva of the Deva dynasty.

The text records:
- Balban treated Danuj Rai "with respect as a sovereign prince"
- Danuj Rai agreed to "block Tughral's escape via the rivers in his territory" (the labyrinthine delta channels of eastern Bengal)
- An *ahad-namah* (formal agreement) was drawn up, involving annual tribute (elephants, horses, goods) in exchange for Balban's military protection against Lakhnauti

**Critically**: The *Tarikh-i-Mubarak Shahi* makes **no mention** of Mongols as a motive for this alliance. The alliance was tactical and immediate — to trap Tughral. There is no anti-Mongol dimension stated in the source.

The Banglapedia article on Mughisuddin Tughral provides supplementary detail: "Tughral left Lakhnauti...Balban made an agreement with Danuj Rai of Sonargaon [to prevent] Tughral's escape by riverways towards the south." This is a straightforward tactical arrangement.

**Interpretation**: In the *broadest* geopolitical sense, one could argue that Balban's need to pacify Bengal was motivated by his desire to concentrate military resources against the Mongol threat — he needed a stable rear. In this indirect sense, the Deva-Balban pact has a Mongol dimension. But this is inferential, not textual. The sources do not describe it as an anti-Mongol coalition.

## 2. The Mongol Invasions of Burma and the Role of the Shan

### 2.1 Three Campaigns (1277, 1283, 1287): An Overview

The Yuan dynasty of Kublai Khan launched three major campaigns against the Pagan Empire of Burma, following the Mongol conquest of Yunnan (Dali Kingdom) in 1253:

- **1277**: Mongol forces under Nasr al-Din (son of the Yunnan governor) invaded the northernmost Pagan territories (modern Dehong and Baoshan prefectures, Yunnan). At the **Battle of Ngasaunggyan**, the Pagan army under Narathihapate was decisively defeated despite their war elephants. The Mongols withdrew due to heat and malaria.
- **1283–1285**: A larger Yuan force (c. 10,000) under the Mongol prince Sangudar invaded northern Burma, capturing the frontier fortress of Kaungsin and occupying Tagaung as far south as that on the upper Irrawaddy. King Narathihapate fled to Lower Burma. The Mongols again withdrew due to climate.
- **1287**: A final Yuan force under Ye-sin Timour drove south, possibly reaching Pagan itself. Narathihapate, who had submitted to Mongol vassalage, was assassinated by his son on 1 July 1287. The Pagan Empire effectively collapsed.

**Source**: Wikipedia, "First Mongol invasion of Burma"; also Facts and Details (factsanddetails.com) citing Lieberman, Htin Aung, and Harvey.

### 2.2 The Shan Role: Neither Pure Allies nor Pure Resisters

The question of whether the Shan "fought with the Mongols" or "against them" has a complex answer that varies by time and actor:

**Phase 1 (1277–1287): Pagan against the Mongols, Shan absent or marginalized**

The resistance to the Mongols in 1277–1287 was conducted by the Pagan royal army, not Shan forces. Many Shan principalities in the hills were largely peripheral to the Pagan government even before the Mongol invasions; some were already encircling Pagan from the north and east in a loose tributary relationship.

**Phase 2 (1287–1297): Power Vacuum, Shan Rise**

After Pagan's collapse, three brothers — Athinkhaya, Yazathingyan, and Thihathu from Myinsaing — emerged as the *de facto* rulers of central Burma. British colonial historian Arthur Purves Phayre (19th century) identified these brothers as ethnically Shan, giving rise to the designation "Age of the Three Shan Brothers." However, modern historian Michael Aung-Thwin has disputed this interpretation in a careful 1996 study, finding no contemporary evidence (Burmese, Shan, or otherwise) that their father or they themselves were ethnically Shan. The Burmese chronicles use *sawbwa* (a Shan title) for their father, but this need not indicate ethnicity. The Myinsaing brothers consistently presented themselves as heirs to Pagan's Buddhist Burmese culture.

**Important nuance**: An inscription dated 19 February 1293 shows the brothers claimed that "they were the ones who defeated the Mongol invaders" — meaning they were claiming credit for resisting the Mongols from 1287 onward, not fighting alongside them.

**Source**: Wikipedia, "Myinsaing Kingdom" (citing Than Tun 1959; Aung-Thwin 1996, 1998; Harvey 1925).

**Phase 3 (1297–1301): Myinsaing vs. the Mongols — Direct Resistance**

The critical moment came when the Myinsaing brothers overthrew the Yuan vassal king Kyawswa in December 1297. This forced a Mongol response. In January 1300, Athinkhaya seized the southernmost Mongol garrisons at Singu and Male on the Irrawaddy. The Yuan government dispatched a 12,000-strong force from Yunnan that reached Myinsaing on 25 January 1301. The siege failed; the Mongols were bribed to withdraw on 6 April 1301 and evacuated northern Burma entirely by 4 April 1303.

Crucially, the Military Wiki Fandom page on Mongol invasions of Burma states: "But at the another fortified, three-walled town of Myinsaing, Shan forces checked the Mongols, of whom about 500 were killed in battle in 1300." [CONTESTED — the "Shan forces" designation itself reflects the colonial British reading that Aung-Thwin disputes, but the resistance to the Mongols at Myinsaing is well-documented]

**Separately**, in northern Burma, ethnic Shan principalities occupied a complex middle position: some became nominal tributaries of the Yuan, others maintained autonomy in the hills. The Yuan invasion's most lasting consequence was the political fragmentation that enabled the rise of Shan principalities throughout the "Shan States" region of modern Myanmar — not because Shan fought for or against the Mongols, but because the Mongols destroyed the power (Pagan) that had kept the Shan tributary. The Shan filled the post-Pagan vacuum.

### 2.3 Summary on Shan-Mongol Relations

| Period | Shan Role |
|--------|-----------|
| 1277–1287 | Marginal; some Shan already encircling Pagan; neither significantly allied with Mongols nor resisting |
| 1287–1297 | Myinsaing brothers (possibly but not certainly Shan) resist Mongol vassalage attempts; northern Shan principalities become nominal Yuan tributaries |
| 1297–1301 | Myinsaing definitively resists Mongol invasion; smaller Shan states remain ambiguous tributaries |
| Post-1303 | Shan rise throughout Upper Burma as Pagan's power vacuum is filled; no further Mongol military presence |

The answer to "Did the Shan fight WITH or AGAINST the Mongols?" is: **it depends on which Shan, and when**. Many Shan principalities paid nominal tribute to Yuan. The central Burmese resistance at Myinsaing (possibly Shan-led, per colonial reading; possibly not, per Aung-Thwin) opposed the Mongols. No Shan force appears in Yuan records as an active Yuan military ally against Pagan.

## 3. The Ahom Kingdom and the Mongol Non-Encounter

### 3.1 The Ahom Kingdom in the 1270s–1290s

Sukaphaa, a Shan (Tai) prince from Mong Mao in Yunnan, founded the Ahom kingdom in the Brahmaputra valley of Assam after an epic thirteen-year journey through the Patkai hills, arriving at Namrup in December 1228. By the 1270s–1290s, the Ahom polity was approximately 40–50 years old and still small — a consolidating frontier kingdom in Upper Assam (roughly modern Sibsagar district), surrounded by Kachari kingdoms and Naga/other hill communities.

The Mongols, who conquered Yunnan (Dali) in 1253 and invaded Burma from the north between 1277 and 1287, maintained a military presence in northern Burma as far south as Tagaung on the upper Irrawaddy. They did NOT advance into the Brahmaputra valley. The barrier between Mongol-controlled northern Burma and the Ahom kingdom was the Patkai/Naga hill ranges — challenging mountain terrain that deterred even small military operations.

### 3.2 Did the Mongols Ever Threaten Assam?

**Direct answer: No.** No Ahom Burnji (chronicle), no Chinese source, and no Persian chronicle records a Mongol invasion of or threat to Assam during the 13th century. The Reddit discussion on this topic (r/assam, "Did Ahom kingdom fight Mongol invaders?") reflects scholarly consensus: "There was no invasion of Assam at that time. They invaded in the 1270s–1300. At that time Ahom kingdom was small and surrounded by Kachari kingdoms." The Mongols' Burma campaigns were geographically far removed from the Brahmaputra valley.

**Mong Mao connection**: Sukaphaa's homeland, Mong Mao (modern Dehong, Yunnan), was eventually absorbed into the Mongol/Yuan sphere after 1253. The Grokipedia article on Möng Mao notes: "The state emerged around 1256 in the Ruili area following the Mongol Yuan Dynasty's conquest." But Sukaphaa had left Mong Mao around 1215 CE, arriving in Assam in 1228 — decades before the Mongol conquest of Yunnan. There is no evidence that the Ahom polity under Sukaphaa or his immediate successors had any diplomatic or military connection with either their Mong Mao homeland (now under Mongol suzerainty) or the Mongol Empire itself.

**The Ahom Burnjis** are the primary indigenous source for Ahom history. They record in detail the dynasty's major conflicts — with Kachari kingdoms, Muslim incursions from Bengal, and later the Mughals. They contain no record of any Mongol encounter, threat, or diplomatic exchange.

### 3.3 The Muslim Invasions of Assam: The Actual Threat

What the Ahom kingdom DID face in the 13th century was the expansion of Muslim power from Bengal. According to the Wikipedia article on "Muslim invasions of Assam," the Ghurid-era Bengal Sultanate made initial incursions into Kamarupa (Assam) as early as 1206. The deeper Assamese context involves the Kamrupa kingdom (not yet Ahom) reacting to Ikhtiyaruddin Yuzbak's invasion around 1257 CE, which caused the Kamrupa capital to relocate from Kamrup Nagar to Kamatapur. The Ahom kingdom proper would not come into direct conflict with the Bengal Sultanate until later — but the threat was from the Islamic west, not the Mongol north.

## 4. Was There Any Bengal-Burma-Shan Coordination Against the Mongols?

### 4.1 The Geographic and Political Chasm

Between eastern Bengal (Sonargaon) and Upper Burma (Pagan/Myinsaing), there was in the 1270s–1290s:

1. The swampy Chittagong Hill Tracts
2. The Arakan hills and the medieval Arakan kingdom (an independent political entity)
3. The Irrawaddy delta and Lower Burma (under Pagan nominal control)
4. Upper Burma (then contested between Pagan remnants and the Mongols)

This is approximately 1,200–1,500 km of highly challenging terrain. There is no documented medieval overland or maritime trade route linking Sonargaon to Pagan in this period (the main Bengal-Burma connection was maritime via Arakan, not through political alliance). There is no evidence of diplomatic exchange between the Deva dynasty and any Burmese or Shan polity.

### 4.2 What the Sources Do and Do Not Say

| Source | What it records | What it omits/contradicts |
|--------|-----------------|--------------------------|
| *Tarikh-i-Mubarak Shahi* (Yahya bin Ahmad, c. 1434) | Danuj Rai's alliance with Balban (1281/82); tribute arrangements; blocking Tughral | No mention of Mongols as motive; no mention of Burma/Shan |
| *Tabaqat-i-Nasiri* (Minhaj-i-Siraj, c. 1260) | Earlier Mongol invasions of Punjab; Bengal's governance under Sultanate | Completed before the events discussed here; does not cover the Deva-Balban alliance |
| *Yuan Shi* (Chinese official history of Yuan dynasty) | Burma campaigns (1277, 1283, 1287, 1300–1301); Yunnan as military base; embassies from Southeast Asian states | No mention of Bengal, Deva dynasty, or Ahom in context of Burma campaigns |
| Ahom Burnjis (collective, composed from 13th c. onward) | Sukaphaa's migration; Ahom consolidation; Muslim incursions | No mention of Mongols; no record of any contact with Bengal or Burma in 13th century |
| Burmese royal chronicles (*Yazawin Thit*, *Hmannan Yazawin*, etc.) | Mongol invasions; Pagan's collapse; Myinsaing's resistance (1300–1301) | No mention of Bengal, Deva dynasty, or Ahom as allies or contacts |

**Verdict**: No source from any tradition — Bengali, Burmese, Chinese, or Assamese — records any diplomatic contact, military alliance, or coordinated military action between the Deva dynasty of eastern Bengal and any Shan/Ahom polity. This is not merely an absence of evidence; it is evidence of absence, because these sources were otherwise reasonably comprehensive about the significant political events of their respective regions.

## 5. Bikramaditya Deva's Retreat of 1294: Mongols or Delhi Sultanate?

The Wikipedia article on the Deva dynasty states simply: "His brother Bikramaditya Deva later moved to the eastern side of the kingdom in 1294." This is the last recorded event of the Deva dynasty.

The framing of "eastward retreat" is significant. Was this caused by Mongol pressure? Evidence strongly suggests **no**.

**Context of 1294**: Balban died in 1287. His successor Jalal-ud-Din Khalji (1290–1296) was consolidating power. In 1292 a Mongol force under Abdullah entered Punjab but was defeated. Alauddin Khalji (nephew of Jalal-ud-Din) was raiding Devagiri in 1294. The Bengal situation in 1290–1294 was managed by Balban's son Bughra Khan (who had become independent Sultan of Bengal after Balban's death) and his son Ruknuddin Kaikaus.

The Banglapedia entry on "History" records that "Muslim settlers first arrived in Sonargaon circa 1281" (coinciding with Balban's campaign). The Bengal Sultanate's expansion under Shamsuddin Firuz Shah (1301–1322) eventually absorbed eastern Bengal fully, with Sonargaon becoming the seat of the eastern Bengal Sultanate in the early 14th century.

**Therefore**: Bikramaditya Deva's eastward retreat in 1294 was most likely a response to the expanding Muslim authority of the Bengal Sultanate (under Ruknuddin Kaikaus's successors) pushing eastward from Lakhnauti toward Sonargaon — not a Mongol threat. The Mongols were in northwest India and Burma/Yunnan. There is no record of any Mongol force in or near eastern Bengal in 1294.

[UNCERTAIN]: The precise trigger for Bikramaditya's 1294 retreat is not recorded in detail in surviving sources. It may reflect internal Deva succession or administrative reorganization as well as external military pressure.

## 6. The Structural Argument: Why No Coordination Was Possible

Even setting aside the specific absence of evidence, there are structural reasons why Bengal-Shan-Ahom anti-Mongol coordination was essentially impossible:

### 6.1 Different Mongol Jurisdictions

The Mongol forces attacking Bengal's northwest were the **Chagatai Khanate** (the western Mongol successor state controlling Central Asia). The Mongol forces attacking Burma were the **Yuan dynasty** (Kublai Khan's eastern successor state, based in China). These were rival khanates, especially after the Toluid Civil War of the 1260s. Even from the Mongol side, the northwest Indian campaigns and the Burma campaigns were administratively and militarily distinct.

The Deva dynasty's situation vis-à-vis the Bengal Sultanate (which was itself loosely under Delhi, which was fighting the Chagatai Khanate) was several degrees removed from the Yuan dynasty's Burma campaigns. No message, diplomatic mission, or military coordination between these theaters would have had a clear target or mechanism.

### 6.2 No Shared Communication Infrastructure

There was no postal, diplomatic, or trade route linking the Sonargaon region to the Ahom heartland in Upper Assam or to Pagan/Myinsaing in Burma that is documented in 13th-century sources. The Ahom kingdom was isolated by the Patkai hills from Burma and by the Kachari kingdoms from Bengal. The Deva dynasty was focused on the delta rivers and the Bengal Sultanate frontier.

### 6.3 Political Incommensurability

- The Deva dynasty was a Hindu Kayastha kingdom allied with a Muslim Sultan (Balban) against a rebellious Muslim governor (Tughral)
- The Ahom kingdom was a Shan-Tai polity in the early stages of assimilating into the Assamese Hindu cultural sphere
- The Shan/Burmese states were Buddhist polities fractured between Pagan's collapse and the Mongol occupation

These three political cultures had no common diplomatic language, no mutual ambassadorial contacts, and no shared strategic interest that would have brought them together.

## 8. Conclusions

### 8.1 Direct Answers to the Research Questions

1. **What happened during the Mongol invasions of Bengal?** The Mongols did not directly invade eastern Bengal or the Deva dynasty's territory. Mongol invasions were focused on northwest India (Punjab, Sindh). The Bengal crisis of 1279–1281 was Tughral's rebellion, emboldened by Mongol distraction of Delhi, not a Mongol attack on Bengal. **STRONG EVIDENCE.**

2. **Was Dasharathadeva's Balban alliance anti-Mongol?** No. The *Tarikh-i-Mubarak Shahi* clearly records this as a tactical anti-Tughral arrangement. Indirectly, Balban wanted a secure rear to fight the Mongols in northwest, but this is inferential, not textual. **STRONG EVIDENCE against direct anti-Mongol framing.**

3. **Did Shan forces resist or aid the Mongols in Burma?** Complex answer. In 1277–1287, it was primarily Pagan (not Shan) that resisted. In 1300–1301, Myinsaing (possibly Shan-led; contested) resisted and repelled the final Mongol invasion of Burma. Some Shan principalities paid nominal Yuan tribute. **Shan were neither systematic Mongol allies nor systematic Mongol opponents.** [PARTIALLY CONTESTED: the ethnicity of Myinsaing rulers is disputed]

4. **Was there any Bengal-Shan-Ahom coordination against the Mongols?** **NO. None whatsoever.** No source from any tradition records any such contact or coordination. [STRONG EVIDENCE of absence]

5. **Did the Ahom Kingdom face Mongol forces?** **No.** The Mongols did not reach Assam. The Ahom Burnjis record no Mongol encounter. The geography (Patkai hills, northern Burma interposition) made this essentially impossible in the 1270s–1290s. [STRONG EVIDENCE]

6. **Is there any joint Bengal-Burma-Shan anti-Mongol record?** **No.** The Yuan Shi, *Tarikh-i-Mubarak Shahi*, *Tabaqat-i-Nasiri*, and Ahom Burnjis, taken together, contain not a single mention of such coordination. [STRONG EVIDENCE of absence]

7. **Was Bikramaditya Deva's 1294 retreat Mongol-related?** **No.** It was most likely caused by expanding Bengal Sultanate pressure from the west (toward Sonargaon) as Lakhnauti governors pushed eastward in the 1290s. [UNCERTAIN in specifics, but Mongol explanation is unsupported]

### 8.2 The Broader Historical Picture

The 1270s–1290s represent one of the most dramatic decades in Asian history: the Mongol Empire was simultaneously attacking Japan (1274, 1281), Vietnam (1258, 1285, 1287–1288), Java (1293), Burma (1277–1301), and maintaining pressure on India through the northwest. Yet each of these theaters was isolated from the others.

Eastern Bengal (Deva dynasty) was fighting the micro-level struggle of Hindu sovereignty in a delta overwhelmed by Muslim polities. Assam (Ahom) was a frontier kingdom just finding its feet. The Shan/Burmese states were trying to survive a civilization-destroying assault from Yunnan. None of them knew about each other's Mongol problems, and none had the diplomatic infrastructure, the geographic accessibility, or the political incentive to attempt coordination.

The romantic narrative of a grand Hindu-Buddhist coalition from the Ganges to the Irrawaddy resisting the Mongols together is historically baseless.

## 9. Research Gaps and Future Investigation

1. **Full text of *Tarikh-i-Mubarak Shahi* on Danuj Rai**: English translations are partial; the original Persian may contain additional context on Balban's motivations for the Sonargaon meeting.

2. **Yuan Shi entries on Bengal**: The Chinese dynastic history (*Yuan Shi*) records Mongol campaigns in meticulous detail. A search of its sections on "Da-gan-la" (medieval Chinese for Bengal) might reveal whether Kublai Khan's government had any awareness of the Bengal political situation during the Burma campaigns.

3. **Arakan as a geographic bridge**: The medieval Arakan kingdom lay between Bengal and Burma. Some scholars have suggested limited maritime contact between Bengal and Burma via Arakan in the 13th century. Whether this ever included intelligence about Mongol movements is unexplored.

4. **Mong Mao records**: If any records from the Mong Mao principality (Sukaphaa's homeland) survive in Yunnan provincial archives or the Tai Ahom manuscript tradition, they might illuminate whether the Ahom court ever received news of the Mongol-Burma wars from their homeland connections.

5. **Bikramaditya Deva's fate**: The mystery of what exactly happened in 1294 — whether it was military defeat, a strategic withdrawal to Chandradwip, or internal succession — remains unresolved in current scholarship.
