## Part I: The Destruction of Hindu Mymensingh

### 1.1 The World That Existed

When Aswini Kumar Dey registered his swan trademark in 1926 and fought his court case in 1937, he lived in a town that was **78% Hindu**. The Burra Bazar where he traded was staffed by Kayastha lawyers, merchants, schoolmasters, and civil servants. The zamindari estates of Mymensingh were largely in Hindu Kayastha and Brahmin hands. The town had a race course, a palace, artificial waterworks built by the Maharaja, and the Ananda Mohan College (founded 1908) where the sons of bhadralok families received higher education.

This world — the Hindu-majority commercial town of Mymensingh — no longer exists. Today, only **3.43% of Mymensingh district's population is Hindu** (2022 Bangladesh census). The transformation from 78% to 3.43% happened over seventy-five years in four waves of violence, expropriation, and flight.

### 1.2 The First Wave: Partition (1947)

The Radcliffe Line, announced on 14-15 August 1947, placed Mymensingh district in East Pakistan. The decision was sudden and, for many Hindu families, unexpected. Mymensingh town's zamindars and professionals had expected either that the line would fall differently, or that Pakistan would protect its Hindu minority.

Many chose to stay initially. Upper-class Hindu families — lawyers, doctors, and merchants like Aswini Kumar Dey — often had commercial establishments and property that they were loath to abandon. Approximately **0.32 million Hindus** from East Pakistan crossed to India in 1947 (Indian government estimate), but many more initially stayed.

**The Evacuees (Property) Act 1947** of Pakistan stripped "evacuees" — defined as those who had crossed into India — of their property. This created a terrible dilemma: if you left, you lost your property legally; if you stayed, you might face violence but kept your home. Many chose to stay.

### 1.3 The Second Wave: The Pogroms of January–February 1950

The decisive break came in early 1950.

In late January 1950, a chain of communal incidents — beginning in Khulna and Rangpur and quickly spreading — ignited the worst anti-Hindu violence East Bengal had seen since Partition. The riots engulfed Dhaka, Barisal, Khulna, **Mymensingh**, and Chittagong. Estimates suggest approximately **10,000 Hindus were killed** and tens of thousands of homes, temples, and businesses were destroyed.

The Wire's investigative article on Bengal's refugees records this moment precisely: *"In the last week of January, 1950, a Hindu family of Mymensingh district in East Pakistan decided to leave their ancestral home by night and started to make the journey westward to India."*

The route from Mymensingh to India typically went:
1. **Mymensingh town → Bhairab Bazar by road or country boat** (the Bhairab bridge over the Meghna River, five miles from Mymensingh's orbit, was a key crossing point — and also a site of attacks on fleeing Hindu families)
2. **Bhairab Bazar → Dacca by train** (via the Dacca-Mymensingh Railway, ironically the same line that had connected Mymensingh to the professional world of Dacca when Kayasthas migrated there a century earlier)
3. **Dacca → Calcutta by train** (crossing the Radcliffe Line)
4. **Arrival at Sealdah Station, Calcutta**

The Wire account continues: *"Their train was attacked by marauders who looted and killed a number of passengers on board. The police and Ansars subjected them to humiliating searches and extracted whatever little cash and gold was found in their possession."*

This was the experience of hundreds of thousands of families from Mymensingh. The Bhairab bridge crossing was particularly notorious for violence against Hindu refugees.

**East Bengal Evacuees Act (East Pakistan)**: As with the 1947 Act, properties left behind by those who crossed to India were classified as "evacuee property" and seized by the Pakistani state. Aswini Kumar Dey's umbrella business, his trademark, his Burra Bazar shop — all would have been subject to this expropriation if the family crossed. The commercial empire built over 25+ years was erased at a stroke.

**Scale of the 1950 exodus**: Approximately **1.17 million additional Bengalis Hindus** crossed into India by the end of 1950, of whom the majority came through Sealdah Station.

### 1.4 The Third Wave: 1964–1965

Even those who stayed through 1950 — perhaps 40-50% of Mymensingh's pre-Partition Hindu community — faced renewed violence in the mid-1960s.

The **1964 East Pakistan riots** (sparked by the alleged theft of a relic from a mosque in Kashmir) resulted in mass anti-Hindu violence across East Pakistan. An estimated **600,000 more refugees** left for India during 1964-65.

The **1965 Indo-Pakistan War** was the final blow for many who had clung on. When India and Pakistan went to war, Hindus in East Pakistan became an enemy fifth column in the eyes of Pakistani authorities and Islamist vigilante groups. The Wikipedia article on Mymensingh records: *"A second spell of exodus took place following the Indo-Pak war of 1965. Many people born and raised [in] Mymensingh have left for West Bengal since the 1960s."*

### 1.5 The Fourth Wave: 1971

The Bangladesh Liberation War (March–December 1971) was accompanied by systematic genocide against the Bengali population, with Hindus specifically targeted. Pakistani Army operations explicitly prioritised the elimination of Hindu communities, religious sites, and leaders. The Hindu population of East Pakistan fell by an estimated 3-4 million during and immediately after the 1971 war.

After Bangladesh achieved independence, the Enemy Property Act (re-named from the Evacuees Act) was retained and applied to the remaining Hindu property. The systematic expropriation of Hindu property in Bangladesh has continued through democratic and military governments alike.

## Part II: Where Mymensingh's Hindus Went — The Settlement Map

Understanding where your family likely settled is the first step in tracing the post-Partition genealogy.

### 2.1 The Sealdah Corridor

The vast majority of refugees from Mymensingh entered West Bengal through **Sealdah Station**, Calcutta's eastern terminus — the endpoint of the Dacca-Mymensingh rail line. Sealdah was the principal entry point for East Bengali refugees and, from 1950 onwards, became synonymous with the refugee crisis. From 1946 to the late 1960s, the platforms of Sealdah were continuously occupied by refugees with nowhere to go.

Families who arrived at Sealdah had several options:

**Option 1: Urban settlement in Calcutta**
Families who already had connections in Calcutta — relatives who had migrated earlier, or professional contacts — could settle in the city. The areas where East Bengali Hindu families typically concentrated included:
- **North Calcutta** (Shyambazar, Bagbazar, Hatkhola, Jorabagan) — near Sealdah, already partly populated by East Bengali migrants
- **Park Circus / Ballygunge** — middle-class areas where educated East Bengalis settled
- **Behala, Jadavpur, Dhakuria** — south Calcutta suburbs, where many bhadralok from Dhaka and Mymensingh settled
- **Salt Lake / Bidhan Nagar** — post-1960s government-planned township, heavily East Bengali
- **Howrah** across the river

**Option 2: District towns near the border**
The districts closest to the East Pakistani border received the largest concentrations of refugees:
- **Nadia district** (especially Ranaghat, Krishnanagar, Kalyani): The first major refugee transit camp was at **Ranaghat Cooper's Camp in Nadia** in March 1950. Nadia received ~20% of all 1971 refugees.
- **North 24 Parganas** (Barasat, Bongaon, Habra): Received 22% of 1971 refugees — the largest concentration
- **South 24 Parganas** and **Hooghly** districts also received significant refugee populations

**Option 3: Government camps and Dandakaranya**
The government relocated some refugees — particularly the very poor — to official camps and, controversially, to the **Dandakaranya** forest region in Madhya Pradesh/Odisha. Middle-class bhadralok families generally resisted this relocation and fought to remain in Calcutta.

**Option 4: Other Indian cities**
A smaller proportion of educated East Bengalis moved beyond Bengal:
- Delhi's **Chittaranjan Park** colony — originally called "East Pakistan Displaced Persons' Colony (EPDP)" — is today a thriving East Bengali community in south Delhi
- Mumbai received an estimated 300,000 East Bengalis
- Assam's **Barak Valley** (Silchar, Cachar) received many Sylheti and Mymensingh Hindus

### 2.2 The "Bangal" Identity in West Bengal

East Bengali Hindu refugees — called *Bangal* (বাঙাল) by West Bengali locals, who called themselves *Ghoti* (ঘটি) — faced significant social prejudice in their new home. Despite sharing the same language, religion, and ethnicity, East Bengalis were considered culturally distinct:

- They spoke a distinct dialect (the Eastern dialect, with Mymensingh's own subdialect)
- They ate differently (more fish varieties, different spice blends)
- They had different cultural practices, particularly in music and festivals
- They were seen as economic competitors for land and employment

The social tension between Bangal refugees and Ghoti locals was real and long-lasting. However, the bhadralok East Bengalis — lawyers, merchants, teachers — typically assimilated faster than the poorer refugees, using their education and professional skills to rebuild.

**Families from Mymensingh**, in particular, tended to be well-educated and commercially skilled, and typically settled in Calcutta or the larger district towns rather than in rural camps.

Upper-class merchants sometimes left earlier than the violence required, preserving more assets. ### 3.2 Physical Archives to Contact

**Priority 1: Calcutta High Court Archives**

The published judgment of AIR 1938 Cal 216 is the summary. The **full case file** would contain:
- Aswini Kumar De's original complaint/petition with his personal details (age, father's name, address)
- His sworn testimony (longer than what's quoted in the judgment)
- Correspondence between his lawyer (Mr. Basu) and the court
- The exhibits (the actual umbrella labels — photographs may exist)

**Where**: Calcutta High Court, Original Side Records Room, Calcutta. The archives of the High Court contain case files going back to the 19th century. You or a professional genealogist/lawyer would need to request AIR 1938 Cal 216 (Criminal Revision No. [unknown] of 1937).

**Priority 2: Bangladesh National Archives (Dhaka)**

Mymensingh district records from the colonial period were transferred to what is now Bangladesh. The Bangladesh National Archives holds:
- Registration records from Mymensingh Registration Office, 1926: **Registry No. 964** (Aswini Kumar De's trademark declaration) should be here — this document would contain his name, address, father's name, occupation, and the full description of his trademark
- Land records from the Mymensingh Collectorate
- Trade licences and municipal records

**Where**: Bangladesh National Archives, Dhaka. Access for international researchers is possible but requires advance planning and potentially a local researcher/agent.

**Priority 3: West Bengal State Archives (Kolkata)**

Post-Partition, refugees who had property in East Pakistan could register claims with the West Bengal state government. The **Displaced Persons (Compensation and Rehabilitation) Act 1954** created a system for registering these claims. If Aswini Kumar De (or his sons) registered a claim for the Mymensingh property (the Burra Bazar shop, the trademark), those records exist at:

**Where**: West Bengal State Archives, 6 Bhawani Dutta Lane, Kolkata 700073. Requires a research visit.

**Priority 4: Municipal Birth/Marriage/Death Records**

Once you know where the family settled in West Bengal, local municipal records can trace births, marriages, and deaths. If the family settled in Calcutta, the **Kolkata Municipal Corporation** holds records going back to the early 20th century.

**Priority 5: Voter Rolls / Electoral Records**

The Election Commission of India digitised voter rolls. These can be searched for family members. The earliest available voter rolls in West Bengal date from the 1952 elections (India's first general election) and refugee families who had settled by then would appear.

**Where**: Chief Electoral Officer, West Bengal, or National Voter Service Portal (nvsp.in)

### 3.4 DNA Testing — The Genetic Genealogy Pathway

For Robin Dey personally, DNA testing offers a parallel research track that can work independently of archival records.

#### 3.4.1 Recommended Tests

**Test 1: Y-DNA (Paternal Line)**

The Y-chromosome passes from father to son unchanged (except for mutations). This means a Y-DNA test on **any male descendant of Aswini Kumar Dey in the direct male line** (Robin's father → grandfather → etc.) will reveal the genetic signature of the Dey family's paternal lineage going back thousands of years.

- **Recommended product**: FamilyTreeDNA Big Y-700 (the most comprehensive Y-DNA test; ~USD 449)
- **What it tells you**: 
  - Your Y-haplogroup (likely R1a, H, J2, or R2 for a Kayastha family — see Report 1B)
  - Your Y-STR (short tandem repeat) profile, searchable in FamilyTreeDNA's database for genetic cousins
  - Any matches with other men with the same paternal lineage
- **Key action**: Join the "India Y-DNA Project" and "Bengali Kayastha Project" on FamilyTreeDNA after testing — these projects aggregate results from South Asian men and can help identify haplogroup subclades

**Test 2: Autosomal DNA (Full Family, Both Sides)**

Autosomal DNA testing analyses all 22 pairs of non-sex chromosomes, catching relatives on all branches of your family tree (not just the paternal line). This is the most useful test for finding living relatives.

- **Recommended product**: AncestryDNA (~USD 99) — the largest database with ~22 million people tested; OR 23andMe (~USD 99) — excellent ancestry analysis tools
- **What it tells you**:
  - Ethnicity estimate: Will show South Asian ancestry percentages, likely with "Bengali" or "Eastern India" subcomponent
  - DNA matches: Lists of people with shared DNA — your genetic relatives who have also tested
  - Shared segments: Identifies exactly which parts of your genome you share with matches
- **Critical action**: Search your matches for people with Bengali surnames (De, Dey, Ghosh, Bose, Chatterjee, etc.) and reach out to them — they may be cousins, second cousins, or more distant relatives from the Mymensingh branch of the family

**Test 3: mtDNA (Maternal Line)**

Mitochondrial DNA traces the maternal line (mother's mother's mother's...). Less directly relevant to the Dey family surname research, but can reveal ancestry on the other side.

- **Recommended product**: FamilyTreeDNA mtDNA Full Sequence (~USD 189)
- **Useful if**: You want to trace your grandmother's family (who married Aswini Kumar Dey) separately

If R1a is detected, additional sub-clade analysis (within the R1a tree) may identify the specific population of origin. R1a-Z93 (Z93 subgroup) is the dominant South Asian R1a and is strongly associated with the Indo-Aryan/Vedic migration.

#### 3.4.3 Genealogical DNA Matching Strategy

Once you have autosomal DNA results:

1. **List all matches with shared DNA > 20 cM** (centimorgans — a measure of shared DNA; > 20 cM = likely a third cousin or closer)
2. **Identify matches with Bengali surnames** — these are your most promising leads
3. **Build shared family trees** — reach out to matches and compare family trees; the overlap will identify shared ancestors
4. **Focus on matches who share YOUR unique combination** of ancestry (Bengali + East Bengali/Mymensingh origin) — these are most likely to be from the Dey family or the families your grandparents married into

## Part V: A Note on the Physical Legacy

### 5.1 The Burra Bazar Today

The Burra Bazar (Boro Bazar) of Mymensingh still exists physically. It is still described as the commercial heart of the city between Durgabari Road and Maharaja Road. But the people who made it are gone. Where Aswini Kumar De had his umbrella shop is now almost certainly occupied by a different business, possibly run by a Muslim family or repurposed for a different trade.

The town's demographic inversion — from 78% Hindu to over 96% Muslim — happened entirely within living memory. There are people in their 80s and 90s in West Bengal today who were born in Mymensingh and remember the Burra Bazar as it was.

### 5.2 The Umbrella Business Legacy

We cannot know what happened to the "Aswini Chhati" brand and trademark (Registry No. 964). There are three possibilities:

**Possibility 1**: The business continued operating under Aswini's sons until the family left East Pakistan, perhaps providing income through the 1940s and 1950s. When the family left, the business was simply abandoned or sold for whatever could be recovered.

**Possibility 2**: The business was expropriated under the Evacuees Property Act immediately on the family's departure — as was the case with most Hindu commercial property.

**Possibility 3**: Aswini Kumar De died before Partition (possibly in the 1940s, given his approximate birth year of 1885-1892), and his sons carried on the business until 1947-1950, at which point they left.

In any of these cases, the physical assets are gone. But the legal record — the court judgment, the trademark registration — survives in India's legal archives. That is more than most refugee families can say.

### 5.3 The Enduring Significance of What Was Found

Report 4B identified your grandfather in the historical record through the court case AIR 1938 Cal 216. This is exceptional. Most families of Aswini Kumar De's background left no trace in publicly accessible records beyond census statistics. The combination of:
- A commercial enterprise large enough to have a registered trademark
- A legal dispute that went to the Calcutta High Court
- A published judgment in the All India Reporter

...means that your grandfather is permanently documented in legal history. His name, his business, his address, and his commercial identity are preserved in a way that no fire, flood, or political upheaval can erase.
