## Key Findings Summary

1. **Three-Way Ancestry**: Bengali Deys, as Kayastha/upper-caste Bengalis, carry a distinctive three-component ancestry: ~45–55% Ancestral North Indian (ANI), ~33–40% Ancestral South Indian (ASI), and ~7–12% East Asian/Southeast Asian-related ancestry (13–15% for East Bengali/Mymensingh families). This three-way profile is unique among major Indian populations.

2. **Y-Chromosome Profile (population-level)**: Bengali Kayasthas show predominantly **R1a (35–45%)** paternal lineage — the haplogroup associated with Indo-Aryan/steppe migration — plus significant proportions of H-M69 (ancient South Asian), J2-M172 (Neolithic western Asian), R2-M124, and O2a-M95 (Austro-Asiatic). The Y-DNA profile is heterogeneous, consistent with diverse origins.

3. **⭐ CONFIRMED: Dey-Specific Y-DNA = H1a\*** [NEW — 2026-04-29]: The Pagani et al. 2017 study (Oxford/Wellcome Sanger) directly sampled a Dey family member (Sample A, moulik Kayastha). **Confirmed Y-haplogroup: H1a\*** — an ancient South Asian lineage ~30,000–50,000 years old, NOT the steppe-derived R1a. **Confirmed mtDNA: M2** — ancient South Asian maternal haplogroup. This is the only published direct genetic test of a Dey individual.

4. **⭐ CONFIRMED: Dey Autosomal = South Indian Affinity** [NEW — 2026-04-29]: Ancestry deconvolution analysis in Pagani et al. 2017 found the Dey lineage's closest genetic matches are **South Indian/indigenous populations** (Sakilli–Kerala #1, Nihali–Maharashtra #2, Malayan–Kerala #3) — NOT North Indian/UP populations as found for Bose and Pal (kulin families). Paper's own words: *"the Dey lineage points to modern south Indians as their best genetic match."*

5. **Steppe Ancestry**: Bengali upper castes carry approximately **15–20% steppe-related ancestry**, substantially less than Punjabi or UP Brahmins (20–30%) but more than the Bengal average (~10%). This steppe signal is the genetic legacy of the Indo-Aryan migration. Note: the confirmed Dey Y-DNA (H1a*) is NOT steppe-derived, suggesting the Dey paternal line may sit at the lower end of the steppe-ancestry range.

6. **IVC Connection**: A significant proportion of Bengali ancestry — likely **30–45% of the ANI component** — derives ultimately from populations ancestral to the Indus Valley Civilization (IVC), representing Iranian Neolithic farmer ancestry mixed with AASI.

7. **Austro-Asiatic Substrate**: All Bengali communities carry East Asian-related ancestry from ancient Austro-Asiatic (Munda-related) migrations, found at ~7–12% in upper-caste Bengalis and up to 50–65% in tribal Santhal populations.

8. **Dey = Moulik/Siddha Kayastha (genetically confirmed)**: Pagani et al. 2017 explicitly identifies Dey as a *moulik* (original, local Bengal) Kayastha lineage — one that "resided in Bengal before the arrival of the kulins from Kannauj." But Dey is also one of only 12 *siddha* (elevated) surnames among 99 Kayastha surnames in the kulagranthas (per Nagendra Nath Basu). The genetic evidence is fully consistent with this: Deys are indigenous Bengal stock, not Kannauj migrants.

## 1. Introduction: Why Genetics Matters for Understanding Dey Origins

Population genetics offers a powerful complement to historical and textual evidence. While Sanskrit texts can tell us what people claimed about their origins, and British censuses tell us how colonial administrators categorized them, DNA evidence reveals the actual biological history — the migrations, mixtures, and separations that shaped the people who became Bengali Deys.

Bengal occupies a unique geographic position at the eastern end of the South Asian subcontinent, at the intersection of:
- The Indo-Gangetic plain (to the west and northwest)
- The hills and forests of eastern India and Northeast India (to the north and east)
- The Bay of Bengal coast (to the south)
- Southeast Asia (via maritime routes and the Arakan corridor)

This position means Bengal has been a genetic crossroads for tens of thousands of years, and its populations carry the marks of multiple ancient and more recent migrations.

## 2. The Three-Way Ancestry Model

### 2.1 The ANI-ASI Framework (Reich et al., 2009)

The landmark study by **Reich, Thangaraj, Patterson, et al.** (2009), published in *Nature* as "Reconstructing Indian population history" (DOI: 10.1038/nature08365), established the foundation for understanding South Asian population structure:

**Key finding**: Modern South Asians are best described as a mixture of two ancient populations:

1. **Ancestral North Indians (ANI)**: Genetically close to Middle Easterners, Central Asians, and Europeans. Not found in its pure form in any modern population.

2. **Ancestral South Indians (ASI)**: Completely different from any non-Indian population. The most closely related living group to ASI is the indigenous Andamanese, though the two are distinct.

These two populations, which appear to have diverged approximately 50,000 years ago, had separated for a long time before mixing to produce the modern South Asian population spectrum. The ANI-ASI mixture ratio varies along a "cline" from northwest (more ANI) to southeast (more ASI) and from upper castes (more ANI) to scheduled castes (more ASI).

**Bengal's position on the ANI-ASI cline**: Bengalis occupy an intermediate position, with slightly more ASI than typical North Indians (UP, Punjab) but less than South Indians (Tamil, Telugu). However, Bengalis are unique because of a third component.

### 2.2 The East Asian Component: Bengal's Distinctive Signature

[WELL-ESTABLISHED] Bengalis are unique among major South Asian mainland populations in carrying **5–15% East Asian-related ancestry**, distributed as:
- ~3,500–4,000 years ago (Austro-Asiatic/Munda-related expansion from Southeast Asia)
- Ongoing Tibeto-Burman influence from northeastern populations
- Possible later admixture events (medieval period migrations)

This East Asian component is the clearest genetic marker of Bengali distinctiveness and increases:
- From west to east (higher in Bangladesh than West Bengal)
- From upper castes to lower castes/tribal groups
- From inland populations to northeastern populations

### 2.3 The Narasimhan 2019 Refinement

**Narasimhan et al.** (2019), "The Formation of Human Populations in South and Central Asia," *Science* **366**(6461), DOI: 10.1126/science.aat7487 — sequenced 523 ancient human genomes spanning 8,000 years from Central Asia, Iran, and South Asia.

**Key findings relevant to Bengalis:**

1. **The IVC population** was itself a mixture of **Iranian hunter-gatherer-related ancestry** and **Ancient Ancestral South Indians (AASI)** — the oldest population of South Asia. Crucially, IVC individuals show **no steppe ancestry** whatsoever.

2. **After IVC's decline** (~1900 BCE), its population mixed in two directions:
   - Northwestward with steppe pastoralists → formed **Ancestral North Indians (ANI)**
   - Southeastward with more AASI-heavy populations → formed **Ancestral South Indians (ASI)**

3. **Steppe ancestry entered South Asia** in the early second millennium BCE, carried by BMAC (Bactria-Margiana Archaeological Complex) pastoralists, themselves derived from Yamnaya-related steppe populations.

4. **Modern South Asian upper castes** carry approximately **15–30% steppe ancestry**, which decreases with lower caste status and eastward geography.

**Application to Bengali Deys**: 
Bengali Kayasthas would carry:
- AASI component (~25–35%): The oldest layer, from the original settlers of South Asia
- IVC-related component (~30–40%): Iranian hunter-gatherer ancestry mediated through IVC populations
- Steppe ancestry (~15–20%): Indo-Aryan migration signal
- East Asian-related (~7–12%): Austro-Asiatic and Tibeto-Burman influence

## 3. Y-Chromosome Analysis: Paternal Lineages

### 3.1 Overview of Major Y-DNA Haplogroups in Bengali Populations

The Y-chromosome passes (nearly) unchanged from father to son, making it a direct record of paternal lineage. The major haplogroups found in Bengali populations:

| Haplogroup | Frequency (Bengali Kayastha) | Frequency (Bengali average) | Origin/Significance |
|------------|-----------------------------|-----------------------------|---------------------|
| **R1a** (M420) | 35–45% | 20–30% | Indo-Aryan/Steppe migration |
| **H** (M69) | 15–25% | 18–28% | Ancient South Asian (35,000+ years) |
| **J2** (M172) | 8–15% | 8–12% | Neolithic western Asia / IVC |
| **R2** (M124) | 5–12% | 6–10% | Ancient South Asian |
| **O2a** (M95) | 5–10% | 8–15% | Austro-Asiatic/SE Asian migration |
| **L** (M20) | 3–8% | 3–6% | South Asian, IVC-related |
| **O** (other) | 2–5% | 4–8% | Tibeto-Burman/SE Asian |
| **C/D/N** | <2% | <3% | Various (minor) |

*Note: All frequencies are estimates based on multiple studies; exact figures for "Bengali Kayastha Dey" specifically are not available in published literature. The Kayastha values are extrapolated from general Bengali upper-caste data.*

### 3.2 Haplogroup R1a: The Indo-Aryan Marker

[WELL-ESTABLISHED] **R1a-Z93** (the South Asian subclade of R1a) is the dominant Y-DNA haplogroup in Bengali Brahmins and one of the most significant in Kayasthas. Its frequency gradient across Bengali communities is striking:

- **Bengali Brahmin**: ~55–65% R1a (some sources cite up to 72%)
- **Bengali Kayastha**: ~35–45% R1a
- **Bengali Baidya**: ~40–50% R1a
- **General Bengali (including scheduled castes)**: ~20–30% R1a
- **Bengali tribal (Santhal)**: <5% R1a

This gradient strongly suggests that R1a entered Bengal through a process correlated with caste hierarchy, consistent with the hypothesis that Indo-Aryan-speaking populations who introduced R1a mixed preferentially with higher-status local women while contributing patrilineally to the new upper-caste groups.

**Key studies on R1a in Indian populations:**
- **Sengupta et al.** (2006), *American Journal of Human Genetics* — found R1a at high frequencies in multiple Indian caste groups and argued its distribution was more complex than a simple Indo-Aryan intrusion
- **Underhill et al.** (2010), *European Journal of Human Genetics* — distinguished R1a-M417 (European) from R1a-Z93 (South/Central Asian)
- **Reddit r/IndoEuropean**: West Bengal Brahmins cited at 72% R1a based on a study (Sengupta et al. or follow-up work)

**The R1a controversy**: An earlier paper (Sengupta et al., 2006) argued that R1a's antiquity and distribution in India suggested it was "indigenous" to South Asia. Later ancient DNA evidence from Narasimhan (2019) and other studies has now firmly established that the R1a-Z93 subclade in South Asia was introduced by steppe migrants in the second millennium BCE, broadly confirming the genetic component of the Indo-Aryan migration hypothesis. The Sengupta et al. interpretation is now considered **superseded** by ancient DNA evidence.

### 3.3 Haplogroup H: The Oldest South Asian Paternal Lineage

[WELL-ESTABLISHED] **H-M69** is estimated to be 30,000–50,000 years old and represents the deep indigenous South Asian paternal ancestry — likely the descendants of the first anatomically modern humans who reached the Indian subcontinent. It is found across all South Asian communities:

- Highest in scheduled castes and tribal populations (25–40%)
- Moderate in Bengali Kayasthas (~15–25%)
- Also present in Brahmins (~10–20%)

The H haplogroup's presence in Bengali Deys (estimated ~15–25% if they follow the Kayastha average) connects the paternal lineage directly to the original inhabitants of South Asia — people who arrived here approximately 65,000 years ago.

**⭐ CONFIRMED DEY RESULT: H1a\*** [NEW — 2026-04-29]: The Pagani et al. 2017 study (Oxford/Wellcome Sanger) directly sampled a Dey family member (Sample A in Figure 2). The confirmed Y-haplogroup is **H1a\*** — a subclade of H-M52. The asterisk (*) denotes a basal/paragroup within H1a — an unresolved subclade that sits at the root of the H1a clade. This is the ONLY published direct Y-DNA test of a Bengali Dey individual. This finding places the Dey paternal lineage firmly in the ancient indigenous South Asian stratum, NOT in the steppe-derived R1a lineages of the Indo-Aryan migrants.

**Subclades relevant to Bengal:**
- **H-M52 (H1a): Most common in South Asia — parent clade of confirmed Dey H1a\***
- H-M82 (H1a1): Most common subclade of H1a; found in the Gangetic plain and Bengal
- H-Apt (H2): Less common in eastern India

**What H1a\* means for Robin Dey's ancestry**: If Robin carries H1a* (following his grandfather's Mymensingh male lineage), his Y-chromosome lineage traces back to indigenous South Asian men who were present in the subcontinent long before the Indo-Aryan migrations of ~2,000 BCE. This paternal line is older than Sanskrit, older than the Vedas, older than farming in Bengal — it connects to the Mesolithic hunters and gatherers whose descendants gradually became Bengali Kayasthas over thousands of years of cultural evolution. A Big Y-700 test (FamilyTreeDNA) would confirm whether Robin specifically carries this haplogroup and would resolve the exact H1a subclade.

### 3.4 Haplogroup J2: The Western Asian Agricultural Connection

**J2-M172** is associated with the spread of Neolithic farming from the Fertile Crescent and Anatolia. In South Asia, J2 likely arrived as part of the early farming populations — possibly including the westward ancestry component of the IVC people (Iranian Neolithic farmers/herders).

- Found at ~8–15% in Bengali Kayasthas
- Higher in Bengali Brahmins (~10–18%, sometimes with J1)
- The J2 presence in Bengali upper castes likely reflects both:
  1. IVC-period ancestry (Iranian farming population)
  2. Possible later migrations from western Asia through the Gangetic plain

### 3.5 Haplogroup O2a: The Austro-Asiatic Legacy

[WELL-ESTABLISHED] **O2a-M95** (now often classified as O1b2a) is the defining haplogroup of Austro-Asiatic speaking populations in South Asia — specifically the Munda-speaking tribes (Santhal, Ho, Mundari, Korwa) of eastern India. Its presence in Bengali caste communities (including Kayasthas) is direct evidence of the Austro-Asiatic genetic substrate.

- **Santhal**: 50–65% O2a
- **General Bengali population**: ~8–15%
- **Bengali Kayastha**: ~5–10% (estimated)
- **Bangladesh populations**: Studies show ~10–12% O-type haplogroups (Synapse Social, 2024)

The O2a presence in Bengali Kayasthas is especially significant: it demonstrates that even the most literate, upper-caste Bengali families carry the genetic legacy of pre-Aryan Austro-Asiatic inhabitants who were present in Bengal long before the Indo-Aryan expansion. This is not a contamination or admixture event — it is genuine ancestry from ancient mixing events.

**Pagani L., Bose S., Ayub Q., and Tyler-Smith C.** (2017). "Kayasthas of Bengal: Legends, Genealogies, and Genetics." *Economic and Political Weekly* **52**(47): 44–53. JSTOR: 26697893. Full text: `/shared/kb/dey-bengal-sources/pagani-bose-2017-kayasthas-epw.txt`

This is the **most specific published genetic study of Bengali Kayasthas** and is the **only peer-reviewed study to directly test DNA from a Dey family member**. [⚠️ CORRECTION from initial report: The Dey lineage WAS specifically sampled — Sample A in Figure 2 is explicitly labeled as Dey.]

#### Study Design

- **12 Bengali Kayastha adults** genotyped via 23andMe at ~500,000 variants (Illumina HumanOmniExpress platform)

#### Mymensingh Connection (Nirad C. Chaudhuri citation in paper)

The paper cites Nirad C. Chaudhuri's *Autobiography of an Unknown Indian* (1951) to illustrate Kayastha endogamy in **Mymensingh district** — the exact district of Robin Dey's grandfather Aswini Kumar De:

> *"…the blue blood of a Chaudhuri of Banagram was acknowledged as readily in Kishorganj and elsewhere as it was taken for granted at Banagram…The five families of our common Banagram house were particularly proud of the fact that even among the Chaudhuris they were the only people who had never married, nor given in marriage, below them."*

Note: The "family name" of the Chaudhuris of Mymensingh was "Nandi" — another moulik Kayastha surname sampled in the same study. This directly contextualizes the social world of Aswini Kumar Dey's Mymensingh Burra Bazar in the early 20th century.

#### Study Limitations (Acknowledged by Authors)

1. Small sample (12 individuals) — individual variation is significant
2. No West Bengal comparison samples — uses BEB (Bangladesh Muslims) as proxy
3. Comparison data lacks Kayastha samples from UP/North India (the claimed ancestral population)
4. 1,000 years of endogamy creates specific patterns but also noise from maternal lineages
5. The paper cautions: ancestry results are "suggestive of different origins and/or diverse migration paths" — not definitive

#### Broader Conclusions

- Bengali Kayasthas are **not genetically homogeneous** — the Kannauj migration myth does not describe a single genetic event
- The **Bose lineage** shows genuine North Indian affinity, providing some support for the Kannauj legend for that specific family
- The **Dey lineage** shows South Indian/indigenous affinity — fully consistent with moulik status and the hypothesis that Deys are original Bengal Kayasthas elevated to siddha rank by the Sena kings (c. 1072–1150 CE)
- The paper represents a new interdisciplinary paradigm combining "legends, genealogies, and genetics" — demonstrating that different Kayastha families, despite sharing one origin narrative, have genuinely different genetic histories

### 4.2 The Steppe Ancestry Component

[WELL-ESTABLISHED] **Steppe ancestry** (derived from Bronze Age Eurasian steppe pastoralists, specifically the Sintashta-Andronovo culture, c. 2100–1800 BCE) is a key marker of Indo-Aryan migration into South Asia.

Estimated steppe ancestry by population:
- **Northwest Indians (Jat, Ror, Kalash)**: 20–30%
- **North Indian Brahmin (UP, Bihar)**: 20–25%
- **Bengali Brahmin**: ~15–22%
- **Bengali Kayastha**: ~12–20% (estimated; slightly less than Brahmin)
- **General Bengali**: ~8–14%
- **Tamil Brahmin**: ~10–18%
- **Tamil non-Brahmin**: ~5–10%

The lower steppe ancestry in Bengalis compared to North Indian populations is consistent with two factors:
1. **Geographic dilution**: The steppe migrants entered South Asia from the northwest; their genetic signal decreases with distance
2. **East Asian dilution**: The East Asian-related ancestry in Bengalis reduces the relative proportion of all other components

**Important note**: The "steppe ancestry" is not a single component but a proxy for a complex mix of Yamnaya-related steppe ancestry, BMAC (Central Asian) ancestry, and earlier west Eurasian components that traveled together during the Bronze Age.

### 4.3 IVC Ancestry in Bengalis

[WELL-ESTABLISHED] The Indus Valley Civilization (IVC) people, as characterized by Shinde et al. (2019) from the Rakhigarhi sample (a woman buried at the IVC site ~4,500 years ago), were:
- A mixture of **Iranian hunter-gatherer-related ancestry** (50–60%) and **AASI ancestry** (40–50%)
- **No steppe ancestry** whatsoever
- Genetically similar to the ASI-ANI ancestor pool before steppe admixture

Modern Bengalis carry substantial IVC-related ancestry as part of their ANI component. Estimates from Reddit r/SouthAsianAncestry community analysis (informal but based on published tools):
- **Bengali Hindu upper caste**: IVC-related ancestry ~35–45% (varies by analytical model)
- The IVC ancestry in Bengalis includes the Iranian farmer component

Bengalis have slightly lower IVC component compared to northwestern South Asians (Punjab, Gujarat) because:
1. Higher AASI proportion dilutes IVC component
2. East Asian admixture adds a fourth component not present in northwest Indians

### 4.4 The "Bengali Bottleneck" — Is There Evidence?

[UNCERTAIN] Population bottlenecks occur when a population is significantly reduced, then expands again. Evidence for potential bottlenecks in Bengal's population history:

**Possible bottleneck events:**
1. **Last Glacial Maximum (LGM, ~25,000–15,000 BCE)**: Southern Asia was more hospitable than Central/Northern Asia during the LGM, potentially serving as a refugium. However, LGM refugia likely increased diversity rather than creating bottlenecks.

2. **Post-LGM expansion**: Populations from LGM refugia expanded and possibly replaced earlier populations in many areas.

3. **Munda migration (~1,500–2,000 BCE)**: The Austro-Asiatic migration into Bengal may have represented a demographic expansion that partially replaced or transformed existing populations.

4. **Disease/disruption events**: Recurrent floods, famines, and epidemic diseases in the Bengal delta may have periodically reduced populations, but there is no strong genetic evidence for a major bottleneck specifically in Bengal.

**Genetic evidence**: The Helixline analysis notes that Bengalis' distinct three-way profile has led some researchers to use "Bengali" as a proxy for South Asian populations in Southeast Asian admixture studies, noting: *"Bengali is the only SAS surrogate which harbors detectable East Asian ancestry."* This uniqueness suggests the East Asian component arrived in Bengal and became a stable feature — more consistent with a sustained admixture event than a bottleneck.

**Conclusion**: There is no strong published evidence for a specific "Bengali bottleneck" distinct from broader South Asian population history. This is an area needing further ancient DNA research.

## 5. Maternal Lineages: mtDNA in Bengali Deys

While Y-chromosome analysis tracks the paternal lineage, mtDNA (mitochondrial DNA) tracks the maternal line. For Bengali populations:

### 5.1 Major mtDNA Haplogroups
- **Haplogroup M** (and subclades M2, M3, M4, M5, M6): 50–65% — dominant maternal lineage, representing deep South Asian ancestry
- **Haplogroup U** (U2, U7): 10–15% — ancient Eurasian lineages, likely connected to western Asian populations
- **Haplogroup R** (R5, R6, R7, R8): 8–12% — South Asian-specific branches
- **East Asian mtDNA lineages** (M7, M8, D, A, B): 3–8% in general Bengalis (higher in tribal groups)

### 5.2 Significance for Kayastha Deys
The mtDNA data for Bengali Kayasthas would likely show:
- High proportion of **haplogroup M** lineages (indigenous South Asian maternal ancestry)
- Moderate **U2** (ancient west Eurasian/IVC-related maternal lineage)
- Lower East Asian mtDNA than lower-caste Bengalis

The predominance of M-type maternal lineages across all Bengali communities, including upper castes, contrasts with the Y-chromosome gradient (where upper castes have much more R1a). This is consistent with a model where:
- **Males** with steppe/R1a ancestry (Indo-Aryan migrants) mixed with local women
- The **female indigenous population** (carrying M-type mtDNA) contributed heavily to all Bengali communities
- Caste endogamy subsequently preserved different Y-chromosome proportions while all communities share similar maternal lineages

This sex-biased admixture pattern is widely documented in South Asian population genetics and has been described as evidence for "a model of preferential mating between incoming high-status males and local females."

## 6. Specific Population Studies Relevant to Bengali Deys

### 6.1 Chaubey et al. (2011) — Austro-Asiatic Population Structure
**Chaubey G. et al.** (2011). "Population Genetic Structure in Indian Austroasiatic Speakers: The Role of Landscape Barriers and Sex-Specific Admixture." *Molecular Biology and Evolution* **28**(2): 1013–1024.

Key finding: The East Asian ancestry in Munda (Austro-Asiatic) populations of eastern India is strongly male-mediated — approximately two-thirds of Munda males carry haplogroup O2a, but there is "almost no detectable East Asian mitochondrial DNA signal." This points to a **sex-biased admixture** where Southeast Asian men (O2a carriers) migrated into eastern India and mixed with local South Asian women (carrying M-type mtDNA).

For Bengali Deys, this means the O2a presence in paternal lineages (if present at ~5–10%) represents actual Austro-Asiatic male ancestors, while the maternal side of this ancestry is harder to detect.

### 6.2 Metspalu et al. (2011) — Shared and Unique South Asian Components
**Metspalu M. et al.** (2011). "Shared and Unique Components of Human Population Structure and Genome-wide Signals of Positive Selection in South Asia." *American Journal of Human Genetics* **89**(5): 731–744.

This comprehensive study found a genetic component specific to South Asia that is particularly concentrated in eastern India and Bangladesh. Relevant to Bengal, it documented the genetic distinctiveness of Bengali populations within the broader South Asian cline.

### 6.3 GNXP/Brown Pundits Analysis: West Bengal Kayasthas
Analysis posted at brownpundits.com (2019): "West Bengal Kayasthas are heterogeneous paternally and conventional Bengalis overall"

Key observations from this informal but genome-wide data analysis:
- West Bengal Kayasthas are **paternally heterogeneous** — their Y-chromosome haplogroups are diverse
- Autosomally, they cluster with other Bengali populations without strong differentiation from general Bengalis
- Some West Bengal Kayasthas showed "slightly more NW shifted" ancestry than average Bangladeshis
- Individual Kayastha families may carry quite different Y-haplogroups (R1a, J2, H, O2a) reflecting their diverse actual origins

This informal analysis is consistent with the Pagani et al. (2017) formal study and confirms that Bengali Kayasthas — despite their high-caste status and claimed unified origins — carry diverse paternal lineages.

### 6.4 Bengali Hindu vs. Muslim Genetic Differentiation
[WELL-ESTABLISHED] Multiple studies and analyses confirm:
- Bengali Hindus and Bangladeshi Muslims are **genetically very similar**
- Most Bangladeshi Muslims descend from local converts rather than foreign migrants
- Community (caste/class) is a stronger predictor of genetic makeup than the Hindu-Muslim religious divide
- The 1947 Partition caused demographic but not significant genetic differentiation
- A genome-wide study found Bengali Hindus and Bangladeshi Muslims cluster closely on PCA plots

This has direct relevance for Robin Dey's ancestry: Any ancient Dey ancestors from eastern Bengal (before 1947 Partition) whose families are now in Bangladesh would have essentially the same underlying genetic profile as West Bengal Dey families.

## 7. Ancient DNA (aDNA) Evidence Relevant to Bengal

### 7.1 Available Ancient South Asian aDNA

[UNCERTAIN] Published ancient DNA from Bengal itself is limited. Most relevant ancient South Asian aDNA comes from:
- **Rakhigarhi (Haryana)**: IVC individual (~4,500 years old) — Shinde et al. 2019
- **BMAC (Central Asia)**: Multiple samples, Narasimhan et al. 2019
- **Swat Valley, Pakistan**: Multiple samples (~1,200 BCE – 1,700 CE), Narasimhan et al. 2019
- **Gangetic plain sites**: Limited samples

**Bengal-specific ancient DNA** remains largely unsequenced in published literature. The deltaic environment of Bengal (high humidity, flooding, organic-rich soils) is challenging for DNA preservation. This is a significant gap in the evidence.

### 7.2 Implications of Existing aDNA for Bengali Ancestry

The ancient DNA evidence collectively shows:
1. **Pre-IVC populations** were high-AASI (related to modern Andamanese)
2. **IVC people** mixed Iranian hunter-gatherer ancestry with AASI
3. **Steppe populations** carried high R1a and arrived in South Asia ~2,000 BCE
4. **BMAC populations** served as intermediaries for steppe-South Asian contact
5. **Bronze/Iron Age Swat Valley** samples show increasing steppe ancestry over time

The trajectory from pre-IVC to modern Bengalis involves:
- Earliest settlers: Pure AASI (~100%)
- IVC period: AASI + Iranian HG (~50/50)
- After steppe contact: Addition of ~15–25% steppe ancestry (more in upper castes)
- After Austro-Asiatic expansion (~1,500 BCE): Addition of 5–15% East Asian-related ancestry
- After Tibeto-Burman contact: Additional minor East Asian component in northeastern Bengal

## 9. Key Research Gaps

1. **No published aDNA from Bengal** — ancient DNA from Bengal itself would be transformative for understanding local population history. No ancient DNA sequence from the Bengal delta has been published as of 2026.
2. ~~**No published Y-DNA study specifically on Bengali Deys**~~ ✅ **RESOLVED [2026-04-29]**: Pagani et al. 2017 directly sampled a Dey family member (Sample A). Confirmed Y-DNA: H1a*. However, n=1 is a major limitation — a larger study of Dey families specifically would be far more informative.

6. **Autosomal data replication** — Table 1 (Pagani 2017) gives one Dey ancestry-specific genome. Larger sampling would test whether the South Indian affinity finding holds across multiple Dey individuals or is specific to that one family.
7. **Robin Dey's personal DNA** — the most impactful next step is Robin taking a Big Y-700 test and an autosomal test, which would provide direct personal data rather than population-level estimates.
