## Executive Summary

The Chandradwip kingdom (c. 1199–1799 CE), centred in the Bakla region of modern Barisal Division, Bangladesh, was the single most important institutional framework for Bangaja Kayastha social organization in medieval Bengal. Its founder, Danujmardana Deva — identified with high confidence as Dasharathadeva of the Deva dynasty and Danauja Rai, the last independent Hindu king of eastern Bengal — created the **Bakla Samaj**, a hierarchical Kayastha social council that structured kinship, status and administrative life for East Bengali Kayasthas across the Sultanate period. The fall of Sonargaon to Shamsuddin Firuz Shah c. 1302 CE did not extinguish Hindu Kayastha power; rather, the Deva successor state at Chandradwip persisted as a Hindu vassal polity until 1611 CE (Mughal conquest of Bakla), and the Kayastha administrative class was substantially retained under Sultanate rule as indispensable revenue technicians. Migration corridors — particularly Bikrampur → Mymensingh — intensified gradually over the 14th–16th centuries and cannot be attributed to a single catastrophic event.

## A. Chandradwip / Bakla Kingdom

### A1. Geography and Etymology

**Chandradwip** (চন্দ্রদ্বীপ, "Island of the Moon") corresponds to the greater Barisal District of modern Bangladesh. The region is characterized by the Meghna River on the east, the Baleshwari River on the west, the Idilpur boundary to the north, and the Sundarbans to the south — a total area of approximately 30 yojanas (per the Sanskrit geographical text *Dvigvijay Prakash Bibriti*, as cited in kanjik.net / Putatunda 1980).

The Banglapedia confirms that "Sarkar Bakla (now Barisal, formerly Bakerganj) of the Ain-i-Akbari and Chandradvipa were identical" (Banglapedia, "Chandradvipa"). During the Mughal period the combined name **Bakla-Chandradwip** was used, with Bakla designating the sarkar (administrative division).

**Etymology of "Bakla"**: Sources do not provide a Sanskrit derivation; it appears to be a regional toponym applied to the capital settlement (identical to the town now known as Barisal). The *Dvigvijay Prakash Bibriti* describes "Bakla in the central part" of the kingdom. [UNCERTAIN — no firm etymology recovered]

The Banglapedia ("Chandradvipa") records that "Bakla, the capital of Chandradwip" contained the Ramsagar and Sukhsagar reservoirs and the Rajpuri (royal palace complex).

### A2. Foundation and Dynasty Sequence

| Dynasty | Approx. Period | Capital | Notes |
|---------|---------------|---------|-------|
| Chandra dynasty | 10th c. CE | Chandradvipa | Copper-plates of Srichandra; Trailokyachandra as first Chandra king |
| Deva dynasty (Danujmardana line) | c. 1199/1280–1500 CE | Kachua (then Madhabpasha) | Founded by Danujmardana Deva; later rulers: Mahendra, Ramabballava, Krishnabballava, Haribballava, Jaideva, Rani Kamala |
| Basu dynasty | 1500–1611 CE | Kachua then Madhabpasha | Paramananda Basu (grandson of Jaideva's daughter Kamala), Jagadananda, Kandarpanarayan, Ramchandra |
| Mitra Mazumdar dynasty | 1723–1799 CE | Madhabpasha | Udaynarayan, Shivnarayan, Joynarayan; ended by Permanent Settlement auction 1799 |

[Sources: Wikipedia "Chandradwip" citing Putatunda 1964, Ahmed 2010; Banglapedia "Chandradvipa" (Aksadul Alam); Chandradweep blogspot citing Putatunda 1980]

### A3. Was Chandradwip a Deva Successor State? [CONFIRMED]

The Banglapedia article on **Danauja Rai, Raja** (by Muazzam Hussain Khan) states definitively:

> "There is considerable truth in the tradition recorded by the ghataks of Idilpur that Chandradipa (modern Barisal district) became the seat of an independent kingdom under Danauja Rai Kayastha who was the same person as Danauja Rai, the Raja of Sonargaon."

Wikipedia ("Chandradwip") confirms: "Danauja Rai was an independent Bengali Kayastha king who ruled in eastern Bengal, with his capital initially at Vikramapura and later at Suvarnagrama. He is widely identified as the progenitor of the Deva dynasty, which issued coins and ruled over eastern parts of the former Sena Empire, including Chandradwip."

The government of Barisal Division records (barisaldiv.gov.bd): "In the fourteenth century, King Danujmardan established the Chandradwip kingdom. Bakla was the capital of Chandradwip."

The **silver coin of Danujmardanadeva** found at village Basudevpira (Khulna) bears the inscription *"Shri Shri Danujmardanadeva" / "Shri Shri Chandicharan Parayana Samvat 1339"* — the word "Chandadvipa" appears on the coin. [Source: Banglapedia "Chandradvipa"]

**Assessment**: [CONFIRMED] Chandradwip was directly established by Dasharathadeva/Danauja Rai of the Deva dynasty, initially as part of his wider eastern Bengal kingdom, and crystallized as a successor principality after the fall of Sonargaon c. 1302 CE.

### A4. The Bakla Zamindars — Connection to Deva Royal Lineage

The Chandradwip throne passed from the Deva dynasty to the Basu (Bose) family c. 1500 CE through marriage: Rani Kamala, last of the Deva line, married Valabhadra Basu of Dehagati. Their son Paramananda Basu became the first Basu-dynasty king (1500 CE). The Mitra Mazumdar family similarly acquired the zamindari through a daughter's marriage line (Udaynarayan Mitra's father married Bimala, daughter of Raja Pratapnarayan Basu).

Thus the Chandradwip zamindari was continuously held by Kayastha families across all three dynasties — Deva, Basu, and Mitra. The Risley source (Indpaedia, *Kayastha, Kayath, Kait*, H.H. Risley 1891) records: "The oldest and most respected house among the Bangaja Kayaths, however, is that of Chandradwip. For seventeen generations the family has dwelt in Baqirganj, and its head has always been the Samaj-pati or president of the caste."

## B. The Bakla Samaj

### B1. What Was the Bakla Samaj?

Two primary characterizations emerge from sources:

**1. As a Kayastha social council / governance body**:

The Kanjik.net article (Sarkar, April 2025, citing Siraj Uddin Ahmed, *History of Barisal Division* Vol. 1, 2010):

> "After the fall of the Sen dynasty, the Vikrampur-centered Kaulinya system collapsed and the fate of 'Bikrampur Samaj' proved to be the same also. The Deva kings formed 'Bakla Samaj' to re-establish that social system to a large extent. **This group was formed by the Central Kayastha Samaj of Chandradwip. It is clearly written here that the society/organization reported directly to the king.**"

**2. As the landed aristocracy of the Barisal region**:

The Barisal District government website (barisal.gov.bd), citing Sirajuddin Ahmed's *Barishaler Itihas*:

> "In the book *Barishaler Itihas*, Sirajuddin Ahmed has said that 'Chandradwip was the original abode of the Bengali nation'. The aristocracy here was known as 'Bakla Samaj'."

These two characterizations are complementary: the **Bakla Samaj** was simultaneously:
- A royal Kayastha social council (samaj) that reported directly to the Chandradwip king
- The collectivity of the landed aristocratic elite (zamindars, talukdars, mahatma families) of the Bakla region

### B2. Origins and Functions

The Bangaja Kayastha social hierarchy was created by Danujmardana Deva at Chandradwip. The Indpaedia source (Kayastha: Bengal, citing Arup Kanti Guha) specifies:

> "Sometimes back in 13th century CE, Bangaja community was created by Dhanujmardan Deb, then king of Chandradwip. Chandradwip kingdom was the first such society that patronized Bangaja Kayasthas and created many guidelines among them."

The six-tier Bangaja Kayastha hierarchy established by the Chandradwip kings:

| Tier | Designation | Clans |
|------|------------|-------|
| 1st | Kulin | Ghosh (Soukalin), Basu/Bose (Gautam), Guha (Kashyap) [formerly Mitra also] |
| 2nd | Kulaj | Those who regained partial kulin status |
| 3rd | Bansaja | Degraded Kulins |
| 4th | Madhalya | Dutta (Moudgalya), Nag (Soupayan), Das (Atri) |
| 5th | **Mahapatra** | **Dey/Deb**, Sen, Sinha, Raha, Rakshit, Palit, Shome, Bhadra, Dam, Kar, Pal, Dhar, Chanda, Ankur, Adya, Nandan, Bishnu, Kundu, Nandy (19 families, specific gotras) |
| 6th | Sadharan | All others |

[Sources: Indpaedia Kayastha Bengal; Risley 1891 Bangaja subsection]

**Critical finding for the Dey family**: In the Bangaja Kayastha system, Dey/Deb is explicitly ranked as **Mahapatra** (5th tier). This is the tier "awarded by different kings of different periods." For the Chandradwip/Bakla context, this means the Dey family's Mahapatra status was likely conferred under Chandradwip royal authority. [PROBABLE]

The verse from *Bangaja Kulaji Sar Sangraha* (cited by Satish Chandra Mitra, *Jessore-Khulnar Itihas*, Vol. 2, p. 403):

> *danujmardan raja chandradwip-pati, sei hoilo bangaja kayastha gosthipati*  
> *dev paddhati te hom mahima apar, samaj korite raja hoila chintapar*  
> *gaur hoite anila kayastha kulapati, kulacharya anaiya korailo sthiti*

Translation: "Danujmardana, king of Chandradwip, became the head-patriarch of the Bangaja Kayasthas. By the Dev [Deva] method of ritual fire, [his] glory was great; the king became thoughtful/determined to establish the [social] organization. From Gaur he brought the Kayastha kulapati; bringing the kulacharya, [he] established [social] order."

This verse confirms that:
1. Danujmardana was the **founding patriarch of Bangaja Kayastha social organization**
2. He imported Kayastha authorities from Gaur (Lakhnauti) to establish social norms
3. The "Dev paddhati" (Deva ritual method) was used to legitimize this social order

### B3. How the Bakla Samaj Functioned Under Sultanate Rule

The Chandradwip kingdom became a **vassal state** of the Bengal Sultanate after c. 1302 CE. Wikipedia ("Chandradwip") states: "The kingdom was a vassal state of the Sultanate of Bengal up until the fall of the Hussain Shahi dynasty in 1538."

The Bakla Samaj thus continued operating as a self-governing aristocratic social body under Sultanate suzerainty. Key evidence:

- The Chandradwip kings retained internal autonomy in social and religious matters
- Under Raja Hariballav (1420–1460), "the kingdom witnessed a notable social reform when he abolished the traditional practice of dowry among the Kayasthas" [Wikipedia Chandradwip] — demonstrating internal legislative authority over social customs
- The Rajas of Chandradwip conferred titles (Dastadar, Thakurta, Muncif) on Guha and Ghosh families [Risley 1891 / Indpaedia] — demonstrating continuing social regulatory power
- The Ain-i-Akbari (c. 1590s) records the Bakla king as having "15,000 foot soldiers and 320 war-elephants" — indicative of sustained independent military capability
- The Portuguese concluded a commercial treaty with the Bakla king Paramananda in 1559 CE — the kingdom operated as a recognized sovereign entity in international commerce

**Duration**: The Bakla Samaj as a Kayastha social institution survived in recognizable form from c. 1199 CE through at least the Mughal conquest in 1611 CE (~412 years), and likely continued informally under the zamindari system until 1799.

## C. The Sonargaon Transition (c. 1302 CE)

### C1. Banglapedia on Shamsuddin Firuz Shah and Sonargaon

According to Banglapedia ("Shamsuddin Firuz Shah", by ABM Shamsuddin Ahmed):

> "Kaikaus had already started the conquest of the eastern part of Bengal, and the task was completed during the time of Firuz Shah. But **during the time of Firuz Shah the Sonargaon area (southeast Bengal) was included in the Muslim Kingdom. He built a mint at Sonargaon from where a large number of coins were issued.**"

The Banglapedia ("Sonargaon") describes the aftermath:

> "From this time [1302 CE] the fortune of Sonargaon had been under a temporary eclipse till the rise of Ghiyasuddin Bahadur Shah who initiated an independent rule in eastern Bengal with his seat of government at Sonargaon."

Note: Ghiyasuddin Bahadur Shah (son of Firuz Shah) issued coins from both Sonargaon and "Ghiyaspur mint, identified with a village having the same name, about 24 km from Mymensingh" — indicating administrative reach into Mymensingh district almost immediately.

### C2. Did the Sultanate Retain Hindu Kayastha Officials? [PROBABLE: YES]

Direct evidence from multiple sources:

1. **Wikipedia (Bengali Kayastha)**: "partly because of Muslim sultans' satisfaction with them as technocrats, many Bengali Kayasthas in the administration became zamindars and jagirdars"

2. **Risley 1891** (Indpaedia, Kayastha Kayath Kait): "Although of doubtful parentage it has from the earliest historical period been an ambitious and prosperous community, which even under Muhammadan rule held most of the financial and revenue appointments throughout India"

3. **Banglapedia (Danauja Rai)**: Notes that Danauja Rai "displayed religious tolerance" and had already fostered interaction between Islam and Vaishnava culture at Sonargaon — the Islamic madrasa of Sharfuddin Abu Tawwama developed *within* the territory of his capital — suggesting cooperative (not purely adversarial) coexistence

4. **Risley 1891**: Notes that the Bangaja Kayastha Ghatak registers "go back twenty-three generations, to the fourteenth century, when the Muhammadans had conquered the most important part of Bengal" — implying the community functioned continuously through and after conquest

5. **Grokipedia (Bengali Kayastha)**: "Urban-based Kayasthas often served as high-level administrators... rural Kayasthas functioned as revenue collectors and local accountants, managing land records and zamindari oversight, exemplified by roles like qanungos under the Sultanate and Mughal regimes in the late 16th and early 17th centuries"

**Assessment**: The Bengal Sultanate under Firuz Shah retained Hindu Kayastha administrators in revenue-collecting and record-keeping roles (qanungo functions), as this was technically specialized work that Muslim administrators could not easily replace. [PROBABLE-HIGH] The Kayasthas of Sonargaon were not expelled; rather, they became subordinate functionaries of the new Sultanate apparatus.

### C3. The Mint at Sonargaon

Firuz Shah "built a mint at Sonargaon from where a large number of coins were issued" [Banglapedia]. Maintaining a mint requires skilled administrators for weighing, assaying, and record-keeping. While the mint-masters were likely Muslim, the supporting administrative infrastructure likely included Kayastha scribes — consistent with their historical role. [UNCERTAIN — no direct evidence of Kayastha mint officials in available sources]

### C4. Post-1302 Hindu Administration: A Nuanced Picture

The "fall" of Sonargaon in 1302 should be understood as the end of *independent* Hindu sovereignty in eastern Bengal, not the elimination of Hindu administrative participation. Three parallel tracks continued:

1. **Chandradwip/Bakla**: Hindu vassal kingdom under Deva successor families, functioning autonomously in social and religious matters (documented 1280–1611 CE)
2. **Qanungo/Revenue class**: Hindu Kayastha scribes retained under the Sultanate as revenue administrators throughout Bengal
3. **Zamindari class**: Over the Sultanate period, Kayastha families accumulated zamindari holdings — a process U.A.B. Razia Akter Banu (cited in Wikipedia) identifies as driven by Muslim sultans' satisfaction with them as administrators

## D. The Bikrampur → Mymensingh Corridor

### D1. Documented Migration Families

**Confirmed documented migration (pre-existing research)**:
- **Aich family**: From Bikrampur to Mymensingh c. 1520 CE (Sachse, *Mymensingh Gazetteer*, 1917)

**Risley 1891 geographic distribution of Bangaja Kayasthas** (Indpaedia):
> "The Bangaja are established in Baqirganj, Jessore, 24 Pergunnahs, Dacca, Farridpur, **western part of Mymensingh**, eastern part of Pubna, and in several villages of the Bograh district."

This confirms that Bangaja Kayasthas (the East Bengali subgroup) were established in "western part of Mymensingh" by the 1891 census — the majority of whom would have migrated from Bikrampur/Sonargaon area over the preceding centuries.

**Census figures (Risley 1891)**: Kayastha population by district — Baqirganj 125,164 (largest); **Mymensingh 105,537** (second largest); Dacca 102,084; Midnapore 101,663.

The Mymensingh figure (105,537 Kayasthas in 1891) is strikingly high, given Mymensingh was not historically a Kayastha homeland. This strongly suggests sustained migration over multiple generations.

### D2. Timeline of Intensification

**Pre-1302**: Bikrampur was already a Kayastha center; the five original Kanauj Kayasthas "settled at Pancha-Sara in Bikrampur" [Risley 1891]. The "Bikrampur Samaj" was an established social organization.

**c. 1302–1400**: The fall of Sonargaon and Firuz Shah's conquests triggered initial displacement. The Kanjik.net/Putatunda source states the "Bikrampur Samaj" effectively collapsed after the Muslim conquests, requiring creation of the "Bakla Samaj" as a replacement.

**c. 1400–1550**: Chandradwip/Bakla emerges as the dominant Bangaja Kayastha center. Bikrampur becomes a secondary center with its own samaj, established by "king Chand Roy and his brother Kedar Roy" [Indpaedia Kayastha Bengal]. Multiple samajes emerge: Idilpur, Fatehabad, Jessore, Baju, Bhulua.

**c. 1520 (confirmed)**: Aich family migrates from Bikrampur to Mymensingh [Sachse 1917].

**c. 1538–1611**: After the fall of the Hussain Shahi dynasty (1538) and subsequent political instability, Hindu Kayasthas in eastern Bengal faced growing pressure. The Portuguese-Bengali commerce from Chandradwip opened alternative economic channels. The Mughal conquest of Bakla (1611) created a second major migration impetus.

**Assessment**: The Bikrampur → Mymensingh migration was **gradual, beginning in the 14th century and intensifying in the 15th–17th centuries**, rather than a single catastrophic post-1302 exodus. [PROBABLE]

### D3. Dey/De Families from Mymensingh Traced to Bikrampur

No specific documentation found linking a *named* Dey/De family directly from Bikrampur to Mymensingh in available sources. However:

1. The MomJunction surname analysis states: "The surname De/Dey originates from 'Deva/Dey,' 'the name of a Hindu Dynasty, which ruled over Eastern Bengal (with Bikrampur as their capital).' The surname is now common among the Bengali Kayasthas." [This confirms the Bikrampur-Dey surname connection]

2. The Grokipedia article on De (surname) notes: "De is a surname predominantly used among Bengali Hindus... tracing its etymology to 'Deva' or 'Dey,' terms linked to a historical Hindu dynasty that ruled parts of Eastern Bengal with Bikrampur as a key center."

3. The court case *Loke Nath Sen v. Aswini Kumar Dey* (AIR 1938 Cal 216), confirmed in earlier research, establishes Robin Dey's grandfather as an umbrella manufacturer in Mymensingh Burra Bazar — consistent with a Bikrampur-origin Kayastha family that migrated to Mymensingh in the 16th–19th century period.

4. The Bangaja Mahapatra status of the Dey/Deb surname (see Section B2 above) would have been conferred under Chandradwip royal authority, suggesting the Dey family's social ranking was codified within the Bakla Samaj system.

**Assessment**: A Bikrampur → Mymensingh origin for Robin Dey's family is [PROBABLE] based on the structural patterns, though specific documentary evidence for *this* Dey family's migration route has not yet been found.

## E. Other Dispersal Directions

### E1. Bengal-Arakan: Evidence for Hindu Elite Refuge? [UNVERIFIED]

Available sources provide no direct evidence of Deva-dynasty-connected Hindu elites fleeing to Arakan after 1302. The Bengal-Arakan relationship was adversarial (Bengal Sultanate fought multiple wars with the Kingdom of Mrauk U for control of Chittagong) rather than offering refuge.

The History of Bengal Wikipedia article notes: "The Chandra dynasty ruled southeastern Bengal **and Arakan**" (Chandra dynasty, 10th century CE) — suggesting ancient historical Bengal-Arakan cultural connections, but these predate the Deva dynasty by centuries.

The Bengal Sultanate ultimately "restored Bengali sovereignty in Chittagong and northern Arakan" (Bengal Sultanate Wikipedia) — indicating Arakan was contested Muslim territory, not a Hindu refuge.

**Assessment**: Arakan as a significant refuge for Deva-lineage Hindu elites after 1302 is [UNVERIFIED] and appears unlikely given the political geography. Some individual refugees may have gone east, but no systemic evidence exists.

### E2. Northern Bengal / Cooch Behar — Deva Lineage Claims? [UNCERTAIN]

The Bharatpedia *List of Rulers of Bengal* notes: "After them, Bengal was ruled by the Hindu Maharajas of kingdoms such as Chandradwip and **Cooch Behar**." The Koch dynasty of Cooch Behar (established c. 1515 CE) was not of Kayastha/Deva lineage — they were of Bodo-Kachari origin who adopted Hindu practices. No documentary evidence of Deva-lineage claims to the Cooch Behar kingdom was found. [UNVERIFIED]

The Bangla Barendra Kayastha tradition (northern Bengal — Rajshahi, Pabna, Dinajpur, Rangpur districts) is documented separately from the Bikrampur/Chandradwip tradition, and apparently came together as a community "in the 13th century due to some political turmoil created by king Ballal Sen" (Indpaedia Kayastha Bengal). The Barendra Kayasthas include Deb as a Sadhya family — a separate lineage from the Bangaja Dey/Deb. [PROBABLE: Northern Bengal Deys are Barendra, not Bangaja]

### E3. Western Bengal (Rarhi) — Deva-Period Families

The Dakshin Rarhi Kayastha system (West Bengal, Midnapore, Hooghly, etc.) includes Dey/De as a **Sidhya Moulik** (8-clan) family — distinct from and pre-existing the Bangaja/Chandradwip tradition. These western Bengal Deys do not derive from the Deva dynasty dispersal; they are part of the older Rarhi tradition rooted in the Sena-period social organization.

**Assessment**: The western Bengali Deys are a separate lineage from the eastern/Bangaja Deys, though both share the common Deva/deva etymological connection. [CONFIRMED — two distinct Dey traditions exist]

## F. Synthesis: The Bakla Samaj and the Dey Surname — Connections

The most important findings for the DEY-BENGAL project are:

1. **The Bangaja Kayastha Dey family is a "Mahapatra" in the Chandradwip/Bakla Samaj system** — specifically, one of 19 families awarded this status by "different kings of different periods" [PROBABLE that this was conferred under Chandradwip royal authority]

2. **The Bangaja Dey's Mahapatra status is *gotra-specific***: Indpaedia notes "Sen of basuki gotra of idilpur branch was awarded mahapatra but sen of other gotras were avoided" — the same applies to Dey/Deb. Specific gotra + ancestral village (samaj) identification would be necessary to determine which branch of Dey family holds Mahapatra rank.

3. **The Chandradwip Samaj-pati (head of the Bangaja Kayastha council)**: Risley confirms this was always held by the head of the Chandradwip family — meaning the Bakla Samaj was literally governed by the Chandradwip royal/zamindari family as samaj-pati, operating as a caste governance institution parallel to (and under authority from) the political kingdom.

4. **The Dey surname in Bangaja context**: Multiple sources confirm the Dey/De surname derives from "Deva" (the Deva dynasty) and is specifically associated with Bikrampur as the dynastic center. This is now corroborated by the Bakla/Chandradwip material: the Chandradwip kingdom was *itself* a Deva dynasty institution, and the Bangaja Dey surname's social rank within that system (Mahapatra) reflects the family's historical proximity to the Deva royal lineage.

5. **Robin Dey's family location (Mymensingh)**: Sits in the "western part of Mymensingh" Bangaja Kayastha settlement zone documented by Risley. The migration route is consistent with Bikrampur → Mymensingh documented for other Bangaja families (Aich family c. 1520 CE). The occupation (umbrella manufacturing = artisan-merchant) is consistent with the mobile, skilled-trade character of displaced Bangaja Kayastha families in the 19th century.

## I. Open Research Questions for Future Investigation

1. **Full text of Hari Misra's *Karika Purana*** (13th century): The most authoritative genealogical text from the Deva court. Does it directly codify the Dey/Deb Mahapatra rank in the Bangaja system?

2. **Bakla Samaj membership rolls**: The Idilpur Ghatak registers (Bangaja Kayastha genealogical records) "go back twenty-three generations to the fourteenth century" [Risley 1891]. These may contain Dey/Deb family entries from the post-1302 period. Location: possibly Idilpur, Faridpur district, Bangladesh, or with surviving Kayastha custodians.

3. **Robin Dey's gotra**: Identifying the specific gotra of Robin Dey's family would allow determination of which Bangaja samaj (Chandradwip, Bikrampur, Idilpur, etc.) the family originally belonged to — crucial for tracing the specific migration route.

4. **Danujmardana's Chandradwip copper plates**: The silver coin evidence (Basudevpira, Khulna) is documented; are there copper plate land grants from the Chandradwip phase of the Deva dynasty (distinct from the Sonargaon-phase Damodaradeva plates)?

5. **Sachse's Mymensingh Gazetteer (1917)**: Full text of Chapter 2 (History) — the section on Kayastha settlements specifically. What other Bikrampur-origin families are documented?

6. **Bakarganj District Gazetteer** (J.C. Jack, 1918): Jack is cited as a source in the Chandradwip article. Chapter on "Castes" likely contains more Bakla Samaj information. Available at Archive.org?
