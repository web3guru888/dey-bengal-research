## Abstract

This report examines the primary historical and epigraphic sources relevant to the Dey (De / দে / দেয়) surname of Bengal, situating it within the broader documentary record of medieval and colonial-era Bengal. The evidence is organised chronologically from the earliest Gupta-era inscriptions through to the 1931 Census of British India. The central finding is that the Dey surname crystallised as a hereditary marker between the twelfth and fourteenth centuries CE, most plausibly in the context of (a) the Deva dynasty's rule over eastern Bengal (c. 1231–1317 CE, with the Chandradwip branch surviving to c. 1500 CE), whose name provides the etymological root; (b) the Sena dynasty's formalisation of the Kayastha caste hierarchy, which positioned Dey-bearing families as *Maulika* (non-Kulin) Kayasthas; and (c) the disruption caused by the Islamic conquest of Bengal (1203–1322 CE), which catalysed the crystallisation of many Bengali Hindu surnames. The report assesses the reliability of each source type and identifies areas where the documentary record is incomplete or contested.

## 1. Introduction

The surname Dey — alternatively spelled De, Dev, Deb, and Debnath — is among the most recognisable Bengali Hindu cognomens. Borne primarily (but not exclusively) by members of the Bengali Kayastha caste, it is today distributed across West Bengal, Bangladesh, Tripura, and the Assamese and Sylheti Bengali diaspora. The name's deep resonance with the Sanskrit root *deva* (divine, godly) has encouraged popular etymologies connecting it to divine lineage or priestly ancestry. More rigorously, historians trace it to the regional Deva dynasties that ruled different parts of Bengal between the eighth and thirteenth centuries CE.

Reconstructing the documentary history of a surname is inherently difficult: unlike dynastic genealogies, surnames of non-ruling communities were rarely recorded in primary sources. The present report traces the Dey surname through four overlapping source traditions: (1) epigraphic evidence from copper-plate inscriptions; (2) indigenous textual traditions including *kulagranthas* (caste genealogies); (3) Mughal administrative records; and (4) colonial-era census and ethnographic documentation.

## 2. The Deva Dynasty and Its Copper-Plate Inscriptions (c. 1231–1294 CE)

### 2.1 The Dynasty: An Overview

The later Deva dynasty [WELL-ESTABLISHED] was a Bengali Hindu ruling house that controlled south-eastern and eastern Bengal from approximately the first quarter of the thirteenth century until the Muslim consolidation of the region around 1294 CE. Its capital was at **Bikrampur**, in present-day Munshiganj District of Bangladesh — a region that remains densely settled by Kayastha families bearing Dey-derivative surnames to this day.

The dynasty must be distinguished from an earlier, unrelated **Buddhist Deva dynasty** of the eighth–ninth centuries CE, which ruled in the Samatata region (centred on Devaparvata/Mainamati, near modern Comilla). That earlier Buddhist dynasty — whose inscriptions and spectacular architectural remains at Shalvan Vihara, Ananda Vihara, and Bhoja Vihara have been exhaustively documented through the Mainamati excavations — was of Koeri caste affiliation and has no demonstrable connection to the Bengali Kayastha Dey community (Banglapedia, *Deva Dynasty* entry). The later Vaishnava Deva dynasty is the genealogically significant one.

The founder of the later Deva line was **Purushottamadeva**, who rose from the position of a *gramani* (village headman), suggesting a non-royal origin [WELL-ESTABLISHED]. His son and successor, **Madhumathana** (also called Madhusudanadeva), was the first to claim independent royal status, adopting the title *nripati* ("lord of men"). The dynasty then passed to **Vasudeva** and to his son **Damodaradeva** (r. 1231–1243 CE), who was the most powerful ruler of the line (Roy, 1993, *Bangalir Itihas*, pp. 408–9; Wikipedia, *Deva dynasty*).

A later ruler, **Ariraja-Danuja-Madhava Dasharathadeva** (also known as Danauja Rai / Danuj Roy), extended the kingdom westward to encompass Bikrampur proper, making it the capital. He was the dynasty's most diplomatically and militarily active ruler: he negotiated a treaty with Sultan Ghiyasuddin Balban (~1281 CE), defeated Balban's son Bogra Khan's punitive expedition, and in 1294 CE formed a naval alliance with Arakan's King Ming-Hette that captured Sonargaon from the Delhi Sultanate — a remarkable military victory for a Hindu kingdom against the most powerful Islamic state in South Asia. It was only in **1317 CE** that Shamsuddin Feroze Shah retook Sonargaon, and in **1318 CE** that Dasharatha's **brother**, **Vikramaditya Dev**, shifted the capital southward to **Chandradwip** (Kachua, modern Barisal). The dynasty thus survived the 1294 Sonargaon campaign by more than two decades, and the Chandradwip branch persisted for a further two centuries (Sarkar 2025, citing Jogendra Nath Gupta, *History of Bikrampur*, vol. I, pp. 323–329; Rajnikanth Chakrabarty, *Gaudher Itihas*).

### 2.2 The Three Copper-Plate Inscriptions of Damodaradeva

The primary documentary sources for the later Deva dynasty are **three copper-plate inscriptions** issued by Damodaradeva in his 4th, 6th, and 13th regnal years — corresponding to 1234 CE, 1236 CE, and 1243 CE respectively [WELL-ESTABLISHED]:

1. **The Mehar Copper Plate (1234 CE)** — discovered in the Mehar area, Comilla District. This is the earliest of Damodaradeva's plates.  
2. **The Shobharampur Copper Plate (1236 CE)** — from the Shobharampur area, Dhaka District. This plate is critical because it helps establish the dynasty's reach into the Bikrampur heartland.  
3. **The Nasirabad/Chittagong Copper Plate (1243 CE)** — from Nasirabad in Chittagong District. This is the most important of the three because it contains the dynastic genealogy of the Deva house, naming the three predecessor rulers (Purushottamadeva, Madhusudanadeva, Vasudeva) and establishing the regnal chronology (Banglapedia, *Inscriptions* entry; Wikipedia, *Deva dynasty*).

In standard Indian inscriptional convention, these copper plates record **land grants** (*bhumi-dana*) — the donation of revenue-exempt land to Brahmin donees or religious institutions. Like virtually all copper-plate *tamrashAsanas* of Bengal, they follow a formulaic structure: an opening invocation (*mangalacharana*), a genealogical section praising the issuing king (*vamsavarnana*), the terms of the grant, the boundaries of the donated land, the list of witnesses and officials, and a closing imprecation against violators of the grant.

**Epigraphic analysis** of the Damodaradeva plates was conducted in the tradition established by **D.C. Sircar** (Dineshchandra Sircar, 1907–1985), the pre-eminent Bengali epigraphist who served as Chief Epigraphist of the Archaeological Survey of India and published extensively in *Epigraphia Indica*. Sircar's *Indian Epigraphy* (1965, Motilal Banarsidass; repr. 2008) provides the methodological framework for reading such plates. His earlier work on Bengal inscriptions — including contributions to *Epigraphia Indica* volumes — established the palaeographic continuity between the Pala, Sena, Deva, and Chandra dynasties' scripts, all being variants of the north Indian Nagari tradition (Sircar, 1965; Banglapedia, *Inscriptions*). The Deva dynasty copper plates also show a style of script that "was the later version of Nagari, which was similar to the script of the Gupta era" (Banglapedia, *Copperplates*).

**Content relevant to surnames:** Like other copper plates of the period, the Deva plates would have named officials (*amatyas*, *vishayapatis*), scribes (*lekhaka* or *kayastha*), and possibly witness-signatories with names ending in *-deva* — a common honorific suffix for high-status Hindus across Bengal since at least the Gupta period. [UNCERTAIN: No published English translation of the Deva copper plates' full witness lists has been consulted for this report; the plates have not been fully transcribed in accessible scholarship.] If the standard pattern of Bengal copper plates holds, Kayastha scribes would appear in the witness lists — and some of these scribes may have borne the *deva* suffix as part of a personal name that later crystallised as a surname.

### 2.3 Copper Plates of Dasharathadeva

Copper plates have also been discovered belonging to **Dasharathadeva**, Damodaradeva's successor. One copper plate of **Ranavankamalla Harkaladeva** and one of **Viradharadeva** have been found at Charpatra Mura, though their precise relationship to the Deva ruling line is debated [UNCERTAIN] (Banglapedia, *Inscriptions*). These plates extend the epigraphic corpus into the second half of the thirteenth century CE.

### 2.4 Fall of the Dynasty and Surname Crystallisation (1317–1322 CE)

A pattern observed in the sociology of South Asian surnames is that hereditary surnames tend to crystallise during periods of **social disruption and dislocation** — precisely when families needed stable markers of identity to preserve status and social networks across dispersal. The fall of the later Deva dynasty fits this model — though the timeline is more drawn-out than earlier versions of this report suggested.

**Revised chronology (updated April 2026):**
- **1294 CE**: Dasharatha Dev (Danauja Rai) and Arakan ally **Ming-Hette** defeat the Delhi Sultanate and capture Sonargaon — a *victory*, not a retreat.
- **1317 CE**: Shamsuddin Feroze Shah and Bahadur Shah retake Sonargaon using full Sultanate forces. Deva power in central Bengal is effectively broken.
- **1318 CE**: Dasharatha's **brother**, Vikramaditya Dev (later called Danujmardan Deb), shifts the capital southward to **Chandradwip** (Kachua, Barisal region), founding what becomes the three-century-long Chandradwip Kayastha kingdom.

**Shamsuddin Firuz Shah** (r. 1301–1322 CE) of the Lakhnauti Sultanate then consolidated Muslim political authority over eastern Bengal, building a mint at Sonargaon and extending the sultanate to encompass the former Deva heartland of Bikrampur and beyond (Banglapedia, *Shamsuddin Firuz Shah*). Hindu Kayastha families who had served the Deva administration — and who perhaps bore *-deva* in their personal names as an honorific or court title — would have found that the consolidation of Muslim rule made it necessary to preserve their identity and status through hereditary means.

This process of surname crystallisation from the Deva name is plausible but [UNCERTAIN]: no surviving document explicitly records the moment when "Deva" became the heritable surname "De/Dey." The scholarly consensus (Risley, 1891; Inden, 1976; Eaton, 1996) treats the crystallisation of Bengali Hindu surnames as a medieval process broadly spanning the eleventh to fourteenth centuries, accelerated by the social upheavals of the Islamic conquest.

## 3. Earlier Epigraphic Sources: Pala and Sena Era (750–1203 CE)

### 3.1 Gupta-Period Foundations: The Kayastha as Administrative Class

The earliest historical documentation of the Kayastha in Bengal derives from **Gupta-period copper plates** (4th–6th centuries CE). These plates — thirteen of which have been found in Bengal, including examples from Damodarpur (Dinajpur), Baigram (Bogra), Jagadishpur (Rajshahi), and Paharpur (Naogaon) — name officials and scribes in the Gupta administrative system (Banglapedia, *Inscriptions*). The term **"Kayastha"** appears in Gupta inscriptions as a professional designation for scribes (*lekhaka*) and revenue officials, not yet as a hereditary caste marker.

The office of **"Prathama-Kayastha"** (chief scribe or "probably the Chief Secretary to the administrative board") appears in Gupta inscriptions from Bengal and Bihar (Sharma, 1978, *Personal and Geographical Names in the Gupta Inscriptions*, p. 115; cited in Wikipedia, *Bengali Kayastha*). The historian **Tej Ram Sharma** observes that surnames like Datta, Dama, Palita, Pala, Kunda (Kundu), Dasa, Naga, and Nandin — found in early Gupta-era Bengal inscriptions — are now confined almost exclusively to Bengali Kayasthas, suggesting strong scribal-caste continuity across fifteen centuries (Sharma, 1978, p. 115). The surname *Deva* is not explicitly present in Sharma's list, but the widespread use of *-deva* as a personal name-suffix in early Bengal inscriptions establishes the linguistic precedent.

**R.C. Majumdar** (1971, *History of Ancient Bengal*, Calcutta: G. Bharadwaj) is more cautious, noting that the argument for large-scale Nagara Brahmin migration into Bengal — sometimes proposed to explain Kayastha origins — is "hardly convincing," since surnames like Datta, Ghosh, Varman, Nag, and Mitra were used widely across India in this period and cannot be taken as proof of a single originating migration (Majumdar, 1971, p. 435).

### 3.2 Pala Dynasty Copper Plates (750–1174 CE)

The **Pala dynasty** — which ruled Bengal and Bihar for nearly four centuries, from the mid-eighth to the late twelfth century CE — produced the largest corpus of copper-plate inscriptions from pre-Islamic Bengal. Key plates include the **Khalimpur copper plate** of Dharmapala (the second Pala ruler), which documents administrative geography and officials; the **Badal Pillar Inscription** (Dinajpur), which records the activities of a ministerial family across three generations of Pala kings from Devapala to Narayanapala; and more recently discovered plates of Gopala II from Sherpur, Bogra (Banglapedia, *Inscriptions*).

These plates are important for establishing that **Kayastha officials were integral to the Pala administrative apparatus**. The witness lists of Pala copper plates regularly include categories of local officials — *mahattaras*, *vishayapatis*, *ashtakuladhikaranas* — alongside scribes designated as Kayastha or Karana. The lexicographer **Vaijayanti** (11th century CE) treats Kayastha and Karana as synonymous terms for scribes, confirming their unified administrative identity by the late Pala period (Banglapedia, *Kayastha*; Wikipedia, *Bengali Kayastha*).

**Personal names ending in *-deva*** are pervasive in Pala copper-plate witness lists. These are not yet family surnames but personal honorifics — a distinction critical to understanding when "Deva" became hereditary. The Pala period, despite its long duration, does not appear to have generated heritable Bengali surnames in the modern sense; that crystallisation occurred later.

### 3.3 Sena Dynasty and the Kulin System (1070–1203 CE)

The **Sena dynasty** (c. 1070–1203 CE) produced a smaller corpus of copper plates — the most important being the copper plate of **Vijayasena** (the dynasty's founder), the **Deopara Stone Inscription** (Rajshahi) commemorating Vijayasena's conquests, the **Paikore Pillar Inscription**, and plates of later Sena rulers from Madanpara and Idilpur (Faridpur district) (Banglapedia, *Inscriptions*).

The Sena period is epigraphically important for the Dey community for a different reason: the legendary social reforms attributed to **Ballala Sena** (r. 1158–1179 CE), which formalised the **Kulin system** among Bengali Brahmins, Kayasthas, and Baidyas. According to this system:

- **Kulin Kayasthas**: five elite lineages — Bose (Basu), Ghosh, Mitra, Guha, and Datta
- **Maulika (Maulik) Kayasthas**: all other Kayastha lineages, including those bearing the Dey/De surname

The Wikipedia article on **Ballala Sena** explicitly notes: "The Brahmins were classified into Kulin, Śrotriya, Vamsaja and Saptasati; the Kayasthas were classified into Kulin and Maulik, **but there is no historical authenticity**" [emphasis added] — meaning this classification appears in later genealogical texts (*kulagranthas*), not in any surviving contemporary Sena-era inscription or royal record. This is a critical evidentiary qualification [CONTESTED].

The **Dey surname's placement as Maulika** (rather than Kulin) is thus attested in later kulagranthas attributed to the Sena period, but the primary documentation for Ballala Sena's reforms comes from much later sources.

### 3.4 The Kulagranthas: Medieval Genealogical Texts

The most direct textual evidence for the Dey surname's caste status comes from the **kulagranthas** (*kula* = lineage/clan, *grantha* = text) — a genre of Bengali genealogical literature that records the hierarchical ranking of Kayastha (and Brahmin and Baidya) lineages. These texts, typically composed or compiled between the fifteenth and eighteenth centuries, retroactively attributed rank assignments to Ballala Sena and, in some versions, to the mythological king Adisur.

A study of Bengal kulagranthas (Citeseer citation, *Communities, Kings and Chronicles: The Kulagranthas of Bengal*) notes that the institution of Kulinism attributed to Ballala Sena "was further modified and regulated" by later rulers, and that the texts themselves represent a "quasi-historical, sociological narrative" deployed to explain caste realities of the early modern period (Gupta, 2009, *Notions of Nationhood in Bengal*, p. 103–104; cited in Wikipedia, *Bengali Kayastha*).

Crucially, the kulagranthas include the **Dey (Deb/Dev) surname** among the Kayastha lineages designated as **"siddha"** (blessed/elevated) within the Maulika tier. According to **Nagendra Nath Basu**, the historian and encyclopaedist who studied these genealogies, the list of elevated (*siddha*) Kayastha lineages included: Bose, Ghosh, Guha, Mitra, Datta, Nag, Nath, Das, **Dey**, Sen, Palit, and Sinha — with the Kulin five (Bose, Ghosh, Mitra, Datta, Guha) being the highest ranked (Indpaedia, *Kayasth* entry, citing Nagendra Nath Basu). Additionally, the Indpaedia article on *Kayastha: Bengal* records that **nineteen families of specific gotras were awarded *mahapatra* status** by different kings: "Sen, **Deb/Dey**, Sinha, Raha, Rakshit, Palit, Shome, Bhadra, Dam, Kar, Pal, Dhar, Chanda, Ankur, Adya, Nandan, Bishnu, Kundu, Nandy." This list places Deb/Dey at second position — immediately after the Sen family — suggesting a relatively high rank within the Maulika tier.

**Ronald Inden's** 1976 monograph *Marriage and Rank in Bengali Culture: A History of Caste and Clan in Middle Period Bengal* (University of California Press) is the most important modern scholarly treatment of Bengal's caste hierarchy in this period. Inden documents the subcaste organisation of the Bengali Kayastha (around 1500 CE) into four major groups: *Daksina-radhi*, *Vangaja*, *Uttara-radhi*, and *Varendra*. Within *Daksina-radhi* and *Vangaja*, the Kulin/Maulika division applies. The Dey families are most commonly found in the **Vangaja** sub-category (eastern Bengal origin), which aligns precisely with the Deva dynasty's geographical sphere. This Vangaja identification is consistent with the Bikrampur/Munshiganj geographical heartland of the Dey surname.

## 4. The Sultanate and Mughal Periods (1204–1757 CE)

### 4.1 The Sultanate Period: Kayastha Continuity Under Muslim Rule (1203–1576 CE)

Following the Islamic conquest of Bengal by **Bakhtiyar Khilji** in 1203–1204 CE, the documentary record for Hindu castes thins dramatically. The new sultanate administration kept records in Persian and Arabic; the era of copper-plate land grants to Brahmin donees effectively ended. As the Banglapedia notes: "After the Muslim occupation of Bengal not a single epigraph on copper-plates has been found" (*Inscriptions*). This creates a significant evidentiary gap for Kayastha family history spanning 1204 to approximately 1580 CE.

What the historical record does establish — drawing on later sources like **Alauddin Husain Shah's** reign (1493–1519 CE), the most prosperous period of the Bengal Sultanate — is that Kayasthas continued to function as administrators and revenue officials under Muslim rule. **Richard Eaton** observes that Kayasthas "continued as the dominant landholding caste" even after the Islamic conquests, absorbing the descendants of the region's old Hindu rulers (Eaton, 1996, *The Rise of Islam and the Bengal Frontier*, p. 102). Eaton further describes the Kayasthas as becoming Bengal's "surrogate Kshatriya or warrior class" — filling the power vacuum left by the collapse of the Pala, Sena, Varman, Chandra, and Deva ruling dynasties.

The political scientist **U.A.B. Razia Akter Banu** writes that "many Bengali Kayasthas in the administration became zamindars and jagirdars" under the Muslim sultans, "partly because of Muslim sultans' satisfaction with them as technocrats" (cited in Wikipediam.org, *Kayastha*). This pattern — Kayastha administrative continuity through political transitions — explains how Dey-bearing families could accumulate land and wealth under sultans who had overthrown the Deva dynasty to which their surname may have been linked.

### 4.2 Mughal Bengal: The Ain-i-Akbari and the Qanungo Records (1576–1757 CE)

The **Ain-i-Akbari** (*Administration of Akbar*), composed by **Abu'l Fazl** between 1589 and 1596 CE as the third volume of the *Akbarnama*, provides the most systematic Mughal-period description of Bengal's administrative geography and its population. The work describes the Bengal Subah (*province*) as one of the fifteen *subahs* of Akbar's empire and includes statistical tables of revenue assessment by *sarkar* (district) and *pargana* (subdivision) [WELL-ESTABLISHED].

**Abu'l Fazl's direct observation on Bengal's Kayastha zamindars** is documented by Banu: the *Ain-i-Akbari* notes that "most of the Hindu zamindars in Bengal were Kayasthas" — a remarkable statement given the total Muslim political authority of the period. This confirms that, by the late sixteenth century, Kayastha families (including Dey-bearing ones) had successfully converted administrative knowledge and connections into substantial landed wealth [WELL-ESTABLISHED, though the precise passage should be verified against an authoritative translation of the Ain-i-Akbari].

The **qanungo** was the key local revenue official in the Mughal system — a hereditary keeper of land records at the *pargana* level. The historian **Irfan Habib** describes Kayasthas as "the second layer of revenue management in Mughal India, dealing with rudiments of revenue collection, land records, and paper management, where their basic Persian literacy and copying skills were put to use. By the eighteenth century, Kayasthas' control of the qanungo position had essentially become hereditary" (Wikipedia, *Chitraguptavanshi Kayastha*, citing Habib). This is entirely consistent with the Dey community's historical role: a Kayastha family bearing the Dey surname serving as hereditary revenue scribes and eventually becoming zamindars under the colonial Permanent Settlement (1793 CE).

A **Jessore case study** is instructive: historical records document that the Jessore *raj* was "created under Mughal rule by a Kayastha soldier of Man Singh's army" in the late sixteenth century (Ray, 1975, *The Bengal Zamindars*, p. 16) — demonstrating how Kayastha families leveraged Mughal service into zamindari status. Similar pathways would have been available to Dey-bearing families elsewhere in Bengal.

The **Baharistan-i-Ghaibi** and other Mughal Bengal chronicles — particularly those covering the campaigns against the *Baro-Bhuyans* (twelve landlords) of eastern Bengal in the late sixteenth and early seventeenth centuries — mention Hindu zamindars generically but rarely by caste surname in ways that would identify Dey families specifically [UNCERTAIN: full Mughal Bengal archive search required].

## 5. Colonial Documentation: Bengal District Gazetteers

### 5.1 The District Gazetteer System

The **Bengal District Gazetteers** were compiled under the Government of Bengal between approximately 1907 and 1923, drawing on information from district collectors, local officials, and literate elites. They constitute the most systematic colonial-era record of community distributions, notable families, zamindari estates, and local history for each district.

The gazetteers most relevant to the Dey community are those for districts with historically dense Kayastha populations:

**Dacca District Gazetteer** (B.C. Allen, 1912; available at Archive.org): The Dacca district encompassed Bikrampur (Munshiganj) — the heartland of the later Deva dynasty and the geographic centre of Vangaja Kayastha settlement. This gazetteer would contain information on the Kayastha zamindari families of the region, local history of Bikrampur, and settlement patterns. The tradition of Adisur inviting five Brahmins and five Kayasthas from Kanauj to resettle Bengal — a founding legend of Bengali Kayastha identity — is associated specifically with Rampal near Munshiganj (Bangladesh on Record, *History of Dhaka*). [This report has not been able to retrieve the full text of the Dacca Gazetteer; direct consultation required.]

**Faridpur District Gazetteer** (L.S.S. O'Malley): Faridpur district (which historically included areas near Bikrampur's zone of influence) and the Sena copper plates found at Idilpur and Madanpara make this gazetteer relevant for Kayastha history in the delta region.

**Jessore Gazetteer**: Contains information on the Kayastha zamindari tradition in southwestern Bengal, including the Jessore raj's founding history noted above.

**Burdwan Gazetteer**: Relevant to Dey families of Rarhi (southwestern) Bengal, where the Suvarna Banik community (which also uses Dey) was historically prominent.

**Murshidabad Gazetteer** and **Chittagong Gazetteer**: The former relevant to Mughal-period Kayastha revenue officials; the latter relevant to the Chittagong copper plate of 1243 CE and the Deva dynasty's eastward extent.

## 6. Census Records and Ethnographic Classification

### 6.1 H. Beverley, *Report on the Census of Bengal, 1872*

**Henry Beverley's** 1872 Bengal Census (*Report on the Census of Bengal, 1872*, Calcutta: Bengal Secretariat Press, 1873) is the first systematic enumeration of the Bengali population by religion and caste. This census recorded a population of approximately 36.9 million in Bengal Province. Kayasthas are recorded as a discrete category among high-caste Hindus.

The 1872 census was primarily a demographic exercise rather than an anthropological one; it did not disaggregate by individual surname within the Kayastha community. However, it established baseline population counts by district, which allow subsequent censuses to track demographic change. The Dacca division (including the Bikrampur area) would have returned some of the highest concentrations of Kayastha population in eastern Bengal [UNCERTAIN: specific figures for Dey-bearing Kayasthas not available without direct consultation].

### 6.2 H.H. Risley, *Tribes and Castes of Bengal* (1891)

**Sir Herbert Hope Risley's** *Tribes and Castes of Bengal* (4 vols., Calcutta: Bengal Secretariat Press, 1891–92) is the most important colonial-era primary source for the Dey community. The work is available in digitised form through Archive.org and Google Books. Volume 1 of the Ethnographic Glossary contains an entry for **"De"** (spelled with variant spellings De, Dey, Deb) at approximately page 216 of the original 1891 edition [WELL-ESTABLISHED location; content requires verification against the original].

Risley's entry on De/Dey in *Tribes and Castes of Bengal* is expected to cover:
- Etymology: derivation from the Deva dynasty name or from *deva* as a title
- Caste affiliation: primarily Kayastha; also Suvarna Banik, Teli, and others
- Geographical distribution: heavier concentrations in eastern Bengal (Dacca, Faridpur, Jessore, Chittagong districts)
- Social rank: Maulika (non-Kulin) Kayastha; higher rank (mahapatra) within Maulika tier
- Marriage practices and kinship rules

Risley's broader ethnographic method — combining anthropometric measurement with social observation — has been extensively critiqued for its racial essentialism (Fuller, 2017, LSE; Stophindudvesha.org article). His racial typology, which attempted to rank castes by nasal index and skull measurements, is now entirely discredited as science. However, his ethnographic observations about community practices, distributions, and internal hierarchies retain evidential value when read critically.

**Banglapedia's Kayastha entry** names "Dev" (with its variants Dey, De, Deb) among the common Kayastha surnames alongside Chanda, Gupta, Nag, Aditya, Nandi, Mitra, Sil, Dhar, Kar, Datta, Rakshit, Ghosh, Bose, and Guha (Banglapedia, *Kayastha*). This confirms the Dey surname's primary Kayastha affiliation in the colonial ethnographic record.

### 6.3 The 1901 Census and the Kayastha Varna Controversy

The **1901 Census of India**, conducted under Risley as Commissioner, introduced a systematic varna-based ranking of castes — assigning each community to Brahmin, Kshatriya, Vaishya, Shudra, or other categories. This provoked one of the most contentious caste disputes in colonial Bengal: **the status of the Bengali Kayastha**.

The controversy has direct relevance to the Dey community. A report by **Risley and Edward Albert Gait** for the 1901 census classified Bengali Kayasthas as **Shudra**, citing their "failure to observe scriptural dogma and rejection of the sacred thread" as evidence of a loss of upper-caste status (Wikipedia, *Bengali Kayastha*, citing Ghosh, 2023). This provoked immediate resistance from educated Kayastha professionals, who formed the **Kayastha Sabha** under **Nagendra Nath Basu** and published the *Kayastha Patrika* journal to argue against the Shudra designation.

The **1884 Calcutta High Court ruling** in *Rajcoomar Lal v. Bissessur Dayal* had earlier adjudged Bengali Kayasthas to be Shudras in law, though the judgment acknowledged their "degradation from an earlier Kshatriya status" (Bellenoit, 2023, *Law and History Review*, 41:1, pp. 43–63). This "degradation" theory was a colonial legal construction that served British administrative interests in fixing what were actually fluid social positions.

**The 1931 Census** (the last census before Indian independence) noted that the Bengal census volume was "disturbingly ambiguous" on Bengali Kayasthas: on one hand associating them with "upper classes of Brahmins and Vaishyas," and on the other noting they "have invariably been held to be Sudras" (Bellenoit, 2023, cited in Wikipedia, *Bengali Kayastha*). **J.H. Hutton** (the 1931 Census Commissioner for India) observed in his *Caste in India* (1961) that the Kayastha "ranks next to Brahman" in Bengal and "is now commonly regarded as twice-born, and itself claims to be Kshatriya, though it was perhaps more often regarded as clean Sudra a hundred years ago" (Hutton, 1961, p. 65) — a statement summarising the trajectory of colonial classification. [WELL-ESTABLISHED for the controversy; the specific status of Dey-bearing families within this debate is not separately documented]

### 6.4 Population Figures and District Distribution

Based on the census records and Risley's ethnographic surveys, the Kayastha population of Bengal in the late nineteenth and early twentieth centuries can be estimated:

- The 1872 census recorded Bengal's total Hindu population at approximately 23 million; Kayasthas typically constituted perhaps 2–4% of the Hindu population
- In eastern Bengal (Dacca, Faridpur, Chittagong, Noakhali divisions), Kayastha concentrations were higher — reflecting Vangaja settlement patterns
- The 1901 census introduced detailed caste tables, but individual surnames within Kayastha were not disaggregated [UNCERTAIN: specific census tables for De/Dey surname not consulted]

A reasonable inference, given that Dey/De is among the most common Kayastha surnames and given the Vangaja heartland connection, is that Dey-bearing families were most heavily concentrated in **Dacca, Faridpur, Jessore, and Barisal** divisions of eastern Bengal — precisely the regions corresponding to the former Deva dynasty territory and its neighbourhood.

## 7. The Deb Variant and Comparative Surname Evidence

### 7.1 De vs. Dey vs. Deb vs. Dev

The surname cluster De/Dey/Deb/Dev/Debnath represents regional and orthographic variations of a single ancestral form. The Wikipedia article on *Deb (surname)* confirms: "In 12th–13th century, a Bengali Hindu dynasty Deva ruled over eastern Bengal after the Sena dynasty" — explicitly linking all these variants to the later Deva dynasty (Wikipedia, *Deb (surname)*).

The distribution of variants is geographically patterned [WELL-ESTABLISHED]:
- **De**: formal/written Bengali; primarily West Bengal (Rarhi and Vangaja contexts)  
- **Dey**: common Bengali spelling; widespread across West Bengal and Bangladesh  
- **Deb**: predominantly North Bengal (Rajshahi, Rangpur divisions), Tripura, and Assam  
- **Dev**: Hindi-medium usage; also found in urban pan-Indian contexts  
- **Debnath**: found among lower-caste groups (e.g., Namasudra community) — may represent a different surname crystallisation process [UNCERTAIN]

### 7.2 The Suvarna Banik Connection

A significant complication is that the Dey surname is not exclusively Kayastha. The writer **Lal Behari Dey** (1824–1892), for example, was born into the **Suvarna Banik** (goldsmith-merchant) caste of Bardhaman, not Kayastha — though different sources variously describe him as Kayastha and Suvarna Banik, itself illustrating the ambiguity (Wikipedia, *Lal Behari Day*; Readomania). This ambiguity is important: it demonstrates that the surname crystallised independently in multiple communities, each with its own relationship to the *deva* root.

Risley's *Tribes and Castes of Bengal* likely addresses this cross-caste distribution. The surname is also attested among **Teli** (oil-presser) and **Kansari** (bell-metal worker) communities, suggesting that "Dey" could be adopted by communities seeking to associate themselves with the prestige of the Kayastha scribal class or the Deva dynastic name.

## 8. The "Siddha" Status in Kulagranthas: Direct Textual Evidence

Perhaps the most direct evidence for the Dey surname in medieval Bengal comes from the kulagrantha tradition. The Indpaedia article on *Kayasth* cites Nagendra Nath Basu's analysis of Bengal's Kayastha genealogical texts, noting that in "the genealogies of Rarh, all Kayasthas except 'Gautam Bose, Soukalin Ghosh, Viswamitra Mitra, Kashyap Guha and Bharadwaj Datta' (where the prefixes refer to gotra or clan) were considered *Gour-kayastha*" — i.e., local stock. The listed surnames designated as "siddha" (blessed/elevated) within the Maulika tier include: **Dey** (explicitly listed).

This kulagrantha evidence establishes the Dey surname's antiquity within the Bengali Kayastha genealogical tradition. While the kulagranthas' attribution of the Kulin system to Ballala Sena is historically problematic (as Wikipedia notes, "there is no historical authenticity" for contemporary documentation of this), the texts themselves are real medieval documents that preserve community memory about surname rankings. The **Vangaja sub-classification** of Dey families within eastern Bengali Kayastha lineages is consistent with the Bikrampur/Munshiganj geographical origin suggested by the Deva dynasty connection.

## 9. Notable Historical Dey Figures as Evidential Markers

The emergence of documented historical figures bearing the Dey surname provides indirect evidence for the community's presence and prominence across different periods:

- **Lal Behari Dey (1824–1892)**: Born in Bardhaman, Bengal; Christian missionary, writer, and author of *Folk Tales of Bengal* (1883). His Dey surname reflects the surname's distribution in the Suvarna Banik community of southwestern Bengal, not Kayastha Bengal. [Source: Wikipedia, *Lal Behari Day*]

- **Bengal zamindari Dey families** (18th–19th centuries): The Permanent Settlement of 1793 froze zamindari rights at the household level, creating a large paper trail of proprietary records. Zamindari records from Dacca, Faridpur, Jessore, and Burdwan districts would likely record Dey-bearing landowning families. [These records are housed in the Bangladesh National Archives and the West Bengal State Archives; not consulted for this report — a significant gap]

- **Santanu Dey** (contemporary): Descendant of Bengal zamindars, subject of an art exhibition exploring the lost legacy of zamindari families (ASAP Art, 2020). While modern, this figure illustrates the zamindari tradition of the Dey community.

The kulagrantha listing of Dey among the *siddha* lineages implies that by approximately the fifteenth–sixteenth centuries CE (when most kulagranthas were compiled or edited), the Dey surname was already a recognised, established marker within the Kayastha community's genealogical consciousness.

## 11. Conclusion

The documentary history of the Dey surname in Bengal can be reconstructed with reasonable confidence at the macro-level, even where specific primary sources are inaccessible. The convergence of several lines of evidence points toward a consistent picture:

1. **The *deva* etymology is well-established** — the surname derives from the Sanskrit *deva*, likely crystallising in the context of the Vaishnava Deva dynasty of eastern Bengal (c. 1231–1294 CE), whose territorial heartland in Bikrampur/Munshiganj corresponds to the heaviest modern concentrations of Dey-bearing Vangaja Kayastha families.

2. **The Sena-era Kulin/Maulika system** (attested in kulagranthas from approximately the 15th–18th centuries, attributed retrospectively to Ballala Sena) places Dey among the elevated Maulika (*siddha*) lineages — the second tier of Bengali Kayastha social organisation.

3. **The Pala and Gupta epigraphic record** establishes the Kayastha community's scribal-administrative role from the 4th–5th centuries CE, creating the social conditions within which the Deva dynastic name could eventually crystallise as a heritable surname for administrative families.

4. **Mughal administrative records** (Ain-i-Akbari; qanungo records) confirm that Kayastha families, including those bearing Dey-type surnames, dominated revenue administration in Bengal, and that this role became hereditary by the 18th century.

5. **Colonial-era documentation** (Risley 1891; census records 1872–1931) systematised and, to some extent, distorted the social realities of the Dey community through the lens of racial ethnography and administrative caste-fixing. The varna-status controversy (Shudra vs. Kshatriya/upper-caste) imposed by colonial classification reflects British administrative imperatives more than organic social reality.

The Dey surname stands at the intersection of dynastic history, scribal administrative tradition, and colonial ethnography — each layer adding to and complicating its identity. A full resolution of remaining uncertainties will require: direct consultation of the Damodaradeva copper-plate texts, the relevant pages of Risley's *Tribes and Castes of Bengal*, accessible Bengal District Gazetteers, and archival records from Munshiganj and Dacca districts.

## 11B. Addendum (April 2026): Military History of Dasharatha Dev — New Sources

*This addendum incorporates material from Jyoishman Sarkar, "Vaishnav Deb Dynasty of Samatat," Kanjik.net (July 2025), which draws on Ziauddin Barani's* Tarikh-e-Firozshahi, *Marco Polo's* Travels *(Yule-Cordier ed.), Burma Gazetteer Akyab District Vol. A, Jogendra Nath Gupta's* History of Bikrampur *vol. I, and R.C. Majumdar's* The Delhi Sultanate *vol. 6.*

### 11B.1 Dasharatha Dev: The Warrior-King

Earlier scholarship available to this report (Phase 2A, April 2026) underemphasised the military and diplomatic achievements of **Dasharathadeva / Danauja Rai**, the fifth and greatest ruler of the Vaishnava Deva dynasty. The Kanjik article, drawing on a wider range of Bengali-language secondary sources and on Barani's *Tarikh-e-Firozshahi*, provides a substantially richer picture.

Dasharatha Dev's full regnal title — **Parameswara Parambhattarak Maharajadhiraja Ariraja-Danuja-Madhava** — translates roughly as: "Supreme Lord, Great Overlord, King of Kings, Enemy-King-Demon-Subduer devoted to Madhava (Vishnu)." It announces a ruler who claimed the highest possible sovereign titles and defined himself in explicitly Vaishnava religious terms. The Adabari copper plate confirms this: it depicts a four-armed Narayana in yoga posture bearing conch, discus, mace, and lotus — classic Vaishnava iconography.

**The Bengali verse** from Harimishra's *Kulkarika* is the most concise summary:
> *"prādurbhavat dharmātmā senavaṃśādanantavam | danujāmadhavaḥ sarva bhūpaiḥ sevyapadāmbujam"*
> ("The righteous Danujamādhava arose, beyond the lineage of the Senas, his lotus-feet worshipped by all kings")

### 11B.2 The Mongol Wars (~1245 and ~1276 CE)

Two separate Mongol contacts with Bengal are documented:

**Event 1 — 1245 CE (Minhaj-us-Siraj, *Tabaqat-i-Nasiri*)**: In Shawwal 642 AH (March 1245 CE), Mongol forces appeared at the gates of Lakhnauti (Gaur). The historian Minhaj-us-Siraj was personally present in the city. This predates the Deva dynasty's major period and relates to the Delhi Sultanate's control of Lakhnauti, not to Dasharatha Dev.

**Event 2 — ~1276–1279 CE (Marco Polo, *Travels*, Yule-Cordier ed. p. 174)**: A Mongol campaign involving approximately 12,000 horsemen against "Bengal and the Ming dynasty" (the latter = the Arakan Mrauk-U dynasty, loosely termed "Ming" by Polo). The Bengal side mustered some 2,000 soldiers but supplemented with war elephants — suggesting a mixed Bengali-Arakanese defensive coalition. The battle's outcome was inconclusive, though Marco Polo attributed nominal victory to Kublai Khan. The **Shan rulers of Burma** fought on the Mongol side.

**Burmese identification**: Dasharatha Dev is recorded in Burmese chronicles as **"Nga-pu-kin"** — a phonetic corruption of "Danuj" (the "-kin" element being a Burmese honorific suffix). This cross-cultural identification confirms that his military confrontation with the Mongols was sufficiently notable to enter Burmese historical memory. Source: Burma Gazetteer, Akyab District, Vol. A, p. 23.

### 11B.3 The Bangal-Delhi Treaty of 1281 CE

The most diplomatically significant episode of Dasharatha Dev's reign is documented in **Ziauddin Barani's *Tarikh-e-Firozshahi*** (a 14th-century primary source composed at the Delhi Sultanate court) and quoted in **R.C. Majumdar's *The Delhi Sultanate***, vol. 6.

**Background**: The Mamluk governor of Bihar-Lakhnauti, **Mughisuddin Tughral**, declared rebellion against Sultan **Ghiyasuddin Balban** in 1280 CE and seized Suvarnagram (Sonargaon). Both Balban and Dasharatha Dev were adversely affected by Tughral's rebellion — the classic "enemy's enemy" scenario.

**The Protocol Problem**: Balban sought to negotiate an anti-Tughral alliance with Dasharatha Dev. However, Dasharatha stipulated that Balban must *stand and salute* him before any treaty would be concluded — a demand that created a serious diplomatic impasse. For the Sultan of Delhi to formally salute a Hindu king would be theologically and politically untenable.

**The Solution**: A courtier named Malik Ikhtiyaruddin Nektar devised the famous stratagem: Balban would already be seated in court holding a falcon; as Dasharatha approached, Balban would rise and release the falcon — so observers would think he stood for the bird, not for the king. Barani's description:

> *"The Sultan sat on the throne with a falcon on his hand, and on the approach of Rai Danuj stood up and let the falcon go, so that the people would surmise that he left the throne only to set the bird in motion. The Muslim honour was saved by playing a trick upon the unsuspecting Hindu king."*
> — R.C. Majumdar, *The Delhi Sultanate*, vol. 6 (quoting Barani)

**Significance**: Majumdar notes that "the fact that such an incident was remembered and recorded nearly two hundred years later, by an eminent Muslim historian, speaks volumes about the way in which a Muslim Sultan was apt to regard even an independent Hindu king." Dasharatha Dev had sufficient political leverage to compel the Sultan of Delhi to engage in diplomatic theatre to avoid a formal slight — an extraordinary position for any Hindu king in 13th-century Bengal.

**Kulkarika reference**: Harimishra's *Kulkarika* also mentions a king "Keshava" who fled to Bengal from Delhi, taking refuge with Danujarimadhav. The Kulkarika verse:
> *"tasmat bhūtanayaḥ pracaṇḍa vinayaḥ śrī keśava-ākhyaḥ svayam | deśam cāpi vihāya vaṅgam agamaṃ bhītatvaraskan tataḥ | tantrāsiṃ danujārimādhavaḥ nṛpaḥ taṃ keśava bhūpati | sainyaiḥ viprāgaṇaiḥ pitāmaha-kṛtena raṇaiś ca yukta gataḥ"*

### 11B.4 Bogra Khan's Defeat — Sultanate Military Expedition Repulsed

Despite the 1281 treaty, Balban ordered his son **Prince Nasiruddin Bughra Khan** (also transliterated Bogra Khan) to conquer "Diyar-e-Bangalah" (دیار بنگلا — "the land of Bengal"). Dasharatha Dev defeated Bughra Khan's expedition and forced its retreat. Barani's *Tarikh-e-Firozshahi* records that Bughra Khan "was unable to carry out the Sultan's orders due to the resistance of Danujmadhav, the Rai (King) of Bengal." [WELL-ESTABLISHED primary source]

This episode establishes that the Deva kingdom under Dasharatha Dev was capable of defeating a full Sultanate military expedition — not merely negotiating from weakness.

### 11B.5 The 1294 Suvarnagram Victory

In 1283 CE, Mughisuddin Tughral seized Suvarnagram (Sonargaon) during the Sen dynasty's civil war. Dasharatha Dev then planned to recover it by exploiting the subsequent civil war between Shamsuddin Feroze Shah and Rukunuddin Kaus over control of Bihar.

**The Alliance**: Dasharatha Dev entered a formal naval alliance with Arakan's ruling king **Ming-Hette** (မင်းထက်). The combined Bengal-Arakan naval force attacked the Sultanate's Sonargaon position:
- Arakan general **Raza-thin-gyan** led the assault on Sonargaon
- Bengal forces built defensive fortresses on either side of the city
- The combined fleet attacked from the south, along the Ganga-Brahmaputra delta system

**Outcome**: The Sultanate forces accepted defeat. The boundary of Delhi Sultanate authority was pushed back to the western bank of the Karatoa river — effectively conceding all of eastern Bengal to Deva-Arakan control. [WELL-ESTABLISHED via Rajnikanth Chakrabarty, *Gaudher Itihas*; Jogendra Nath Gupta, *History of Vikrampur* vol. I; Burma Gazetteer Akyab]

### 11B.6 The Final Fall and Chandradwip Transition (1317–1318 CE)

**Shamsuddin Feroze Shah** and **Bahadur Shah** retook Sonargaon in **1317 CE**, using the full resources of the Bengal Sultanate. This was the end of Deva power at the centre of Bengal.

In **1318 CE**, **Vikramaditya Dev** — documented in the Kanjik article as Dasharatha Dev's *brother* — shifted the capital southward to **Chandradwip** (Kachua, modern Barisal Division). Vikramaditya took the regnal title **Danujmardan Deb** — "the Deb who crushes the demons," echoing his brother Dasharatha's "Danujamadhav" epithet and maintaining the dynasty's self-identification as demon-slayers in the Vaishnava tradition.

This founding act created the **Chandradwip Deb dynasty** that served as *samaj-pati* (community president) of all Bangaja Kayasthas for 17 generations — the institution that maintained the Kayastha social classification system (including the De/Dey surname's Mahapatra status) throughout the Sultanate and early Mughal periods.

**Note on "Danujmardan"**: Earlier versions of this report and the MEMORY file dated Danujmardan Deb to "c. 1280 CE." The Kanjik article's evidence — citing Jogendra Nath Gupta and the *Gaudher Itihas* — places the Chandradwip foundation definitively in 1318 CE. The earlier date appears to be a conflation with the beginning of the Bikrampur dynasty's main period.
