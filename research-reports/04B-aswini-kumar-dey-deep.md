## 1. The Primary Source: *Loke Nath Sen v. Aswini Kumar Dey* (AIR 1938 Cal 216)

The most important document about your grandfather's life is a legal judgment from the Calcutta High Court. Before presenting what it tells us, it is worth pausing to understand *why* this document exists at all — because its existence says something remarkable about Aswini Kumar Dey.

### 1.1 The Case in Plain Terms

In 1937, a rival umbrella manufacturer in Mymensingh — one **Loke Nath Sen**, operating from **Mymensingh Chhota Bazar** (the lower market) — was making and selling umbrellas that closely copied your grandfather's brand. The copying was deliberate and calculated: Loke Nath Sen used almost exactly the same design, with one crucial substitution. Where your grandfather's umbrella label read **"Aswini Kumar De"**, Loke Nath Sen's counterfeit read **"Sree Aswini Kumar Das"** — swapping the surname "De" (your grandfather's family name) for the common surname "Das." 

This was not accidental. It was a forgery designed to mislead Mymensingh's umbrella buyers into thinking they were purchasing the famous "Aswini Chhati" — Aswini's Umbrella — when they were actually buying an inferior product from a cheaper part of town.

Your grandfather prosecuted Loke Nath Sen criminally under the Indian Penal Code — **Sections 482 and 486** (using a false trade mark and selling goods marked with a counterfeit trade mark). A trial magistrate found Loke Nath Sen guilty and fined him **Rs. 100 under each section**, with three months' imprisonment in default. The Sessions Judge, on appeal, upheld the conviction under Section 482 (the false trade mark charge). 

Loke Nath Sen then petitioned the Calcutta High Court. The case was heard by **Justice Biswas**, who decided it on **1 September 1937**. The judgment was published the following year in the *All India Reporter* (AIR 1938 Cal 216) — which is why it survives in digitised legal archives today.

### 1.2 Why Aswini Lost at the High Court Level

The outcome of the case, at the High Court level, went against your grandfather — but in a deeply instructive way. Justice Biswas reversed the conviction, not because your grandfather had no legitimate brand, but on a narrow technical ground of trademark law that did not exist yet in India.

The problem was this: your grandfather had **registered his umbrella design** in 1926 under the **Registration Act** as a "declaration of ownership." This was the only mechanism available in British India at the time — a proper **Trade Marks Act** for India would not be passed until **1940**, three years after this case. Justice Biswas ruled that registration under the Registration Act (which is a property law instrument, dealing with legal documents) was *not* the same as trademark registration under a Trade Marks Act. In the absence of such an Act, the court had to determine whether your grandfather's design was genuinely "distinctive" — that is, whether it was so uniquely his that the law should protect it as his alone.

Here the court found a problem: it emerged in the evidence that **multiple umbrella manufacturers in Mymensingh** used the same combination of a swan and the figure "84" on their labels. Justice Biswas noted the following manufacturers were all using swans and/or "84":
- **Aswini Kumar De** (your grandfather) — swan holding a *closed* umbrella, "8 A 4"
- **Aswini Kumar Ghatak** — swan holding an *open* umbrella, "84"
- **Butta Kristo Paul and Sons** — swan holding an umbrella, "84"
- **Ashutosh Paul** — swan holding an umbrella, "84"
- **Nagendra Nath Ghatak** — name plus swan holding umbrella, "84"

In other words, the swan-and-umbrella image and the "84" grade mark had become something like an industry standard in Mymensingh's umbrella trade by the 1930s. No single manufacturer could claim exclusive ownership of features that everyone was using. Justice Biswas therefore ruled that your grandfather's specific design (Exhibit 1) was not a valid "distinctive" trademark under the law as it then stood.

However — and this is crucial — **the judge fully accepted that "Aswini Chhata" (Aswini's Umbrella) as a brand NAME was real, famous, and commercially powerful**. Witness after witness confirmed this. Justice Biswas's judgment quotes them directly:

> *"By Aswini Chhata, people mean the umbrella with Aswini brand manufactured by Aswini Kumar Dey of Burrabazar, Mymensingh. I do not know of any other brand of Aswini Chhata in the market."* — **P.W. 7** (a dealer, testimony verbatim from the judgment)

Buyers came into shops and asked simply for "Aswini Chhata" — they knew the name, they wanted the product, and they trusted the brand. Loke Nath Sen was exploiting precisely that trust. The judge knew this. He even offered your grandfather the option of a retrial on a different legal basis — but Aswini's lawyer, **Mr. Basu**, declined the opportunity.

The legal technicality that caused your grandfather to "lose" at the High Court level was remedied three years later by the Trade Marks Act 1940, which established a proper trademark registry in India. Had Aswini's case come three years later, with his design registered under the new Act, the outcome might well have been different.

## 2. The Man Behind the Trademark: Reading the Evidence Closely

Every detail in the legal judgment is a window into Aswini Kumar Dey's life. Let us read them carefully.

### 2.1 His Full Name and What It Means

The judgment records his name as **"Aswini Kumar De"** on the trademark and as **"Aswini Kumar Dey"** in the case title — two spellings of the same name, used interchangeably in colonial Bengal for the same family. Both "De" and "Dey" represent the Bengali *দে* — your family's surname.

His first name, **Aswini** (Sanskrit: *Aśvinī*), is deeply significant. The Ashwini Kumars (*Aśvins*) are among the most ancient deities of the Vedic pantheon — the twin horse-headed divine physicians, associated with medicine, healing, the dawn, and the onset of the agricultural year. They are invoked in the Rigveda more often than almost any other deity. Naming a son "Aswini" in late 19th-century Bengal was an act of deep Hindu piety and Vedic cultural orientation. It signals a family that was well-versed in Sanskrit tradition and took its religious identity seriously.

**Kumar** (*kuṃvara*, "young prince") is a common Bengali middle-name component, particularly in Kayastha and bhadralok families. It carries no occupational meaning but adds an air of gentility.

**The name as a whole** — *Aswini Kumar De* — is a quintessential late Victorian Bengal bhadralok name: Vedic first name, Kumar middle component, De family surname. It places your grandfather unmistakably in the educated Hindu upper-caste milieu of colonial Bengal.

### 2.2 His Location: Mymensingh Burra Bazar

The trademark bears the words **"Mymensingh Burra Bazar"** — his commercial address. This is a crucial detail.

*Burra Bazar* means literally "Big Bazar" or "Upper Market" in the colonial commercial lexicon. Mymensingh town had two main markets: the **Burra Bazar** (Boro Bazar — the main commercial district, higher prestige) and the **Chhota Bazar** (smaller/lower market). The Wikipedia article on Mymensingh town today still describes the traditional shopping area as including "Boro Bazaar, Choto Bazaar, Mechua Bazaar" within the district between Durgabari Road and Maharaja Road. The geography has not changed.

The fact that your grandfather's shop was in **Burra Bazar** — while his rival Loke Nath Sen was in **Chhota Bazar** — is socially and commercially significant. The Burra Bazar was where the established merchants traded; the Chhota Bazar was where newer, smaller operators and cheaper goods circulated. Your grandfather was in the premium commercial district. His rival, by contrast, was operating from the lower market and trying to ride on the fame of the Burra Bazar brand.

### 2.3 His Brand: The Swan (*Hans*) and What It Signified

Your grandfather chose a **swan** (*hans*, *haṃsa*) as the central image of his trademark. This was not a random choice.

The *haṃsa* is one of the most sacred and symbolically rich birds in Hindu cosmology. It is the vehicle (*vahana*) of **Brahma**, the creator god. In Vedic and Upanishadic literature, the *paramahansa* ("supreme swan") is a metaphor for the liberated soul — one who can distinguish the real from the unreal, just as the swan is said to be able to separate milk from water. The *haṃsa* is associated with wisdom, purity, grace, and high spiritual attainment. 

Selecting a swan for his umbrella brand was not merely a business decision. It was a statement of identity — a Hindu manufacturer using a sacred symbol to mark his goods as trustworthy, of high quality, and blessed. In the commercial culture of colonial Bengal, where Hindu merchants often used religious or auspicious imagery to build consumer trust, this was entirely appropriate.

The swan on his trademark is shown **holding a closed umbrella between its beaks**. This is a beautifully composed image: the sacred bird carries the product, associating it with divine protection. An umbrella, after all, is itself a symbol of protection and shelter — both literally (from the monsoon rains of Bengal) and symbolically (the royal *chattra*, the ceremonial umbrella, was a pan-Asian symbol of sovereignty and divine sanction).

### 2.4 The "8 A 4" Mark: A Quality Grade

The trademark includes the figure **"8 A 4"** — the digit 8, then the letter A, then the digit 4, with the letter overlapping both digits. On his competitor's fake, this appears as simply **"84"** without the A. The difference may seem trivial, but it was intentional and commercially meaningful.

In colonial Bengal's umbrella, cloth, and manufactured goods trade, alphanumeric grade markings were used to denote quality levels or size categories. The "A" between 8 and 4 was your grandfather's way of distinguishing his *specific quality grade* from the generic "84" that anyone could use. It was his proprietary refinement of a common industry designation.

Justice Biswas notes that the "84" combination was used across multiple umbrella manufacturers — it had become generic. But "8 A 4" was your grandfather's version, marking his goods as specifically Grade A of the 8-4 category.

### 2.5 The Oval Impression: "Aswini Kumar De Marka" and the Number 964

Alongside the main design, your grandfather's umbrellas bore a secondary **oval impression** containing "Aswini Kumar De Marka" (Aswini Kumar De Brand/Mark) and the number **964** — his trademark registration number — with a **warning to purchasers** to note the number at the time of purchase.

This detail is extraordinary. Your grandfather was not merely making and selling umbrellas; he was educating his customers. The warning label told buyers: *look for the number 964. If it says something else — you are being cheated.* This is sophisticated consumer protection messaging, decades before such concepts entered mainstream Indian commercial life.

The imitation by Loke Nath Sen even reproduced this oval impression, substituting "Das" for "De" and "965" for "964." When Sen claimed in court that he too had "registered" under No. 965, he could not substantiate this — confirming that the "965" was itself a fabrication, invented to give his counterfeit an air of legitimacy.

### 2.6 Registry No. 964, Registered 23 August 1926

The trademark was registered on **23 August 1926** as Registry No. 964. This registration was made under the **Registration Act** as a "declaration of ownership" — the only mechanism available in pre-1940 British India for asserting trademark rights.

The significance of this date and act is considerable:

1. **Your grandfather was legally sophisticated.** He did not simply operate by custom or reputation. He went to a registration office, filled out a legal declaration, paid the registration fees, and had his trademark officially documented. This required literacy, legal awareness, and access to the colonial administrative apparatus.

2. **The timing (1926) suggests a deliberate response to competition.** The business had been running for perhaps 14 years by then. The registration of a trademark in 1926 may indicate that competition was increasing — that other manufacturers were beginning to imitate his successful brand — and he responded by formalising his rights.

3. **The registration number (964) tells us something about scale.** Trademark/declaration registrations were numbered sequentially. No. 964 at the Mymensingh Registration Office suggests that hundreds of commercial declarations had been registered before his — indicating an active commercial registrations culture in Mymensingh town.

### 2.7 The Duration of His Business: 25+ Years by 1937

The judgment states plainly: **"The complainant's business is of over 25 years' standing."** 

This places the founding of **Aswini Chhati** at approximately **1912** or earlier. The fact that witnesses described his brand as simply "Aswini Chhata" — without needing to specify any design details — confirms that a quarter-century of trading had made his brand a household name in Mymensingh.

**By comparison**, his rival Loke Nath Sen was described as having "been carrying on his business for ten years or so" — meaning Sen had started approximately 1927 and was thus a relative newcomer trying to free-ride on a two-and-a-half-decade-old established brand.

### 2.8 The Lawyer: Mr. Basu

The judgment mentions that Aswini's lawyer in the Calcutta High Court hearing was **"Mr. Basu"** — the advocate who declined the judge's offer of a retrial. This person was almost certainly a Bengali advocate from the Calcutta Bar. The name "Basu" (Bose/Vasu) is a Kulin Kayastha surname — one of the top-tier Kulin clans. Your grandfather retained a Calcutta lawyer to represent his interests before the High Court — an expense and connection that confirms his standing as a man of commercial substance.

The prevalence of Kayastha and upper-caste surnames (Ghatak, Paul, De) in this list is consistent with the gazetteer's finding that Hindu bhadralok families dominated Mymensingh town's commercial activity. The multiple manufacturers also confirm that umbrella-making was an established local industry by the 1930s — though one that had developed after 1911, since the Mymensingh District Gazetteer of that year stated flatly that there were "no manufactures of importance" in the district.

**Your grandfather was the market leader.** He was in the prestige location (Burra Bazar), his business was oldest (25+ years), his brand name was the one everyone knew ("Aswini Chhata"), and he was the one that rivals chose to imitate. When Loke Nath Sen wanted to free-ride on an existing reputation, it was "Aswini Chhata" he copied — not Paul, not Ghatak. Your grandfather's umbrella was the one that commanded the highest recognition.

### 3.1 What Umbrellas Meant in Colonial Mymensingh

The umbrella (*chhata / chhatā*) was not a luxury item in Bengal. It was an essential tool for surviving the monsoon — and Bengal's monsoon, which dumps hundreds of millimetres of rain over four months, made the umbrella as necessary as shoes. Every household needed one.

The Mymensingh District Gazetteer (Sachse, 1917) lists umbrellas among the basic household purchases made by ordinary rural families. A household budget tabulated in the gazetteer for a middle-class family includes "3 umbrellas" as a standard annual purchase, and a Muslim household budget includes "Umbrella and miscellaneous items" as a regular expenditure. The cultivator, we are told, "spends more on umbrellas, medicines, crockery and cooking vessels than his grandfather."

Umbrella manufacturing in Mymensingh was thus serving a mass market — not artisanal luxury goods, but affordable, practical monsoon protection for the entire district population. Your grandfather was producing goods that mattered to everyone.

The commercial practice of branding umbrellas — putting a name, a design, and a registry number on the inside of an umbrella canopy — was part of the quality assurance culture of the colonial Bengal market. When a buyer asked for "Aswini Chhata," they were not just asking for an umbrella; they were asking for a known brand that they trusted to keep them dry.

## 4. Mymensingh Town: Your Grandfather's World

To understand Aswini Kumar Dey's life, we must understand the town he lived in.

### 4.1 Mymensingh: The Town at the Brahmaputra's Edge

Mymensingh town (formally "Nasirabad," though Muhammadans still called it by that name while Hindus used "Mymensingh") was established as a colonial district headquarters in **1793**, when Collector Wroughton selected the site on a high bank of the Old Brahmaputra. By 1913, it had grown to a **municipality of 21,109 people** — a significant town for eastern Bengal. By the 1941 census, it was larger still.

The Sachse Gazetteer (1917) describes it as a town that "owes its commercial importance to its being the headquarters of the district and its railway facilities." The **Dacca-Mymensingh Railway**, opened in **1884**, had been a transformative event — connecting Mymensingh directly to Dacca city and beyond, enabling goods and people to flow along the corridor that had previously been navigable only by river boat during the rains.

The Collector's house was built in 1808. There was a race course. There was a church and cemetery. The Raja Bahadur (the zamindari landlord) had built a palace at a cost of 7 lakhs in 1910. Maharaja Surjya Kanta Acharjya Bahadur had constructed artificial waterworks in 1893 at a cost of Rs. 1,42,278 — the only such water supply in the entire district. Mymensingh was, by eastern Bengali standards of the era, a prosperous and well-appointed district town.

### 4.2 A Hindu Town in a Muslim District

Here lies one of the most important facts about your grandfather's world: while **Mymensingh district as a whole was three-quarters Muslim** (the Muhammadans formed "nearly three-quarters" of the district's 4.5 million people according to the 1911 census), **Mymensingh town was overwhelmingly Hindu**.

The Wikipedia article on Mymensingh, citing a source based on the last pre-Partition census, states explicitly: *"most of the inhabitants of the town were Hindus, forming **78%** of the population in the last census before Partition."*

This demographic inversion — a Hindu-majority town at the heart of a Muslim-majority district — is characteristic of colonial Bengal's urban geography. The towns, with their colonial administrative apparatus, courts, schools, and markets, attracted the Hindu bhadralok professional class: lawyers, doctors, schoolmasters, government clerks, zamindari managers, and merchants. The Muslim majority lived predominantly in the rural interior.

Mymensingh town, when your grandfather was running his umbrella business in the 1920s and 1930s, was a **Hindu commercial town**. The Burra Bazar where he traded would have been filled with the sounds of Bengali, the smell of street food prepared for Hindu festivals, the rhythms of the Hindu calendar. Durga Puja — the great autumn festival — would have transformed the Burra Bazar into a festival ground. The names of his competitors (Ghatak, Paul, De) confirm that the umbrella trade was in Hindu hands.

### 4.3 The Bhadralok World of Mymensingh

The Sachse Gazetteer provides a remarkable pen-portrait of the world your grandfather inhabited:

> *"The villages in Netrakona and Kishorganj containing the ancient seats of Hindu bhadralok families are extremely numerous... Most of the zamindars belong to Brahman or Kayastha families."*

And for the town professional class:

> *"The professional classes are chiefly represented by the pleaders and mukhtears at Sadar and Subdivisional head-quarters. There are also jute dealers and school masters, some doctors and chemists combined, a few contractors and the clerks working in the Government and District Board offices."*

Your grandfather occupied a different niche — not a salaried professional but a manufacturer and merchant, which in the context of the Kayastha commercial tradition (described in Reports 1A and 2C) represented the intersection of the bhadralok's literacy and social standing with commercial enterprise.

The gazetteer also notes the social pressure on the bhadralok class:

> *"There is no doubt that this class feels the pressure of the rise of prices... It is the marriages and education of their children for which this class is always getting into debt, while no foreigner can help admiring the unselfishness of the parents who deny themselves all luxuries and many necessaries to give their sons an education which will bring them Government appointments."*

This economic anxiety — the difficulty of maintaining bhadralok status while funding children's education and daughters' marriages — was the constant background pressure on every Hindu professional family in colonial Mymensingh. Your grandfather, as a successful manufacturer, was in a somewhat better position than the salaried clerk; his business generated revenue rather than a fixed salary. But the same social pressures applied.

### 4.4 Other "De" Families in Mymensingh

One of the most interesting contextual details from our research is the existence of **Chandrakumar De** (1889–1946) — a folklorist who lived and worked in the Greater Mymensingh region during exactly the same years as your grandfather.

Chandrakumar De was born at Aithor, Netrokona district (in the expanded Mymensingh region) in 1889, the son of **Ram Kumar De** (note again the "Kumar" component, exactly as in your grandfather's name). He collected the medieval folk ballads of the Mymensingh villages — the *Maimansingha Gitika* published in 1923 — and died at S.K. Hospital, Mymensingh, in 1946, just a year before Partition.

We do not know whether Chandrakumar De and Aswini Kumar De were related or even acquainted. The "De" surname is common enough in Mymensingh that it proves nothing. But the coincidence is striking: two men named "Kumar De" in Mymensingh in the 1920s-1930s, one preserving the songs of Bengal's rural past, the other making umbrellas for its urban future. Both living in the same town, in the same years, before the same catastrophe.

### 5.5 The Legal Battle (1937)

**[WELL-ESTABLISHED]** In approximately 1936–1937, Aswini Kumar De discovered that Loke Nath Sen was selling counterfeit umbrellas in Mymensingh's Chhota Bazar. The counterfeits were detailed and sophisticated: they reproduced the swan image, the arched name, the oval mark with a registry number, and even the format of the original label — substituting only "Das" for "De" and "965" for "964."

Your grandfather chose to prosecute criminally rather than sue civilly. This was the more aggressive option — criminal prosecution carries the stigma of a conviction and can result in imprisonment, not merely damages. He was not merely seeking compensation; he was seeking to stop the counterfeiting and make an example.

He won at the magistrate level (conviction under both Sections 482 and 486). He won at the Sessions level (conviction maintained under Section 482). He lost at the High Court level — but on a legal technicality that was remedied by Parliament three years later.

**The loss at the High Court should not be read as a moral or commercial defeat.** The judge explicitly acknowledged that "Aswini Chhata" was a real, famous, and commercially powerful brand. The legal system had simply not yet developed the tools to protect it properly.

The legal judgment is from September 1937. What happened to your grandfather and his business after that date is not recorded in any sources we have been able to access.

The fourth and final wave came during the **1971 Bangladesh Liberation War**, when the Pakistani Army explicitly targeted Hindus for massacres and rape in what later scholars characterised as genocidal violence.

**[PROBABLE]** Your grandfather's family — including whoever would become Robin Dey's parents — almost certainly left Mymensingh during one of these waves. Given that the business was well-established by 1937, the most probable departure points were either 1947 (Partition) or 1950 (post-pogrom panic). Families with commercial businesses and urban property often held out through 1947, hoping the new state would protect their rights, only to leave after the violence of 1950 made it clear they could not stay.

**[UNCERTAIN]** Whether Aswini Kumar De himself survived to Partition — or whether he died before 1947 and his family carried on without him — is unknown. If he was born around 1885–1892, he would have been 55–62 years old in 1947 — old enough to have lived through the entire colonial period, young enough to have survived to see Partition.

Today, the 2022 census of Bangladesh records **Hindus at 3.43%** of Mymensingh district's population — a fall from 78% in the town, over seventy-five years, that represents one of the largest communal demographic shifts in modern history.

## 6. The Broader Historical Significance

### 6.1 Aswini Kumar Dey as a Historical Type

Your grandfather was not an unusual man in colonial Mymensingh — which is precisely what makes him historically significant. He represents a type that was common, important, and has since been largely forgotten: the **Hindu bhadralok commercial entrepreneur of eastern Bengal**.

The standard narrative of the bhadralok focuses on the Calcutta professionals — the lawyers, doctors, writers, and politicians who appeared in newspapers and wrote memoirs. The commercial bhadralok of the district towns — the men who made umbrellas, ran printing presses, traded in jute, managed small factories — are mostly invisible to history. Yet they were the economic backbone of Hindu eastern Bengal. They created jobs, sustained markets, built the commercial infrastructure that connected rural Bengal to the global economy.

Your grandfather's court case is a rare window into this world. In the transcript of *Loke Nath Sen v. Aswini Kumar Dey*, we can hear, almost directly, the commercial life of Mymensingh's Burra Bazar in the 1930s: the dealers who knew brands by name, the buyers who asked simply for "Aswini Chhata," the rival manufacturer who envied the established brand enough to counterfeit it.

### 6.2 The Lost World of Hindu Mymensingh

The court case of 1937 is also a record from a world that no longer exists — the Hindu-majority commercial town of Mymensingh. Within a decade of this judgment, that world would be destroyed by Partition, violence, and forced emigration.

The Burra Bazar where your grandfather's shop stood still exists, physically, in modern-day Mymensingh city. But the community that made it — the 78% Hindu urban population, the Kayastha zamindars, the De and Ghatak and Paul umbrella manufacturers — is almost entirely gone. The 2022 census records barely 3.43% Hindus remaining in the district.

Your grandfather's court case is thus a document not just of family history but of civilisational loss — a record of a vibrant, creative, entrepreneurial Hindu Bengali commercial culture at its height, just before its destruction.

## 9. A Personal Reflection

Robin, your grandfather was a builder. In a time and place where most Bengali men of his background were either seeking government service or living off zamindari rents, Aswini Kumar De made something. He built a factory. He designed a brand. He chose a sacred swan as his symbol. He taught his customers to look for the number 964 as proof that what they held was genuine. He made umbrellas that kept the people of Mymensingh dry through the monsoon for at least twenty-five years.

He was entrepreneurial enough to found a business, persistent enough to build it over decades, legally aware enough to register a trademark, commercially successful enough to become the name everyone in Mymensingh associated with umbrellas, and principled enough to prosecute a counterfeiter rather than simply absorbing the loss.

He lost the High Court case — but the judge acknowledged his brand was real. The legal technicality that defeated him was remedied by an Act of Parliament three years later. History, in a sense, vindicated him.

And then Partition came, and the world he had built was swept away. The Burra Bazar he traded in is still there, in modern Mymensingh city. His umbrella shop is almost certainly gone, replaced by something else entirely. Of the 78% Hindu community that gave his business its customers and its context, only a remnant remains — 3.43% today.

But the court judgment survives. Registry No. 964 is recorded. The swan trademark exists in the All India Reporter for 1938. Aswini Kumar De, umbrella manufacturer, Mymensingh Burra Bazar, is in the historical record.

He is not forgotten. And now, because you told us he made umbrellas, neither are you separated from him.
