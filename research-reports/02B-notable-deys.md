## Abstract

The surname De/Dey (Bengali: দে) has produced an exceptional number of historically significant figures across Bengali cultural, commercial, intellectual, and artistic life. This report catalogues and analyses notable individuals bearing the Dey/De surname, covering the period from the late eighteenth century through the early twenty-first century. The biographies assembled here reveal a consistent pattern: Dey-surnamed individuals frequently appear at sites of cultural and social transformation — the Bengal Renaissance, the Swadeshi movement, the emergence of Indian cinema, the modernist literary movement, and post-Independence sports culture. The analysis supports the hypothesis that the Dey surname, while spanning multiple jati communities, operated primarily within a Kayastha social milieu defined by literacy, administration, and cultural production.

## 1. Introduction: The Dey Surname in Kulagranthas and Historical Sources

Before cataloguing individuals, it is necessary to situate the Dey surname within its documented social history. The Dey/De surname appears in Bengali kulagranthas (written caste genealogies) as one of the foundational Kayastha surnames. A 2017 analysis of kulagrantha records published in the *Economic and Political Weekly* (Bose, vol. 52, no. 47, 2017) found that of the 99 Kayastha surnames listed in surviving genealogies, twelve were classified as *siddha* (blessed/elevated status): Bose, Ghosh, Guha, Mitra, Datta, Nag, Nath, Das, **Dey**, Sen, Palit, and Sinha. This places Dey among the twelve most socially recognized Kayastha surnames, though — critically — Dey falls into the *Maulika* (original/local) tier rather than the Kulin (highest-ranked) tier comprising Bose, Ghosh, Mitra, Guha, and Datta.

Contemporary sources confirm that De (दे / দে) is "a native Bengali surname commonly used by the Bengali Hindu community of India and Bangladesh" associated "mainly with Bengali Kayasthas, but also found among Suvarna Banik, Teli, Tili Barujibi, Kansari, Tambuli, Baishya Kapali, Tanti" and other communities (*De (surname)*, Wikipedia). This cross-caste distribution is analytically significant: as the biographies below demonstrate, notable Dey figures have emerged from Kayastha, Suvarna Banik, and Vaishya backgrounds, indicating that the surname functioned across multiple social registers.

## 2. Pre-Colonial Dey Figures: The Deva Dynasty and Mughal-Era Records

### 2.1 The Deva Dynasty Connection [UNCERTAIN]

The most compelling pre-colonial connection to the Dey/De surname is the **Deva dynasty** of Bikrampur (Munshiganj District, present-day Bangladesh), which ruled eastern Bengal from approximately 1231 to 1294 CE. The Devas are documented primarily through copper plate inscriptions and were succeeded by the Sultanate of Bengal following the Islamic conquest of the region. Contemporary genealogical scholarship (e.g., momjunction.com, citing standard genealogical sources) explicitly states that "the surname De/Dey originates from 'Deva/Dey,' the name of a Hindu dynasty which ruled over Eastern Bengal with Bikrampur as their capital" and that "the surname is now common among Bengali Kayasthas."

It is important to note that no peer-reviewed study has definitively traced *individual* present-day Dey families back to the Deva ruling clan. The connection may reflect a commemorative adoption of the dynasty's name by administrative-class families in the region following its collapse — a common pattern in Bengali genealogy wherein the surname honors a prestigious but extinct polity. The coincidence of Bikrampur (the Deva capital) with the birthplace of multiple notable Dey figures (most strikingly Mukunda Das/Yajneshwar De, see §5 below) is suggestive, though not probative without documentary lineage evidence.

### 2.2 Mughal-Era Records [UNCERTAIN]

The Mughal administrative apparatus (Bengal Subah, 1576–1757) employed large numbers of Bengali Kayastha scribes, revenue collectors, and *munims* (accountants). The presence of De/Dey families in this apparatus is implied by the social position of the community but has not been documented with specific named individuals in any primary source available for this study. The Mughal *ain* (administrative records) occasionally preserve individual Kayastha names, but no systematic extraction of Dey-surnamed individuals from these sources has been published in secondary literature. This represents a gap warranting archival research at the West Bengal State Archives and the Bangladesh National Archives.

## 3. The Colonial Period (1757–1947): Renaissance, Commerce, and Independence

### 3.1 Ramdulal Dey, *alias* Ramdulal Sarkar (1752–1825): The Merchant Prince [WELL-ESTABLISHED]

**The most spectacular commercial figure to bear the Dey surname**, Ramdulal Dey is routinely identified as the first Bengali millionaire with international trading connections. He is known also as "Ramdulal Sarkar" and "Ramdulal De Sarkar," the title *Sarkar* being an honorific denoting a managerial or scribal role.

**Origins and early life**: Born in 1752 in the village of Rekjani near Dum Dum (site of the present-day international airport, West Bengal), Ramdulal came from a family of modest means. His maternal grandfather, Ramsundar Biswas, brought him to Calcutta, where he found employment in the household of the wealthy merchant Madan Mohan Dutta of the Hatkhola Dutta family, who served as the *Dewan* of Export Warehouses. Sources differ on whether his father was named "Balaram Sarkar" (Wikipedia) or whether "Balram Sarkar" was a separate teacher (Banglapedia). [UNCERTAIN] He received informal education alongside the Dutta household's sons and trained in accounting (*Dey, Ramdulal*, Banglapedia, 2023 edition; *Ramdulal Sarkar*, Wikipedia).

**The windfall and the fortune**: Ramdulal rose from the position of *bill sarkar* (Rs. 5/month) to *ship sarkar* (Rs. 10/month), supervising the loading and unloading of cargo at Diamond Harbour. The turning point came when, attending an auction on behalf of his employer, he purchased a condemned ship wreck on the Hooghly River for Rs. 14,000, then promptly resold it to an Englishman for Rs. 100,000. His employer Madan Mohan Dutta, recognizing the windfall, returned the profit to Ramdulal saying "Ramdoolal, the money is yours." This capital became the basis of his subsequent commercial empire.

**American connections**: From approximately 1790, Ramdulal became the *banian* (local agent and commercial intermediary) for American merchant houses from Salem, New York, Philadelphia, and Boston — cities then competing with the British East India Company for Bengal's trade. His American partners, grateful for his expertise, honoured him in remarkable ways: in 1801, twenty-two American merchants presented him with a life-sized portrait of George Washington by William Winstanley (one of the earliest such portraits). One of their trading ships was named *Ram Dolloll* in his honour. Upon his death in 1825, a formal condolence resolution was recorded in the minutes of the East India Marine Society at Salem, Massachusetts, and letters of condolence arrived from merchant houses in Boston, Salem, New York, Philadelphia, Newburyport, and Marblehead (Bean, Susan S., 1990, *Calcutta Banians for the American Trade*, Peabody Museum of Salem).

**Philanthropy and social standing**: Ramdulal was among the largest individual donors to the founding of Hindu College, Calcutta (established 1817), contributing the highest personal amount of any subscriber, and supported numerous charitable and educational institutions. He died on 1 April 1825. His sons Asutosh De ("Chatu Babu") and Pramathanath De ("Latu Babu") became famous for their patronage of classical music and their lavish lifestyle. Their house, *Ramdulal Nibas* or Chatu Babu Latu Babu Rajbari at 67E Beadon Street, North Kolkata, remains extant.

**Caste note**: Sources do not explicitly state Ramdulal Dey's jati affiliation. The use of both "Dey" and "Sarkar" as surnames, his employment in a scribal-mercantile role, and his North Calcutta social milieu suggest Kayastha origin, though some researchers have categorized him within the Vaishya-commercial sphere. [UNCERTAIN]

**Assessment**: Ramdulal Dey represents a Dey-surnamed individual who achieved extraordinary economic and social ascent entirely within the colonial commercial system, prefiguring the Bengali entrepreneurial class by at least two generations.

### 3.2 Lal Behari Dey (1824–1892/1894): Evangelist, Novelist, Folklorist [WELL-ESTABLISHED]

**Full name**: Lal Behari Dey Mondal (surname sometimes anglicized as "Day")  
**Born**: 18 December 1824, Sonapalasi, near Bardhaman, Bengal Presidency  
**Died**: 28 October 1892 or 1894 (sources differ) [UNCERTAIN], Calcutta  
**Caste**: **Suvarna Banik** (gold/silver merchant community; Vaishya varna)

Lal Behari Dey occupies a unique position in this catalogue: he is the most prominent Dey-surnamed figure confirmed to belong to the **Suvarna Banik** mercantile community rather than the Kayastha scribal class, demonstrating the surname's cross-caste reach. His father, Radhakanta Dey Mondal, was a small bill broker in Calcutta (Roy, Samaren, 1999, *The Bengalees: Glimpses of History and Culture*, Allied Publishers, p. 59).

**Conversion and education**: Admitted to Reverend Alexander Duff's General Assembly Institution in Calcutta in 1834, Lal Behari was among the first five students enrolled. Under Duff's influence, he formally converted to Christianity on 2 July 1843, having already published a prize-winning anti-Hindu polemic *The Falsity of the Hindu Religion* in 1842. This conversion was an act of considerable personal courage given the social penalties involved.

**Career**: From 1855 to 1867, Lal Behari served as a missionary and minister of the Free Church of Scotland. He subsequently transitioned to academic life, serving as Professor of English and Mental and Moral Philosophy at Hooghly Mohsin College (University of Calcutta affiliation, 1872–1888). He was made a Fellow of the University of Calcutta in 1877.

**Literary contributions**: Two works established Lal Behari Dey as a significant figure in Indo-English literature and the study of rural Bengal:

1. *Govinda Samanta: or the History of a Bengal Raiyat* (1874) — winner of the prize offered by zamindar Joy Kissen Mookerjea of Uttarpara for the best novel depicting the rural poor; Charles Darwin wrote to the publishers on 18 April 1881 praising the novel.
2. *Folk-Tales of Bengal: Life's Secret* (1883/1875) — one of the earliest systematic collections of Bengali folklore in English, preserving oral traditions that might otherwise have been lost; considered a foundational text in Bengali folk literature studies.

Lal Behari also edited three English-language periodicals (*Indian Reformer*, 1861; *Friday Review*, 1866; *Bengal Magazine*, 1872) and contributed to the *Calcutta Review* and *Hindu Patriot*.

**Social significance**: As an opponent of the zamindari system, Lal Behari Dey's writings on peasant conditions contributed to the intellectual environment that produced the Bengal Tenancy Act of 1885, described by contemporaries as the "Magna Carta of peasant rights in Bengal" (Wikipedia, *Lal Behari Day*).

**Bibliographic note**: The standard biography is MacPherson, G. (1900), *Life of Lal Behari Day: Convert, Pastor, Professor, and Author* (Edinburgh: T&T Clark). His works are available via Project Gutenberg.

### 3.3 Mukunda Das, born Yajneshwar De (1878–1934): The Patriotic Bard [WELL-ESTABLISHED]

**Birth name**: Yajneshwar De  
**Born**: 22 February 1878, Banari village, Bikrampur *pargana*, Dhaka District (present-day Munshiganj, Bangladesh)  
**Died**: 18 May 1934, Kolkata  
**Community**: Vaishnava; grandfather was a boatman, father a grocer [WELL-ESTABLISHED]

Mukunda Das (commonly referred to as *Charan Kabi* — "Wandering Poet") represents one of the most remarkable cases of Dey-surnamed patriotic activism. **His birthplace in Bikrampur is the same region associated with the Deva dynasty** whose name most likely generated the De/Dey surname — a topographic coincidence of considerable genealogical interest, though no direct familial link can be established at this distance.

**Background**: Born Yajneshwar De to Gurudayal De and Shyamasundari Devi in Bikrampur, Mukunda Das came from a modest Vaishnava family. His grandfather was a boatman; his father operated a grocery store in Barisal after the family's migration. He attended Barisal Zilla School and Brojomohun School but dropped out after the eighth standard. The patriot Ashwini Kumar Dutta became his political mentor. In 1900, he received Vaishnava initiation and was given the spiritual name Mukunda Das.

**Swadeshi activism**: The 1905 Partition of Bengal catalysed Mukunda Das's genius. Within three months of hearing Ashwini Kumar Dutta's speech at Barisal Town Hall, he composed *Matripuja* (Worship of Mothers), a *jatra* (folk theatre) play that became one of the most powerful vehicles of Swadeshi sentiment in rural Bengal. He formed travelling theatre companies that performed across Barisal, Noakhali, Tripura, Faridpur, and Khulna. The plays were publicized by nationalist publications including *Bande Mataram*, *Yugantar*, and *Prabasi*.

**Persecution**: In 1908, Mukunda Das was arrested on sedition charges and imprisoned for three years by the British authorities. His plays were periodically banned by the colonial government. He rejoined the national movement during Gandhi's Non-Cooperation Movement of 1921. By 1932, all his plays had been banned. He died on 18 May 1934 after a final performance.

**Legacy**: A 1954 Bengali biographical film, *Charan Kavi Mukundadas*, commemorates his life. The standard scholarly source is Chanda, Pulak (2011), *Jagaran Charan Mukundadas o tar Rachanasamagra* (Dey's Publishing).

**Note on birth name**: The task document referred to this figure as "Mukunda Das (born Kedarnath Dey)," but all available sources (Wikipedia, Banglapedia) confirm his birth name was **Yajneshwar De**. The "Kedarnath Dey" reference in the task may reflect confusion with the eminent Calcutta Medical College physician Dr. Kedarnath Das (1867–?), a different person with a different surname entirely.

### 3.4 Krishna Chandra Dey / K.C. Dey (1893–1962): The Blind Maestro [WELL-ESTABLISHED]

**Born**: 24 August 1893, Calcutta  
**Died**: 28 November 1962, Calcutta  
**Father**: Shibchandra Dey

Krishna Chandra Dey (known universally as K.C. Dey) was one of the most distinctive musical personalities of Bengal and of early Indian cinema. At the age of fourteen (1906), he lost his sight entirely, becoming completely blind. Rather than defeating his career, this loss seems to have deepened his musical sensibility. He trained in Kirtan devotional music, became a gramophone recording artist and radio performer, and joined the New Theatres studio in Kolkata, which was then the centre of the Bengali and Hindi film industry.

**Career and recordings**: K.C. Dey recorded approximately 600 songs in Bengali, Hindi, Urdu, and Gujarati, including eight Muslim devotional *naats*. He composed and sang for films from 1932 to 1946, also acting in several productions (appearing as a blind beggar or wandering singer — roles that drew on his personal condition). He appeared in the 1935 Hindi *Devdas* and the 1937 *Vidyapati*, among others.

**Musical legacy**: He was *S.D. Burman's first musical teacher and mentor* — a fact of cardinal importance for the history of Hindi film music, as Sachin Dev Burman went on to become one of the greatest composers in Indian cinema. K.C. Dey's nephew, Prabodh Chandra Dey (Manna Dey), became even more celebrated than his uncle (see §3.6). K.C. Dey was patronized by elite Calcutta families including the Rajbari of Sovabazar and the Mitra House of Beadon Street.

**Assessment**: K.C. Dey occupies a crucial position in the genealogy of Bengali and Hindi popular music, functioning as the bridge between classical Kirtan devotional tradition and modern playback cinema.

### 3.5 Mukul Chandra Dey (1895–1989) and Manishi Dey (1909–1966): The Visual Arts Dynasty [WELL-ESTABLISHED]

The Dey family of artists associated with Santiniketan and the Bengal School of Art constitutes perhaps the most concentrated artistic talent cluster associated with the Dey surname. The siblings shared the parentage of **Kula Chandra Dey** and Purnashashi Devi.

**Mukul Chandra Dey (23 July 1895 – 1 March 1989)**

Born in Sridharkhola, Bengal Presidency, Mukul Dey received early training at Rabindranath Tagore's Santiniketan. He is considered a **pioneer of drypoint-etching in India** and the first Indian artist to travel abroad specifically to study printmaking.

Key milestones:
- **1916**: Travelled to Japan and studied under master painters Yokoyama Taikan and Kanzan Shimomura at Tokyo and Yokohama; lived as a guest of Japanese silk-merchant Tomitaro Hara at Sankeien alongside Rabindranath Tagore.
- **1916**: Proceeded to America, studying etching in Chicago under James Blanding Sloan and Bertha Jaques; became a life member of the Chicago Society of Etchers.
- **1920**: Further study in London at the Slade School of Fine Art (under Professor Henry Tonks) and the Royal College of Art (under Frank Short and Muirhead Bone).
- **1924**: Exhibited at the Royal Academy and New English Art Club, London.
- **1928**: Appointed the **first Indian Principal of the Government School of Art, Calcutta** — a landmark appointment representing the Indianization of a major colonial cultural institution. He introduced a women's section to the institution.

Mukul Dey's drypoint etchings of Bengali village life, Santhal villagers, and Calcutta markets are held in the Victoria and Albert Museum (London), the Indian Museum (Kolkata), and the National Gallery of Modern Art (Mumbai and New Delhi). His portraits include Albert Einstein, Mahatma Gandhi, and members of the Tagore and Tata families. His archive is housed at Chitralekha, his former home in Santiniketan.

**Manishi Dey (22 September 1909 – 31 January 1966)**

Born in Dhaka, Bengal Presidency; original name Bijoy Chandra Dey. The younger brother of Mukul, Manishi studied under Abanindranath Tagore and became a member of the Progressive Artists' Group. His work combined Bengal School aesthetics with post-Impressionist and Expressionist influences. He died in Kolkata at 56, at what contemporaries considered the height of his powers.

**Combined assessment**: The Mukul-Manishi Dey artistic dynasty, together with their sisters Annapura and Rani Chanda (accomplished in crafts), and Rani's later connection to painter Shantanu Ukil through marriage, represents a striking case of concentrated artistic talent within a single Dey family, nurtured by the Tagore-Santiniketan ecosystem.

## 4. The Literary-Intellectual Sphere: Bishnu Dey (1909–1982)

**Born**: 18 July 1909, North Calcutta  
**Died**: 3 December 1982, Calcutta  
**Father**: Abinash Chandra Dey (attorney)  
**Awards**: Sahitya Akademi Award (1965); **Jnanpith Award (1971)** — India's highest literary honour

Bishnu Dey stands as the most critically decorated literary figure to bear the Dey surname in modern Bengal. A leading poet of the modernist *Kallol* era, he was part of a cohort that included Jibanananda Das, Buddhadeva Basu, Sudhindranath Dutta, and Amiya Chakravarty — poets who collectively constructed the post-Tagore landscape of Bengali literature.

**Early life and formation**: Bishnu Dey was the fifth of seven children of Abinash Chandra Dey, an attorney — placing him firmly in the professional-class milieu associated with educated Bengali upper-caste households. He completed his schooling at Sanskrit Collegiate School (1927), received his intermediate degree from Bangabasi College (1930), and his bachelor's from St. Paul's College, Kolkata (1932), majoring in English with Philosophy and History. He received his master's from Calcutta University in 1934, the same year he married Pranati Ray Chaudhuri.

**Career**: In 1935, Dey joined Ripon College (now Surendranath College) as professor of English. He later taught at Presidency College and Central Calcutta College (now Maulana Azad College), retiring in 1969. His home at 1/10 Prince Golam Mohammad Road became a celebrated intellectual salon frequented by figures including scientist Satyendra Nath Bose, artist Jamini Roy, writers E.M. Forster (on his Indian visit) and Pablo Neruda.

**Political and artistic commitments**: Deeply influenced by Marxist philosophy, Dey co-founded the Anti-Fascist Writers' and Artists' Association (1942). He was a complex figure — an internationalist who never left India, a Marxist who resisted Marxist dogma, an admirer of T.S. Eliot who remained rooted in Bengali cultural forms.

**Works**: His seminal collection *Smriti Satta Bhabishyat* (Memory, the Being, the Future; composed 1955–61, published 1963) won him the 1965 Sahitya Akademi Award. The Jnanpith Award followed in 1971 — making him one of the rare Dey-surnamed individuals to receive independent India's highest literary honour. He also received the Soviet Land Award. His complete collected poems (*Kobita Samogro*) runs to multiple volumes.

**Father's profession note**: The fact that Bishnu Dey's father was a professional attorney (vakil) in Calcutta — typically a Kayastha or Brahmin occupation — is consistent with the Kayastha social profile of many educated Bengali Dey families, though his specific jati has not been publicly documented.

## 5. Post-1947 Figures

### 5.1 Manna Dey, born Prabodh Chandra Dey (1919–2013): The Legendary Voice [WELL-ESTABLISHED]

**Born**: 1 May 1919, Simla neighbourhood, North Calcutta  
**Died**: 24 October 2013, Bengaluru (aged 94)  
**Parents**: Purna Chandra Dey (father; worked as a clerk); Mahamaya Dey (mother)  
**Uncle**: Krishna Chandra Dey (K.C. Dey; see §3.4)  
**Awards**: Padma Shri (1971); Padma Bhushan (2005); National Film Awards (multiple); **Dadasaheb Phalke Award (2007)** — India's highest film honour; Banga-Vibhushan (2011); honorary D.Litt from Jadavpur University (2008) and posthumously from Cambridge University (2015)

Prabodh Chandra Dey, universally known as Manna Dey, is the most globally recognized individual to bear the Dey surname and ranks among the greatest playback singers in the history of Indian cinema.

**Family and social background**: The Dey family of North Calcutta provides a compelling cross-sectional portrait. Manna's father Purna Chandra Dey was a clerk — the classic scribal-administrative occupation historically associated with Kayastha identity. His uncle K.C. Dey (the blind musician, §3.4) was already a celebrated classical-devotional vocalist. The family's home was in the Simla area of North Calcutta, a locality historically associated with Bengali middle-class and Kayastha communities.

**Education**: He received early schooling at Indu Babur Pathshala, then attended Scottish Church Collegiate School and Scottish Church College before graduating from Vidyasagar College. In his youth, he was an accomplished wrestler and boxer, trained under the legendary Gobar Guha. He won inter-collegiate singing competitions for three consecutive years in different categories.

**Career**: At his uncle K.C. Dey's suggestion, Prabodh Chandra adopted the stage name "Manna Dey." He began his playback career with the film *Tamanna* (1942) — the musical score for which was composed by K.C. Dey himself — singing a duet with Suraiya. His solo career took off with "Gayi Tu Gayi Seeta Sati" from *Ram Rajya* (1943), the only Hindi film song that Mahatma Gandhi reportedly heard.

Over a career spanning more than seven decades (1938–2013), Manna Dey recorded approximately 3,500–4,000 songs in sixteen languages, including Hindi, Bengali, Bhojpuri, Punjabi, Assamese, Gujarati, Kannada, and Malayalam. He worked with more than 102 music directors. He trained under Ustad Aman Ali Khan of the Bhendi Bazaar gharana and Ustad Dabir Khan, giving his voice an unusually sophisticated classical foundation.

**Peak and decline**: His golden period ran from the mid-1950s through the 1970s. Among his most celebrated recordings:
- "Ek Chatur Naar" (*Padosan*, 1968) — a comic-classical tour de force
- "Pyar Hua Ikrar Hua" (*Shree 420*, 1955)
- "Zindagi Kaisi Hai Paheli" (*Anand*, 1971)
- "Coffee Houser Sei Addata" (1983) — Bengali; composed by Suparna Kanti Ghosh; became an anthem for nostalgic educated Bengali culture
- "Laga Chunri Mein Daag" (*Dil Hi To Hai*, 1963) — one of the great classical renditions in Hindi cinema

**Personal life**: In December 1953, he married Sulochana Kumaran, a Keralite woman — an intercommunity marriage unusual for its era. They had two daughters: Shuroma Herekar (1956–2016), a US-based scientist, and Shumita Dev (b. 1958), a Bengaluru-based businesswoman. After Sulochana's death in January 2012, he moved to Kalyan Nagar, Bengaluru, where he died on 24 October 2013.

**Published memoirs**: *Jiboner Jalsaghorey* (Bengali autobiography, Ananda Publishers, 2005); translated into English as *Memories Come Alive* (Penguin Books), Hindi as *Yadein Jee Uthi*, and Marathi.

**Assessment**: Manna Dey's career represents the highest artistic achievement of any Dey-surnamed individual in the post-Independence era. That a clerk's son from a North Calcutta scribal family became the recipient of the Dadasaheb Phalke Award and honorary Cambridge degree is itself a testament to the educational and cultural capital accumulated by Kayastha families over generations.

### 5.2 Krishanu Dey (1962–2003): The Maradona of Bengal [WELL-ESTABLISHED]

**Born**: 14 February 1962, Naktala, South Kolkata  
**Died**: 20 March 2003, Kolkata

Krishanu Dey is the most celebrated footballer to bear the Dey surname, earning the nickname "Maradona of India" or "Maradona of Bengal" for his exceptional dribbling and goal-scoring ability. An attacking midfielder/striker, he began his career with Calcutta Police AC (1979), moved to Calcutta Port Trust (1980), then joined Mohun Bagan (1982–1984) before transferring to East Bengal (1985–1991, 1992–1994), where he is most remembered.

His most celebrated moment came with a spectacular goal in 1989 that won him legendary status. He captained East Bengal in 1989–90, when the team won the Kolkata League. During his career he won one Federation Cup, three IFA Shields, three Durand Cups, five Calcutta Leagues, and one Rovers Cup. He died prematurely on 20 March 2003.

### 5.3 Jyotirmoy Dey (1955–2011): The Crime Journalist [WELL-ESTABLISHED]

**Born**: 1955  
**Died**: 11 June 2011, Mumbai (murdered)

Jyotirmoy Dey, known professionally as "J Dey" or "Commander J," was a crime reporter and investigations editor for the Mumbai tabloid *Mid-Day*, recognized as a leading expert on the Mumbai underworld. He authored two books: *Zero Dial: The Dangerous World of Informers* and *Khallas*. On 11 June 2011, he was shot dead while returning home on his motorcycle in Ghatkopar, Mumbai — murdered on the orders of gangster Chhota Rajan. A special MCOCA court sentenced Chhota Rajan and seven others to life imprisonment in the case. Dey's murder and its aftermath became the subject of the 2023 Netflix series *Scoop*.

### 6.4 Note on "Dr. Kedarnath Dey" [CORRECTION]

The task document referenced "Dr. Kedarnath Dey" as a possible prominent colonial physician. Research has identified that the prominent physician at Calcutta Medical College associated with the name "Kedarnath" was **Dr. Kedarnath Das** (b. 20 February 1867), an eminent obstetrician at Calcutta Medical College who in 1928 published *Obstetric Forceps*, introducing the Das Forceps to Indian medicine. His surname was **Das**, not Dey. No comparably prominent colonial physician named Kedarnath *Dey* has been identified in available sources. This may be a confusion arising from the phonetic similarity of the surnames Das and Dey.

## 7. Cross-Cutting Analysis: The Dey Surname and Social Mobility

### 7.1 Caste Distribution Among Notable Deys

The biographies assembled above reveal an important pattern: while the Dey surname is most commonly associated with Bengali Kayastha identity, the historically notable Deys span multiple jati communities:

| Figure | Period | Community | Field |
|--------|--------|-----------|-------|
| Ramdulal Dey | 1752–1825 | Uncertain (possibly Kayastha or Vaishya) | Commerce |
| Lal Behari Dey | 1824–1894 | **Suvarna Banik** (Vaishya) | Literature/Religion |
| Mukunda Das (Yajneshwar De) | 1878–1934 | Vaishnava/Vaishya | Theatre/Patriotism |
| K.C. Dey | 1893–1962 | Likely Kayastha | Music |
| Mukul Chandra Dey | 1895–1989 | Likely Kayastha | Visual Arts |
| Manishi Dey | 1909–1966 | Likely Kayastha | Visual Arts |
| Bishnu Dey | 1909–1982 | Likely Kayastha (father: attorney) | Literature |

| Jyotirmoy Dey | 1955–2011 | Bengali Hindu | Journalism |

The case of Lal Behari Dey is particularly instructive: despite belonging to the Suvarna Banik community rather than the Kayastha scribal class, he pursued the classic *Kayastha* pathway of English education, colonial employment, and literary production — suggesting that the Dey surname, when associated with the Bengal Renaissance's educational institutions, could function as a shared cultural marker transcending jati boundaries.

### 7.2 The Kayastha Educational-Cultural Template

The majority of notable Dey figures confirm rather than challenge the standard Kayastha social profile identified in the literature:

1. **Scribal/Administrative fathers**: Manna Dey's father was a clerk; Bishnu Dey's father was an attorney. Both represent the Kayastha tradition of literate employment in colonial administration.
2. **Elite educational institutions**: Scottish Church Collegiate School (Manna Dey), Sanskrit Collegiate School (Bishnu Dey), Mission schools (Lal Behari Dey). Access to colonial-era English education was a Kayastha advantage.
3. **Cultural-artistic excellence**: The Dey surname is disproportionately represented in music (K.C. Dey, Manna Dey), visual arts (Mukul Dey, Manishi Dey), and literature (Bishnu Dey, Lal Behari Dey) — precisely the fields in which Bengali upper-caste families historically invested cultural capital.
4. **North Calcutta geography**: Multiple Dey figures (K.C. Dey, Manna Dey, Bishnu Dey) were associated with North Calcutta — the traditional home of the Bengali bhadralok (educated upper-class). This geographic concentration suggests social clustering consistent with Kayastha networks.

### 7.3 Disruptions to the Template

Not all notable Deys fit the Kayastha template:

- **Ramdulal Dey** rose from genuine poverty (his grandmother worked as a cook) to become the city's wealthiest merchant — a trajectory that bypassed the literary-administrative pathway entirely.
- **Mukunda Das** (Yajneshwar De) came from a family of boatmen and grocers, used folk theatre rather than English literature as his medium, and was imprisoned for sedition — representing a counter-cultural Dey identity rooted in rural Bengal rather than Calcutta's *bhadralok* milieu.
- **Krishanu Dey** found his path through athletics rather than education or arts.

These exceptions suggest that the Dey surname functioned as a sufficiently broad social marker to encompass both the Calcutta-centric bhadralok trajectory and more regionally diverse social backgrounds.

### 7.4 The Dey Surname and the Bengal Renaissance: A Structural Analysis

The Bengal Renaissance (approximately 1810–1920) was driven by the *bhadralok* — the educated middle and upper-middle classes of Bengali society, predominantly Brahmin and Kayastha. The notable Deys of this period (Ramdulal Dey as a philanthropic patron; Lal Behari Dey as a writer-reformer; K.C. Dey as a classical musician) participated in all three major dimensions of Renaissance activity: economic modernization, literary-linguistic reform, and classical cultural revival. The subsequent generation (Mukul Dey, Manishi Dey, Bishnu Dey, Manna Dey) represent the deepening and internationalization of these Renaissance projects into modernism, printmaking, Marxist poetry, and Hindi film music respectively.

This pattern confirms the hypothesis that the Dey surname, while not among the five Kulin Kayastha clans, occupied a socially productive position in the second tier of Bengal's educated classes — high enough to access elite institutions and cultural networks, yet not so rigidly defined as to prevent cross-community movement.

## 8. The Dey Surname in the Diaspora: Preliminary Notes

The systematic study of Dey family migration requires separate investigation, but preliminary notes are offered here:

- **Post-1947**: The Partition of 1947 led to a large-scale migration of Hindu Bengalis from East Pakistan to West Bengal. Dey families from Dhaka, Chittagong, Sylhet, and other regions relocated. This wave is distinct from earlier migration patterns.
- **1971**: The Bangladesh Liberation War created a second major diaspora wave, predominantly Muslim, but also affecting Hindu communities including Dey families with long ancestral roots in what became Bangladesh.
- **Professional diaspora (1970s–present)**: Bengali Kayastha families, with their tradition of English-language education, participated in the professional emigration to the United States, United Kingdom, and Canada from the 1970s onward.
- **Notable diaspora Deys**: Manna Dey's daughter Shuroma Herekar (d. 2016) was a US-based scientist — representing the second-generation diaspora pattern of professional specialization. No other diaspora Dey figures of established international prominence have been identified in this study.

## 10. Conclusions

The catalogue of notable Dey figures presented here supports several conclusions of analytical significance for the DEY-BENGAL project:

1. **The surname spans jati communities**: While predominantly Kayastha in its most prominent instances, the Dey/De surname has produced notable figures from Suvarna Banik (Lal Behari Dey), Vaishnava-Vaishya (Mukunda Das/Yajneshwar De), and uncertain backgrounds (Ramdulal Dey). This confirms findings from Report 1A that the surname is not exclusively Kayastha.

2. **The Kayastha template dominates the colonial and post-colonial period**: Manna Dey, Bishnu Dey, K.C. Dey, Mukul Dey, and Manishi Dey all exhibit the classic markers of Kayastha social positioning: literate fathers, elite colonial schools, cultural production in arts and letters.

3. **Bikrampur/Dhaka as a Dey heartland**: Two notable Dey figures — Mukunda Das (born Yajneshwar De, from Bikrampur) and Manishi Dey (born in Dhaka) — originate from the region most closely associated with the Deva dynasty, which the genealogical literature identifies as the probable etymological source of the Dey surname. Whether this geographic concentration reflects descent from Deva-era families or simply reflects the density of the Dey surname in that region cannot be determined without archival genealogical research.

4. **Cross-generational musical dynasty**: The K.C. Dey → Manna Dey uncle-nephew relationship, combined with K.C. Dey's role as S.D. Burman's first teacher, places the Dey family at the origin point of multiple streams of twentieth-century Indian popular music. This is the most remarkable documented kinship cluster among notable Dey figures.

5. **Gaps requiring further research**: Pre-colonial Mughal-era Dey families; zamindari records mentioning Dey families; the specific genealogies connecting individual Dey families to medieval records. These gaps can only be addressed through primary archival research at the West Bengal State Archives, Bangladesh National Archives, and through examination of surviving kulagranthas.
