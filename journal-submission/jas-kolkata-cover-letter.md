Dear General Secretary,

I am pleased to submit for consideration by the *Journal of the Asiatic Society* the article:

> **"Origins, Social Identity, and Documentary History of the De/Dey Family of East Bengal, c. 1200–1947"**

The paper traces the Bengali Hindu surname De/Dey through five analytical frameworks: etymology, epigraphy, caste sociology, population genetics, and local family history. Its central arguments are:

1. **Historical origin**: The surname crystallised as a hereditary Kayastha identifier between 1231 and 1317 CE, in association with the Vaishnava Deva dynasty of Bikrampur and Sonargaon, whose fall to the Bengal Sultanate catalysed the fixing of many East Bengali surnames.

2. **Caste classification**: Drawing on five volumes of Nagendranath Basu's *Banger Jatiya Itihas* and three additional independently produced Kayastha social texts (Vidyanidhi's *Sambandha Nirnaya*, 1896; N. Basu's *Kayastha Barna Nirnay*, 1913; G. Basu's *Kayastha Tattwa-Koumodi*, 1928), the paper establishes that Dey families occupy the *Mahapatra* tier — the highest division within the Maulika rank — in the Bangaja (East Bengal) Kayastha hierarchy.

3. **Genetic evidence**: Pagani, Bose, Ayub, and Tyler-Smith (2017) provide the only published direct genetic test of a Bengali Dey individual, showing Y-haplogroup H1a* — an ancient indigenous South Asian paternal lineage predating Indo-Aryan steppe migration — consistent with the *maulika* (original Bengal stock) classification.

4. **Micro-history**: The paper's original contribution is a micro-historical case study centred on *Loke Nath Sen v. Aswini Kumar Dey* (AIR 1938 Cal 216) — a Calcutta High Court trademark dispute involving the author's grandfather, Aswini Kumar De, an umbrella manufacturer of Mymensingh Burra Bazar whose business is documented from c. 1912 and whose trademark (Registry No. 964, 23 August 1926) is one of the few surviving primary documents for a Bengali Dey *bhadralok* family in the pre-Partition period.

The paper applies a critical methodological framework — distinguishing Category A (epigraphically corroborated), Category B (tradition-internal with partial corroboration), and Category C (single-source) claims — that reflects established scholarly standards in kulagrantha studies (following Inden and Chatterjee) and that is, I believe, suited to the *Journal*'s tradition of rigorous and original scholarship.

The article is approximately 6,400 words (within the 5,000–8,000 word guideline), accompanied by an abstract of fewer than 100 words. It employs British English spelling throughout. References are given as numbered endnotes in the format prescribed by the *Journal*'s contributor guidelines. The paper has not been submitted elsewhere and does not duplicate any previously published work.
